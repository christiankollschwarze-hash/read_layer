###CHAPTER### Introduction

The little book by Epictetus called _Enchiridion_, or “manual,” has played a disproportionately large role in the rise of modern attitudes and modern philosophy. As soon as it was translated into the vernacular languages, it became a bestseller among independent intellectuals, anti-Christian thinkers, and philosophers of a subjective cast. Montaigne had a copy of the _Enchiridion_ among his books. Pascal violently rejected the megalomaniac pride of the Stoic philosopher. Frederick the Great carried the book with him on all campaigns. It was a source of inspiration and encouragement to Anthony, Earl of Shaftesbury, in the serious illness which ended only in his death; many pages of his diaries contain passages copied from the _Enchiridion_. It has been studied and widely quoted by Scottish philosophers like Francis Hutcheson, Adam Smith, and Adam Ferguson, who valued Stoic moral philosophy for its reconciliation of social dependency and personal independence.

That there was a rebirth of Stoicism in the centuries of rebirth which marked the emergence of the modern age was not mere chance. Philosophical, moral, and social conditions of the time united to cause it. Roman Stoicism had been developed in times of despotism as a philosophy of lonely and courageous souls who had recognized the redeeming power of philosophical reason in all the moral and social purposes of life. Philosophy as a way of life makes men free. It is the last ditch stand of liberty in a world of servitude. Many elements in the new age led to thought which had structural affinity with Roman Stoicism. Modern times had created the independent thinker, the free intellectual in a secular civilization. Modern times had destroyed medieval liberties and had established the new despotism of the absolute state supported by ecclesiastical authority. Modern philosophies continued the basic trend in Stoicism in making the subjective consciousness the foundation of philosophy. The Stoic emphasis on moral problems was also appealing in an era of rapid transition, when all the values which had previously been taken for granted were questioned and reconsidered.

While it is interesting to observe how varied were the effects produced by this small volume, this epitome of the Stoic system of moral philosophy, these effects seem still more remarkable when we consider that it was not intended to be a philosophical treatise on Stoicism for students. It was, rather, to be a guide for the advanced student of Stoicism to show him the best roads toward the goal of becoming a true philosopher. Thus Epictetus and his _Enchiridion_ have a unique position in Roman Stoicism. Seneca and Marcus Aurelius had selected Stoic philosophy as the most adequate system for expressing their existential problems of independence, solitude, and history. In this enterprise, Seneca made tremendous strides toward the insights of social psychology as a by-product of his consciousness of decadence (in this he was close to Nietzsche), but he was not primarily concerned with the unity of the Stoic system. Marcus Aurelius changed the philosophical doctrine into the regimen of the lonesome ruler. In contrast to both, Epictetus was teaching Stoic philosophy as a doctrine and as a way of life. The _Enchiridion_ is a summary of theoretical and applied Stoicism.

Epictetus was the son of a woman slave, born between 50 and 60 A.D. at Hieropolis in Phrygia. We do not know how he came to Rome. He was there as slave to one of Nero’s distinguished freedmen who served as the Emperor’s secretary. While still in service, Epictetus took courses with Musonius Rufus, the fashionable Stoic philosopher. Musonius was impressed by the sincere and dynamic personality of the young slave and trained him to be a Stoic philosopher. Epictetus became a free man and began teaching philosophy on street corners and in the market, but he was not successful. During the rule of Domitian, Epictetus was exiled from Rome along with many other philosophers, probably between 89 and 92 A.D. He went to Nicopolis, across Actium in Epirus, where he conducted his own school. He was so well regarded and highly esteemed that he established the reputation of the place as the town of Epictetus’ school. Students came from Athens and Rome to attend his classes. Private citizens came to ask his advice and guidance. Some of his students returned to their homes to enter the traditional careers to which they were socially obligated. Others assumed the philosophic way of life in order to escape into the sphere of Stoic freedom.

Among the students was a young Roman, Flavius Arrian, who took courses at Nicopolis when Epictetus was already old. Flavius, who was born in 108 A.D., was one of the intimates of Hadrian, who made him consul in 130 A.D. He probably studied with Epictetus between the years 123 and 126 A.D. The informal philosophical talks which Epictetus had with his students fascinated him. Needless to say there were also systematic courses in the fields of philosophy. But it was the informal discourses which convinced Arrian that he had finally discovered a Stoic Socrates or a Stoic Diogenes, who was not merely teaching a doctrine, but also living the truth. Arrian recorded many of the discourses and informal conversations of Epictetus with his intimate students. He took them down in shorthand in order not to lose the ineffable liveliness, grace, and wit of the beloved teacher. Arrian retired into private life after the death of Hadrian in 138 A.D. and dedicated himself to his literary work. He published his notes on Epictetus’ teaching under the title: _Discourses in Four Books_. The _Enchiridion_, which was also arranged by Arrian, is a brief summary of the basic ideas of Stoic philosophy and an introduction to the techniques required to transform Stoic philosophy into a way of life.

Thus we do not have any original writings of Epictetus. Like G. H. Mead in recent times, he was completely dedicated to the human and intellectual problems of his students. He left it for them to preserve what they considered to be the lasting message of the teacher. In contrast to Seneca and Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus had no subjective approach to the Stoic doctrines. Moral philosophy was the center of his teaching, and epistemology was only instrumental. It is even permissible to say that he took physics or cosmology too lightly. If this is granted, we must admit that he is completely absorbed by the fundamentals of Stoic thought as presented in the _Enchiridion_. Epictetus’ personality is totally integrated in the act of reasoning which establishes conformity with nature.

A remarkable difference between the *Discourses* and the *Enchiridion* should be mentioned. The *Discourses* are a living image of the teacher in action; they present the process of philosophizing, not the finished product. They show the enthusiastic and sober, the realistic and emotional moralist in constantly changing perspectives. These perspectives are shaped by the changing students with their various concerns, problems, and questions. His teachings and his formulations directly relate to the various life situations in which the students should apply and practice the master’s Stoic teaching. No human situation is omitted; as a guide to conduct, philosophy has relevance for all. Whether the students have to attend a dinner party, whether they are among competitors in a stadium or in a swimming pool, whether they have to present themselves at court or in an office, whether they are in the company of their mothers and sisters or of girl friends—in all human situations the philosopher knows the correct advice for the philosophical apprentice. Thus, in the *Discourses*, Arrian presents the unique individuality of the philosopher and of his applied moral method in living contact with various students in concrete situations. Epictetus as teacher anticipates very modern educational methods in his regard for the structure of situations and the changing perspectives in human relationships.

Nothing like this is revealed in the _Enchiridion_. Gone is the Stoic philosopher as a living spirit. What remains is the living spirit of Stoicism. The _Enchiridion_ is a manual for the combat officer. This analogy should be taken seriously. The Roman Stoics coined the formula: _Vivere militare!_ (Life is being a soldier.) The student of philosophy is a private, the advancing Stoic is a non-commissioned officer, and the philosopher is the combat officer. For this reason all Roman Stoics apply metaphors and images derived from military life. Apprentice students of Stoicism are described as messengers, as scouts of God, as representatives of divine nature. The advancing student who is close to the goal of being a philosopher has the rank of an officer. He is already able to establish inner freedom and independence. He understands the basic Stoic truth of subjective consciousness, which is to distinguish what is in our power from what is not in our power. Not in our power are all the elements which constitute our environment, such as wealth, health, reputation, social prestige, power, the lives of those we love, and death. In our power are our thinking, our intentions, our desires, our decisions. These make it possible for us to control ourselves and to make of ourselves elements and parts of the universe of nature. This knowledge of ourselves makes us free in a world of dependencies. This superiority of our powers enables us to live in conformity with nature. The rational philosophy of control of Self and of adjustment to the Whole implies an asceticism of the emotional and the sensitive life. The philosopher must examine and control his passions, his love, his tenderness at all times in order always to be ready for the inevitable moment of farewell. The Stoics practiced a Jesuitism _avant la lettre_. They were able to live in the world as if they did not live in it. To the Stoic, life is a military camp, a play on the stage, a banquet to which we are invited. The _Enchiridion_ briefly indicated the techniques which the philosopher should apply in acting well the diverse roles which God might assign to those whom he loves, the Stoic philosophers. From the rules of social conduct to the recommendations of sexual asceticism before marriage, and the method of true thinking, the advanced Stoic will find all principles of perfection and all precepts for realizing philosophical principles in his conduct in this tiny volume.

Thus the _Enchiridion_ was liberating for all intellectuals who learned from it that there are philosophical ways of self-redemption. From its time, the secular thinker could feel jubilant because he was not in need of a divine grace. Epictetus had taught him that philosophical reason could make him free and that he was capable of redeeming himself by sound reasoning.

In the Stoic distinctions of personality and world, of I and mine, of subjective consciousness and the world of objects, of freedom and dependence, we find implicit the basic elements of modern philosophies of rationalism and of objective idealism or pantheism. For this reason there is a continuous renascence of Stoicism from Descartes, Grotius, and Bishop Butler, to Montesquieu, Adam Smith, and Kant. In this long development in modern times, the tiny _Enchiridion_ of Epictetus played a remarkable part.

The translations of Epictetus and of all other Stoics had the widest effect on philosophers, theologians, and lay thinkers. They were studied by the clergy of the various Christian denominations, by the scientists who were striving for a natural religion, and by the independent philosophers who were eager to separate philosophy from religion. There were many outstanding bishops in the Catholic and Anglican Churches who were eager to transform the traditions of Roman Stoicism into Christian Stoicism. Among the Calvinistic denominations were many thinkers who were in sympathy with Stoic moral principles because of their praise of the austerity of life and of the control of passions. Likewise the adherents of natural religion were propagating Stoicism as the ideal pattern of universally valid and intelligible religion. Renascent Stoicism had three functions in the rise of the modern world. First, it reconciled Christian traditions to modern rationalistic philosophies; secondly, it established an ideal pattern of natural religion; and, thirdly, it opened the way for the autonomy of morals.

###CHAPTER### The Enchiridion

I

There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own. Beyond our power are body, property, reputation, office, and, in one word, whatever are not properly our own affairs.

Now the things within our power are by nature free, unrestricted, unhindered. But those beyond our power are weak, dependent, restricted, alien. Remember, then, that if you attribute freedom to things by nature dependent and take what belongs to others for your own, you will be hindered, you will lament, you will be disturbed, you will find fault both with gods and men. But if you take for your own only that which is your own and view what belongs to others just as it really is, then no one will ever compel you, no one will restrict you. You will find fault with no one, you will accuse no one, you will do nothing against your will. No one will hurt you, you will not have an enemy, nor will you suffer any harm.

Aiming, therefore, at such great things, remember that you must not allow yourself any inclination, however slight, toward the attainment of the others. But you must entirely quit some of them, and for the present postpone the rest. But if you would have these, and possess power and wealth likewise, you may miss the latter in seeking the former. And you will certainly fail of that by which alone happiness and freedom are procured.

Seek at once, therefore, to be able to say to every unpleasing semblance, “You are but a semblance and by no means the real thing.” And then examine it by those rules which you have. And first and chiefly by this: whether it concerns the things which are within our own power or those which are not. And if it concerns anything beyond our power, be prepared to say that it is nothing to you.

###CHAPTER### Ii

Remember that desire means wanting to get what you long for, and aversion means wanting to avoid what you dislike. Someone who fails to get what they desire is disappointed, and someone who meets what they try to avoid is miserable. If you avoid only those unwanted things that you can control, you will never meet anything you try to avoid. But if you try to avoid sickness, death, or poverty, you risk making yourself miserable. So stop applying aversion to everything outside your power, and apply it instead to unwanted things that are within your power. For now, hold back desire altogether. If you desire anything outside your own power, you are bound to be disappointed. And you are not yet sure about the things within your power, which are the proper objects of desire. Where it is practically necessary for you to pursue or avoid anything, do even that with care, gentleness, and moderation.

###CHAPTER### Iii

With regard to whatever objects either delight the mind or contribute to use or are tenderly beloved, remind yourself of what nature they are, beginning with the merest trifles: if you have a favorite cup, that it is but a cup of which you are fond of—for thus, if it is broken, you can bear it; if you embrace your child or your wife, that you embrace a mortal—and thus, if either of them dies, you can bear it.

###CHAPTER### Iv

When you set about any action, remind yourself of what nature the action is. If you are going to bathe, picture to yourself the things that usually happen in the bath—some people splashing, others pushing in, others scolding, others stealing. And you will go about this action more safely if you say to yourself, “I will now go to bathe and keep my own will in harmony with nature.” And so with every other action. For in this way, if anything gets in the way while bathing, you will be able to say, “It was not only to bathe that I wanted, but to keep my will in harmony with nature; and I shall not keep it that way if I am out of humor at things that happen.”

V

Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. Thus death is nothing terrible, or it would have seemed so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our idea of death, that it is terrible. When, therefore, we are hindered or disturbed, or grieved, let us never blame others, but ourselves—that is, our own views. It is the act of an uninstructed person to reproach others for his own misfortunes; of one beginning instruction, to reproach himself; and of one perfectly instructed, to reproach neither others nor himself.

###CHAPTER### Vi

Do not be proud of any excellence that is not your own. If a horse were proud and said, “I am handsome,” that might be bearable. But when you are proud and say, “I have a handsome horse,” know that you are proud only because of the horse’s merit. What, then, is your own? The use of the experiences of life. So when you act in harmony with nature in this way, you will have some reason to be proud; for you will be proud of some good that is truly your own.

###CHAPTER### Vii

Think of a sea voyage. When the ship is at anchor and you go ashore to get water, you may amuse yourself along the way by picking up a shellfish or a truffle. But your thoughts should stay focused on the ship, and you should be constantly alert in case the captain calls. Then you must leave all those things behind, so that you are not carried on board tied up like a sheep. In the same way, in life, if something like a wife or a child is granted to you instead of a truffle or shellfish, there is no objection. But if the captain calls, run to the ship, leave all these things, and never look back. And if you are old, never go far from the ship, so that you are not missing when you are called for.

VIII

Do not demand that events should happen as you wish. Instead, wish them to happen as they do happen, and you will go on well.

###CHAPTER### Ix

Sickness limits the body, but not the will, unless the will chooses to be limited. Lameness limits the leg, but not the will. Say this to yourself about everything that happens. For you will find that it limits something else, but not truly yourself.

X

Whenever something happens, remember to turn inward and ask what ability you have for dealing with it. If you meet a beautiful person, you will find self-control is the ability needed; if pain, then courage; if insult, then patience. When you make this a habit, the events of life will not overwhelm you.

###CHAPTER### Xi

Never say of anything, “I have lost it,” but rather, “I have given it back.” Has your child died? It has been given back. Has your wife died? She has been given back. Has your property been taken away? That too has been given back. “But it was a bad man who took it.” What does it matter to you through whom the giver asked for it back? While he allows you to have it, treat it as something not your own, like travelers at an inn.

###CHAPTER### Xii

If you want to improve, set aside thoughts like these: “If I neglect my affairs, I will have no way to support myself; if I do not punish my servant, he will be good for nothing.” For it would be better to die of hunger, free from grief and fear, than to live in wealth with a troubled mind. And it is better that your servant should be bad than that you should be unhappy.

Begin therefore with little things. Is a little oil spilled or a little wine stolen? Say to yourself, “This is the price paid for peace and tranquillity; and nothing is to be had for nothing.” And when you call your servant, consider that he may not come when you call; or, if he does, that he may not do what you wish. But it is not at all desirable for him, and very undesirable for you, that it should be in his power to cause you any disturbance.

###CHAPTER### Xiii

If you want to improve, be willing to be seen as foolish and dull in outward matters. Do not wish to appear to know anything; and even if you seem important to others, do not trust that impression. For be sure of this: it is not easy to keep your will in harmony with nature and at the same time secure outward things. While you are focused on one, you will necessarily neglect the other.

###CHAPTER### Xiv

If you wish your children, your wife, and your friends to live forever, you are being foolish, because you are wishing for things that are not in your power, and for what belongs to others to be your own. In the same way, if you wish your servant to be without fault, you are being foolish, because you are wishing that vice should not be vice but something else. But if you wish not to be disappointed in your desires, that is in your own power. Therefore, practice what is in your power. A man’s master is the person who is able to give or take away whatever that man seeks or avoids. Whoever, then, would be free should wish for nothing and avoid nothing that depends on others; otherwise he must necessarily be a slave.

###CHAPTER### Xv

Remember that you must behave as at a banquet. Is anything brought round to you? Put out your hand and take a moderate share. Does it pass by you? Do not stop it. Is it not yet come? Do not yearn in desire toward it, but wait till it reaches you. So with regard to children, wife, office, riches; and you will some time or other be worthy to feast with the gods. And if you do not so much as take the things which are set before you, but are able even to forego them, then you will not only be worthy to feast with the gods, but to rule with them also. For, by thus doing, Diogenes and Heraclitus, and others like them, deservedly became divine, and were so recognized.

###CHAPTER### Xvi

When you see someone crying in grief, either because his son has gone abroad or because he has suffered losses in his business, be careful not to be overwhelmed by what seems to be the misfortune. Instead, make a distinction and be ready to say, “What hurts this man is not the event itself—another person might not be hurt by it—but the way he chooses to see it.” In conversation, however, do not hesitate to meet him where he is and, if necessary, to grieve with him. But be careful not to grieve inwardly as well.

###CHAPTER### Xvii

Remember that you are an actor in a drama of such sort as the Author chooses—if short, then in a short one; if long, then in a long one. If it is his pleasure that you should play a poor man, a cripple, a ruler, or a private citizen, see that you act it well. For this is your business—to act well the part you are given, but choosing it belongs to another.

###CHAPTER### Xviii

When a raven happens to croak unluckily, do not be overcome by appearances. Instead, think clearly and say, “Nothing is predicted for me—not for my insignificant body, property, reputation, children, or wife. For me, all signs are lucky if I choose them to be. Whatever happens, it is up to me to draw some benefit from it.”

###CHAPTER### Xix

You can be unconquerable if you never enter a fight where victory is not within your own power. So when you see someone famous for honors or power, or highly respected for any other reason, be careful not to be dazzled by appearances and call that person happy. For if the essence of good lies in things within our own power, there will be no room for envy or rivalry. But as for you, do not wish to be a general, a senator, or a consul, but to be free. The only way to this is to disregard things that are not within our own power.

###CHAPTER### Xx

Remember that it is not the person who insults or hits you who wrongs you, but your own belief that these things are insulting. So when someone provokes you, know that it is your own opinion that provokes you. Therefore, first of all, try not to be carried away by appearances. For if you once gain time and delay, you will more easily control yourself.

###CHAPTER### Xxi

Let death and exile, and all other things that seem terrible, be daily before your eyes, but death most of all; and you will never have a low or cowardly thought, nor desire anything too eagerly.

###CHAPTER### Xxii

If you truly long for wisdom, prepare yourself from the very start to have the crowd laugh and mock, and say, “He has come back to us a philosopher all of a sudden”; and, “Where does this arrogant look come from?” Now, for your part, do not actually wear an arrogant look, but hold steadily to the things that seem best to you, as someone placed by God in this particular role. For remember that, if you keep at it, the very people who first made fun of you will later admire you. But if you let them defeat you, you will earn twice the mockery.

###CHAPTER### Xxiii

If you ever happen to turn your attention to outward appearances, just to please someone, be assured that you have ruined your plan for life. Be content, then, in everything, with being a philosopher. And if you wish to seem like one to anyone else, appear so to yourself, and that will be enough.

###CHAPTER### Xxiv

Do not let thoughts like these upset you: “I will live in disgrace and be nobody anywhere.” For if disgrace is an evil, you can no more be involved in evil through another person than you can be involved in baseness. Is it any business of yours, then, to get power or to be invited to a banquet? By no means. How then, after all, is this disgrace? And how is it true that you will be nobody anywhere, when you ought to be somebody only in those things which are within your own power, in which you may be of the greatest importance? “But my friends will be unassisted.” What do you mean by “unassisted”? They will not have money from you, nor will you make them Roman citizens. Who told you, then, that these are among the things within our own power, and not rather the affairs of others? And who can give to another the things which he himself does not have? “Well, but get them, then, so that we too may have a share.” If I can get them while preserving my own honor and fidelity and self-respect, show me the way and I will get them. But if you require me to lose my own proper good, so that you may gain what is no good, consider how unreasonable and foolish you are. Besides, which would you rather have, a sum of money or a faithful and honorable friend? Rather help me, then, to gain this character than require me to do those things by which I may lose it. Well, but my country, you say, as far as depends upon me, will be unassisted. Here, again, what assistance is this you mean? It will not have porticos nor baths provided by you? And what does that matter? Why, neither does a smith provide it with shoes, nor a shoemaker with arms. It is enough if everyone fully performs his own proper business. And were you to supply it with another faithful and honorable citizen, would he not be of use to it? Yes. Therefore neither are you yourself useless to it. “What place, then,” you say, “shall I hold in the state?” Whatever place you can hold while preserving your fidelity and honor. But if, by desiring to be useful to it, you lose these, how can you serve your country when you have become faithless and shameless?

###CHAPTER### Xxv

Is someone else given a better place than you at a party, or treated with more courtesy, or trusted with more private conversation? If these things are good, you should be glad that the other person has them. If they are bad, do not be upset that you do not have them. And remember that you cannot expect to compete with others for outward advantages unless you use the same means to get them. For how can someone who refuses to wait at a person's door, refuses to attend on him, and refuses to praise him, expect an equal share with someone who does all these things? You are being unfair and unreasonable, then, if you are unwilling to pay the price these things are sold for, and yet still want them for nothing. For how much are lettuces sold? An obulus, for example. If another person pays an obulus and takes the lettuces, and you do not pay and go without them, do not imagine that he has gained any advantage over you. For just as he has the lettuces, so you still have the obulus you did not give. So, in the present case, you have not been invited to such a person's party because you have not paid him the price for which a supper is sold. It is sold for praise; it is sold for attendance. Give him the value, then, if it is worth it to you. But if you want to avoid paying the one and still receive the other, you are being unreasonable and foolish. Have you nothing, then, in place of the supper? Yes, indeed, you do—you do not have to praise someone you do not like to praise, and you do not have to put up with the rudeness of his servants.

###CHAPTER### Xxvi

The will of nature can be understood from things we all agree on. For example, when our neighbor's child breaks a cup or something similar, we are quick to say, "These are things that just happen." Be sure, then, that when your own cup breaks in the same way, you should feel just as you did when another person's cup broke. Now apply this to bigger things. Has another person's child or wife died? There is no one who would not say, "This is part of being human." But if someone's own child happens to die, they immediately cry, "Oh, how miserable I am!" We should always remember how we feel when we hear about the same thing happening to others.

XXVII

Just as a target is not set up for the purpose of missing it, so the nature of evil does not exist in the world.

###CHAPTER### Xxviii

If someone handed your body over to a passer-by, you would certainly be angry. So why do you feel no shame in handing over your own mind to any critic, letting yourself be unsettled and confused?

###CHAPTER### Xxix[2]

In every affair consider what comes before and what follows, and then undertake it. Otherwise you will begin with spirit, indeed, careless of the consequences, and when these appear, you will shamefully give up. “I would conquer at the Olympic Games.” But consider what comes before and what follows, and then, if it is to your advantage, take on the matter. You must follow rules, submit to a diet, refrain from rich foods; exercise your body, whether you choose to or not, at a set hour, in heat and cold; you must drink no cold water, and sometimes no wine—in a word, you must give yourself up to your trainer as to a doctor. Then, in the fight, you may be thrown into a ditch, dislocate your arm, turn your ankle, swallow plenty of dust, receive lashes [for carelessness], and, after all, lose the victory. When you have counted up all this, if your inclination still holds, set about the contest. Otherwise, take notice, you will behave like children who sometimes play wrestlers, sometimes gladiators, sometimes blow a trumpet, and sometimes act a tragedy, when they happen to have seen and admired these shows. So you too will be at one time a wrestler, and another a gladiator; now a philosopher, now an orator; but nothing in earnest. Like an ape you mimic all you see, and one thing after another is sure to please you, but falls out of favor as soon as it becomes familiar. For you have never entered upon anything carefully, nor after having surveyed and tested the whole matter, but carelessly, and with a halfway zeal. Thus some, when they have seen a philosopher and heard a man speaking like Euphrates[3]—though, indeed, who can speak like him?—have a mind to be philosophers, too. Consider first, man, what the matter is, and what your own nature is able to bear. If you would be a wrestler, consider your shoulders, your back, your thighs; for different persons are made for different things. Do you think that you can act as you do and be a philosopher, that you can eat, drink, be angry, be discontented, as you are now? You must keep watch, you must labor, you must overcome certain appetites, must leave your acquaintances, be despised by your servant, be laughed at by those you meet; come off worse than others in everything—in offices, in honors, before courts. When you have fully considered all these things, approach, if you please—that is, if, by parting with them, you have a mind to purchase serenity, freedom, and tranquillity. If not, do not come here; do not, like children, be now a philosopher, then a tax collector, then an orator, and then one of Caesar’s officers. These things are not consistent. You must be one man, either good or bad. You must cultivate either your own reason or else externals; apply yourself either to things within or without you—that is, be either a philosopher or one of the mob.

###CHAPTER### Xxx

Duties are always defined by relationships. Is a certain man your father? Then it is implied that you should take care of him, submit to him in all things, and patiently accept his criticism and correction. But he is a bad father. Is your natural bond, then, to a good father? No, but to a father. Is a brother unjust? Well, preserve your own just relationship toward him. Do not consider what he does, but what you must do to keep your own will in a state that conforms to nature. For another cannot hurt you unless you allow it. You will be hurt when you agree to be hurt. In this way, therefore, if you accustom yourself to contemplate the relationships of neighbor, citizen, and commander, you can deduce from each the corresponding duties.

###CHAPTER### Xxxi

Be assured that the heart of piety toward the gods lies in this—to form right opinions about them, as existing and as governing the universe justly and well. And fix yourself in this resolution, to obey them, and yield to them, and willingly follow them in all events, as being ruled by the most perfect wisdom. For in this way you will never find fault with the gods, nor accuse them of neglecting you. And this cannot be achieved in any other way than by withdrawing yourself from things that are not within our own power, and by making good or evil consist only in those that are. For if you suppose any other things to be either good or evil, it is inevitable that, when you are disappointed in what you wish for or meet with what you would avoid, you should reproach and blame those who caused it. For every creature is naturally formed to flee and hate things that appear harmful and whatever causes them, and to pursue and admire those that appear beneficial and whatever causes them. It is impossible, then, that one who believes himself to be hurt should rejoice in the person who, as he thinks, hurts him, just as it is impossible to rejoice in the hurt itself. Hence, also, a father is insulted by his son when he does not give him the things that seem to be good; and this made Polynices and Eteocles[4] mutual enemies—that empire seemed good to both. For this reason the farmer insults the gods; and so do the sailor, the merchant, or those who have lost wife or child. For where our interest is, there, too, piety is directed. So that whoever is careful to regulate his desires and aversions as he ought is thus made careful of piety as well. But it also becomes incumbent on everyone to offer libations and sacrifices and first fruits, according to the customs of his country, purely, and not carelessly nor negligently; not greedily, nor yet extravagantly.

###CHAPTER### Xxxii

When you turn to fortune-telling, remember that you do not know what will happen, and you go to learn it from the fortune-teller. But if you have a philosophical mind, you already know what kind of thing it is before you go. For if it is among the things not in your own power, it can by no means be either good or evil. Therefore, do not bring desire or fear with you to the fortune-teller. Otherwise you will approach him trembling. Instead, first clearly understand that every event is indifferent and nothing to you, whatever kind it may be. For it will be in your power to make good use of it, and no one can prevent this. Then come with confidence to the gods as your counselors. And afterwards, when any advice is given to you, remember what counselors you have chosen, and whose advice you will ignore if you disobey. Come to fortune-telling as Socrates prescribed: only in cases where the whole question relates to the outcome, and where reason or any other skill offers no way to discover the matter at hand. Therefore, when it is our duty to share the danger of a friend or of our country, we should not consult the oracle about whether we should share it with them or not. For even if the fortune-teller warns you that the omens are unfavorable, this means no more than that death, injury, or exile is predicted. But we have reason within us, and it directs us to stand by our friend and our country even with these dangers. So listen to the greater fortune-teller, the Pythian God, who once threw out of the temple the man who failed to save his friend.

###CHAPTER### Xxxiii

First, decide on a character and manner for yourself that you can maintain both when you are alone and when you are with others.

Be mostly silent, or say only what is necessary, and in few words. We may sometimes join in conversation sparingly when the occasion calls for it, but do not let it drift onto common subjects like gladiators, horse races, athletic champions, food, or drink—the vulgar topics of talk—and especially not onto other people, whether to blame them, praise them, or make comparisons. If you can, steer the conversation of your company toward proper subjects by your own example, but if you happen to find yourself among strangers, remain silent.

Do not let your laughter be loud, frequent, or excessive.

Avoid taking oaths altogether if possible; at any rate, avoid them as much as you are able.

Avoid public and vulgar entertainments, but if an occasion ever calls you to them, keep your attention alert so that you do not slip unnoticed into vulgarity. For be assured that even if a person is entirely pure himself, if his companion is corrupted, the one who keeps company with him will be corrupted as well.

Provide for the needs of the body only as far as absolute necessity requires, such as food, drink, clothing, housing, and servants. But cut off everything that tends toward display and luxury.

Before marriage, guard yourself with all your ability from unlawful sexual relations with women, yet do not be unkind or harsh to those who are led into this, and do not boast frequently that you yourself behave differently.

If anyone tells you that a certain person speaks ill of you, do not make excuses about what is said of you, but answer: “He did not know my other faults, otherwise he would not have mentioned only these.”

It is not necessary for you to appear often at public spectacles, but if there is ever a proper occasion for you to be there, do not appear more concerned for anyone else than for yourself—that is, wish things to be only just as they are, and only the best man to win, for then nothing will go against you. But abstain entirely from shouting, mockery, and violent emotions. And when you leave, do not talk at length about what happened and what contributes nothing to your own improvement, for such talk would make it seem that you were dazzled by the show.

Do not be quick or eager to attend private recitations, but if you do attend, preserve your seriousness and dignity, while still avoiding making yourself disagreeable.

When you are going to meet with anyone, and especially with someone who seems your superior, picture to yourself how Socrates or Zeno would behave in such a case, and you will not be at a loss to handle properly whatever may occur.

When you are going before anyone in power, imagine to yourself that you may not find him at home, that you may be shut out, that the doors may not be opened to you, that he may not notice you. If, with all this, it is still your duty to go, bear what happens and never say to yourself, “It was not worth so much,” for this is vulgar and like a man confused by external things.

In company, avoid talking too often or too much about your own actions and dangers. However pleasant it may be to you to mention the risks you have run, others do not find it equally pleasant to hear about your adventures. Avoid also trying to make people laugh, for this can easily lead you into vulgarity and may lower you in the esteem of your acquaintances. Approaches to indecent talk are likewise dangerous. Therefore, when anything of this sort happens, use the first suitable opportunity to rebuke the person who speaks that way, or at least show by silence, blushing, and a serious look that you are displeased by such talk.

###CHAPTER### Xxxiv

If you are dazzled by the appearance of any promised pleasure, guard yourself against being carried away by it. Let the matter wait until you are ready, and give yourself some delay. Then bring to mind both points of time: the time when you will enjoy the pleasure, and the time when you will regret it and blame yourself after you have enjoyed it. Set against these how you will rejoice and praise yourself if you hold back. And even if it seems like a fitting pleasure at the time, take care that its charms and temptations do not overcome you. Set against this how much better it is to know that you have won so great a victory.

###CHAPTER### Xxxv

When you decide clearly that something should be done, never hesitate to do it openly, even if other people misunderstand you. If the action is wrong, avoid doing it at all. If it is right, why fear those who criticize you unfairly?

###CHAPTER### Xxxvi

The statement “either it is day or it is night” works well in an either-or argument, but not at all in a both-and one. In the same way, at a meal, taking the largest portion suits the appetite of the body, but it is completely out of step with the social spirit of the gathering. So when you eat with someone else, remember to consider not only what the food before you is worth to your body, but also the value of showing proper courtesy to your host.

###CHAPTER### Xxxvii

If you have taken on a role beyond your strength, you have both lowered yourself in that and given up one which you might have managed.

###CHAPTER### Xxxviii

Just as when you walk you are careful not to step on a nail or twist your foot, be equally careful not to harm the ruling part of your mind. And if we guarded against this in every action, we would begin any action more safely.

###CHAPTER### Xxxix

The body is the right measure for its possessions, just as the foot is the right measure for a shoe. If you stop at this, you will keep the proper measure. But if you go beyond it, you will be carried forward as if down a cliff. For example, with a shoe, if you go beyond what fits the foot, it first becomes gilded, then purple, and then studded with jewels. For what once goes beyond the proper measure has no limit.

###CHAPTER### Xl

Women from fourteen years old are flattered by men with the title of mistresses. Therefore, perceiving that they are regarded only as qualified to give men pleasure, they begin to adorn themselves, and in that to place all their hopes. It is worth while, therefore, to try that they may perceive themselves honored only so far as they appear beautiful in their demeanor and modestly virtuous.

###CHAPTER### Xli

It is a sign of a weak mind to spend too much time on matters of the body, such as overdoing exercise, eating and drinking, and other physical needs. These things should be done only as needed, while our main energy should go toward reason.

###CHAPTER### Xlii

When someone treats you badly or speaks badly of you, remember that he acts or speaks because he believes it is the right thing for him to do. He cannot follow what seems right to you, only what seems right to him. So if he judges from false appearances, he is the one who is hurt, because he is the one who is deceived. For if anyone takes a true statement to be false, the statement is not hurt; only the man is deceived. Starting from these principles, you will bear patiently with a person who insults you, for you will say on every occasion, “It seemed so to him.”

###CHAPTER### Xliii

Everything has two handles: one by which it can be carried, and another by which it cannot. If your brother acts unjustly, do not take hold of the matter by the handle of his injustice, because by that handle it cannot be carried. Instead, take hold of it by the opposite handle—that he is your brother, that he was brought up with you. In this way you will take hold of it as it is meant to be carried.

###CHAPTER### Xliv

These arguments have no logical connection: “I am richer than you, therefore I am your superior.” “I am more eloquent than you, therefore I am your superior.” The true logical connection is rather this: “I am richer than you, therefore my possessions must exceed yours.” “I am more eloquent than you, therefore my style must surpass yours.” But you, after all, consist neither in property nor in style.

###CHAPTER### Xlv

Does anyone bathe in a hurry? Do not say that he does it badly, but that he does it hastily. Does anyone drink a lot of wine? Do not say that he does badly, but that he drinks a great deal. For unless you perfectly understand his motives, how should you know if he acts badly? In this way you will not risk giving in to any impressions except those you fully understand.

###CHAPTER### Xlvi

Never call yourself a philosopher, and do not talk much among the ignorant about your principles, but show them through your actions. For example, at a dinner, do not lecture people on how they ought to eat, but eat as you ought to. Remember that Socrates also avoided all showing off in this way. When people came to him and asked to be introduced to philosophers, he simply took them and introduced them; so well did he accept being overlooked. If a discussion of principles ever arises among the ignorant, remain silent for the most part. There is great danger in hastily throwing out what is not yet digested. And if someone tells you that you know nothing, and you are not irritated by it, then you may be sure that you have really begun your work. For sheep do not hastily throw up the grass to show the shepherds how much they have eaten, but, inwardly digesting their food, they produce it outwardly in wool and milk. In the same way, do not make a display of your principles before the ignorant, but show the actions to which their digestion gives rise.

###CHAPTER### Xlvii

When you have learned to feed your body simply, do not be proud of it. And if you drink water, do not keep saying on every occasion, “I drink water.” First think how much more simply the poor live than we do, and how much more patiently they endure hardship. If at any time you want to train yourself through practice to endure work and going without, do it for your own sake and not to impress others. Do not attempt great feats. But when you are very thirsty, just rinse your mouth with water, and tell no one.

###CHAPTER### Xlviii

The condition and characteristic of a vulgar person is that he never looks for either help or harm from himself, but only from externals. The condition and characteristic of a philosopher is that he looks to himself for all help or harm. The marks of a proficient are that he censures no one, praises no one, blames no one, accuses no one; says nothing concerning himself as being anybody or knowing anything. When he is in any instance hindered or restrained, he accuses himself; and if he is praised, he smiles to himself at the person who praises him; and if he is censured, he makes no defense. But he goes about with the caution of a convalescent, careful of interference with anything that is doing well but not yet quite secure. He restrains desire; he transfers his aversion to those things only which thwart the proper use of our own will; he employs his energies moderately in all directions; if he appears stupid or ignorant, he does not care; and, in a word, he keeps watch over himself as over an enemy and one in ambush.

###CHAPTER### Xlix

When someone is proud of being able to understand and explain the works of Chrysippus, say to yourself: "Unless Chrysippus had written unclearly, this person would have had nothing to be proud of. But what do I want? To understand nature and follow her. So I ask who explains nature. When I hear that Chrysippus does, I turn to him. I do not understand his writings. So I look for someone to explain them." Up to this point, there is nothing to be proud of. And when I find an interpreter, what remains is to put his teachings into practice. This alone is what matters. But if I admire only the explanation, what do I become more than a scholar of language instead of a philosopher? The only difference is that instead of Homer, I explain Chrysippus. So when someone asks me to read Chrysippus to him, I feel ashamed when I cannot show actions that match and agree with his teachings.

L

Whatever principles you have adopted, keep them as laws, as if it would be wrong to break them. Do not pay attention to what anyone says about you, because that is not your concern. How much longer will you wait to demand the best improvements from yourself, and never go against the judgments of reason? You have received the philosophical principles you should be familiar with, and you have been familiar with them. So what other teacher are you waiting for to excuse this delay in improving yourself? You are no longer a boy but a grown man. If you remain careless and lazy, always adding delay to delay, intention to intention, and setting one day after another to attend to yourself, you will quietly continue to accomplish nothing. Living and dying, you will remain ordinary in mind. So right now, think yourself worthy of living as a grown man making progress. Let whatever seems best be an unbreakable law for you. And if any experience of pain or pleasure, fame or disgrace, comes before you, remember that now is the contest, now the Olympic games have arrived, and they cannot be postponed. Remember that by one failure and defeat, honor may be lost or won. This is how Socrates became complete, improving himself through everything and following reason alone. And though you are not yet a Socrates, you should still live as someone trying to become one.

###CHAPTER### Li

The first and most necessary topic in philosophy is the practical application of principles, such as, "We ought not to lie." The second is that of demonstrations, such as, "Why is it that we ought not to lie?" The third is that which gives strength and logical connection to the other two, such as, "Why is this a demonstration?" For what is demonstration? What is a consequence? What is a contradiction? What is truth? What is falsehood? The third point is then necessary on account of the second, and the second on account of the first. But the most necessary, and that on which we ought to rest, is the first. But we do just the contrary. For we spend all our time on the third point and employ all our diligence about that, and entirely neglect the first. Therefore, at the same time that we lie, we are very ready to show how it is demonstrated that lying is wrong.

Upon all occasions we ought to have these maxims ready at hand:

Conduct me, Zeus, and thou, O Destiny,

Wherever your decrees have fixed my lot.

I follow cheerfully; and, did I not,

Wicked and wretched, I must follow still.[8]

Who'er yields properly to Fate is deemed

Wise among men, and knows the laws of Heaven.[9]

And this third:

"O Crito, if it thus pleases the gods, thus let it be."[10]

"Anytus and Melitus may kill me indeed; but hurt me they cannot."[11]
