###TITLE### The Cask of Amontillado
###AUTHOR### Edgar Allan Poe

###CHAPTER### 

I had put up with a thousand injuries from Fortunato as best I could, but when he insulted me, I vowed revenge. You, who know my soul so well, will not suppose that I made any threat. In the end I would be avenged; this was definitely settled—but the very certainty of it ruled out any risk. I must not only punish, but punish without being caught. A wrong is not made right when the one who corrects it is punished in return. It is also not made right when the avenger fails to make himself known as such to the one who has done the wrong.

It must be understood that I had given Fortunato no reason, by word or deed, to doubt my good will. I continued, as was my habit, to smile in his face, and he did not see that my smile now was at the thought of his destruction.

He had a weak point—this Fortunato—although in other ways he was a man to be respected and even feared. He prided himself on his expertise in wine. Few Italians have the true spirit of the expert. For the most part their enthusiasm is put on to suit the time and opportunity—to deceive the British and Austrian millionaires. In painting and gems, Fortunato, like his countrymen, was a fake—but in the matter of old wines he was sincere. In this respect I did not differ from him much: I was skillful in Italian vintages myself, and bought largely whenever I could.

It was about dusk, one evening during the wild madness of the carnival season, that I met my friend. He greeted me with excessive warmth, for he had been drinking a great deal. The man wore a clown's costume. He had on a tight-fitting outfit of mixed stripes, and his head was topped by a cone-shaped cap with bells. I was so pleased to see him that I thought I would never stop shaking his hand.

I said to him, "My dear Fortunato, you are luckily met. How remarkably well you are looking today! But I have received a cask of what is supposed to be Amontillado, and I have my doubts."

"How?" he said. "Amontillado? A cask? Impossible! And in the middle of the carnival!"

"I have my doubts," I replied, "and I was silly enough to pay the full Amontillado price without asking you about it. You were nowhere to be found, and I was afraid of losing a bargain."

"Amontillado!"

"I have my doubts."

"Amontillado!"

"And I must settle them."

"Amontillado!"

"Since you are busy, I am on my way to Luchesi. If anyone has a critical taste, it is he. He will tell me—"

"Luchesi cannot tell Amontillado from Sherry."

"And yet some fools will claim that his taste is a match for your own."

"Come, let us go."

"Where?"

"To your vaults."

"My friend, no; I will not take advantage of your good nature. I see you have an engagement. Luchesi—"

"I have no engagement; come."

"My friend, no. It is not the engagement, but the severe cold that I see you are suffering from. The vaults are unbearably damp. They are covered with saltpeter."

"Let us go, nevertheless. The cold is nothing. Amontillado! You have been cheated. And as for Luchesi, he cannot tell Sherry from Amontillado."

Saying this, Fortunato took my arm. Putting on a mask of black silk and drawing a cloak closely about my body, I let him hurry me to my palazzo.

There were no servants at home; they had slipped away to celebrate the occasion. I had told them I would not return until morning and had given them strict orders not to leave the house. I knew these orders were enough to guarantee their immediate disappearance, every one of them, as soon as my back was turned.

I took two torches from their holders, gave one to Fortunato, and bowed him through several rooms to the archway that led into the vaults. I went down a long and winding staircase, asking him to be careful as he followed. We finally reached the bottom and stood together on the damp ground of the Montresor catacombs.

My friend's walk was unsteady, and the bells on his cap jingled as he moved.

"The cask," he said.

"It is farther on," I said; "but look at the white web-like growth gleaming on these cavern walls."

He turned toward me and looked into my eyes with two cloudy orbs that watered from drunkenness.

"Saltpeter?" he asked at last.

"Saltpeter," I replied. "How long have you had that cough?"

"Ugh! ugh! ugh!--ugh! ugh! ugh!--ugh! ugh! ugh!--ugh! ugh! ugh!--ugh! ugh! ugh!"

My poor friend could not answer for many minutes.

"It is nothing," he said at last.

"Come," I said firmly, "we will go back; your health is precious. You are rich, respected, admired, loved; you are happy, as I once was. You are a man who would be missed. For me it does not matter. We will go back; you will become ill, and I cannot be responsible. Besides, there is Luchesi—"

"Enough," he said; "the cough is nothing; it will not kill me. I shall not die of a cough."

"True—true," I replied; "and indeed I did not mean to alarm you unnecessarily—but you should take proper precautions. A drink of this Medoc will protect us from the damp."

Here I knocked the neck off a bottle I took from a long row of its companions lying on the mold.

"Drink," I said, offering him the wine.

He raised it to his lips with a sly grin. He paused and nodded to me in a familiar way, while his bells jingled.

"I drink," he said, "to the buried who rest around us."

"And I to your long life."

He took my arm again, and we went on.

"These vaults," he said, "are vast."

"The Montresors," I replied, "were a great and numerous family."

"I forget your coat of arms."

"A huge human foot of gold, on a blue field; the foot crushes a serpent rearing up whose fangs are sunk into the heel."

"And the motto?"

"No one attacks me with impunity."

"Good!" he said.

The wine sparkled in his eyes and the bells jingled. My own imagination grew warm with the Medoc. We had passed through walls of piled bones, with casks and large barrels mixed among them, into the deepest recesses of the catacombs. I paused again, and this time I dared to seize Fortunato by an arm above the elbow.

"The saltpeter!" I said; "see, it increases. It hangs like moss upon the vaults. We are below the riverbed. The drops of moisture trickle among the bones. Come, we will go back before it is too late. Your cough—"

"It is nothing," he said; "let us go on. But first, another drink of the Medoc."

I broke open and handed him a flask of De Grave. He emptied it in one breath. His eyes flashed with a fierce light. He laughed and threw the bottle upward with a gesture I did not understand.

I looked at him in surprise. He repeated the movement—a grotesque one.

"You do not understand?" he said.

"No," I replied.

"Then you are not of the brotherhood."

"How?"

"You are not one of the Masons."

"Yes, yes," I said; "yes, yes."

"You? Impossible! A Mason?"

"A Mason," I replied.

"A sign," he said, "a sign."

"It is this," I answered, producing a trowel from beneath the folds of my cloak.

"You joke," he exclaimed, stepping back a few paces. "But let us proceed to the Amontillado."

"So be it," I said, putting the tool back beneath my cloak and again offering him my arm. He leaned upon it heavily. We continued our route in search of the Amontillado. We passed through a series of low arches, descended, passed on, and descending again, arrived at a deep crypt, where the foulness of the air caused our torches to glow rather than flame.

At the most remote end of the crypt there appeared another, less spacious one. Its walls had been lined with human remains, piled to the vault overhead, in the fashion of the great catacombs of Paris. Three sides of this interior crypt were still decorated in this manner. From the fourth side the bones had been thrown down, and lay scattered upon the earth, forming at one point a mound of some size. Within the wall thus exposed by the displacing of the bones, we perceived a still deeper recess, about four feet deep, three feet wide, and six or seven feet high. It seemed to have been constructed for no particular purpose of its own, but formed merely the interval between two of the colossal supports of the roof of the catacombs, and was backed by one of their surrounding walls of solid granite.

It was in vain that Fortunato, lifting his dull torch, tried to peer into the depth of the recess. The feeble light did not allow us to see its end.

"Proceed," I said; "herein is the Amontillado. As for Luchesi—"

"He is an ignoramus," interrupted my friend, as he stepped unsteadily forward, while I followed immediately at his heels. In an instant he had reached the end of the niche, and finding his progress stopped by the rock, stood stupidly bewildered. A moment more and I had chained him to the granite. In its surface were two iron staples, about two feet apart, horizontally. From one of these hung a short chain, from the other a padlock. Throwing the links about his waist, it was but the work of a few seconds to secure it. He was too astounded to resist. Withdrawing the key, I stepped back from the recess.

"Pass your hand," I said, "over the wall; you cannot help feeling the nitre. Indeed, it is very damp. Once more let me implore you to return. No? Then I must positively leave you. But I must first give you all the little attentions in my power."

"The Amontillado!" exclaimed my friend, not yet recovered from his astonishment.

"True," I replied; "the Amontillado."

As I said these words I busied myself among the pile of bones of which I have already spoken. Throwing them aside, I soon uncovered a quantity of building stone and mortar. With these materials and with the aid of my trowel, I began vigorously to wall up the entrance of the niche.

I had scarcely laid the first tier of the masonry when I discovered that Fortunato's drunkenness had largely worn off. The earliest sign I had of this was a low moaning cry from the depth of the recess. It was not the cry of a drunken man. There was then a long and stubborn silence. I laid the second tier, and the third, and the fourth; and then I heard the furious rattling of the chain. The noise lasted for several minutes, during which, so that I might listen to it with more satisfaction, I stopped my work and sat down upon the bones. When at last the clanking subsided, I picked up the trowel again and finished without interruption the fifth, the sixth, and the seventh tier. The wall was now nearly level with my chest. I paused again, and holding the torch over the masonry, threw a few feeble rays upon the figure within.

A series of loud and shrill screams, bursting suddenly from the throat of the chained form, seemed to thrust me violently back. For a brief moment I hesitated—I trembled. Drawing my rapier, I began to grope with it about the recess; but a moment's thought reassured me. I placed my hand upon the solid fabric of the catacombs and felt satisfied. I approached the wall again. I replied to the yells of him who clamoured. I echoed them—I aided them—I surpassed them in volume and in strength. I did this, and the clamourer grew still.

It was now midnight, and my task was drawing to a close. I had completed the eighth, the ninth, and the tenth tier. I had finished a portion of the last and the eleventh; there remained but a single stone to be fitted and plastered in. I struggled with its weight; I placed it partially in its intended position. But now there came from out the niche a low laugh that made the hairs on my head stand up. It was followed by a sad voice, which I had difficulty in recognizing as that of the noble Fortunato. The voice said—

"Ha! ha! ha!—he! he! he!—a very good joke indeed—an excellent jest. We shall have many a rich laugh about it at the palazzo—he! he! he!—over our wine—he! he! he!"

"The Amontillado!" I said.

"He! he! he!—he! he! he!—yes, the Amontillado. But is it not getting late? Will they not be waiting for us at the palazzo, the Lady Fortunato and the rest? Let us be gone."

"Yes," I said, "let us be gone."

"For the love of God, Montresor!"

"Yes," I said, "for the love of God!"

But to these words I listened in vain for a reply. I grew impatient. I called aloud—

"Fortunato!"

No answer. I called again—

"Fortunato—"

No answer still. I thrust a torch through the remaining opening and let it fall within. There came forth in reply only a jingling of the bells. My heart grew sick on account of the dampness of the catacombs. I hurried to finish my work. I forced the last stone into its position; I plastered it up. Against the new masonry I re-erected the old rampart of bones. For half a century no mortal has disturbed them. In pace requiescat!
