CAMBRIDGE.

To ---- ----.

Spring saw my little venture just begun; and then your hospitable message came, inviting me to taste the strawberries

at Strawberry Hill. I went. How long I stayed, urged by dear friends and the restoring breeze, let me not say; long enough to complete my rhythmic structure. Day by day it grew, and all sweet influences helped its growth. The lawn sloped green and ample till the trees met on its margin; and the Hudson's tide rolled beautiful beyond, where purple gleams fell on the Palisades or touched the hills of the opposing shore. For all without was but an emblem of the symmetry

I found within, where love held perfect sway, with taste and beauty and domestic peace for its allies.

We do not praise the rose, since all who see it know it is the rose. And so, dear lady, praise of thee would seem, to all who know thee, quite superfluous. But if from any of these thoughts be shed aught of the fragrance and the hue of truth, to thee I dedicate the transient flower in which the eternal beauty reappears; knowing, should poison mingle with the sweet, thou, like the eclectic bee, with instinct sure, wilt take the good alone, and leave the bad.

E. S.

THE WOMAN WHO DARED.

I.

OVERTURE.

Blessed Power that can transform common things, and, like the sun, make the clod burst into bloom—unseal the fountain so silent these many days, and help me sing of Linda! Why of her, since she would shrink with obvious recoil if she knew her deeds were made a theme for measured verse? Why leave the garden flowers to fix the eye on one poor violet

that sheds fragrance on the solitary grove? There are themes enough that court wide regard and prompt a strenuous flight; and yet from all, my thoughts come back to Linda. Let me spare, as best I may, her modest privacy,

while under Fancy's not unsuitable disguise I give substantial truth, and deal with no unreal beings or fantastic facts:

Bear witness to it, Linda!

Now while May keeps me a restless prisoner in the house—for the first time the Spring's unkindness ever held me apart from her companionship,

however roughly from the east her breath came, as if all the icebergs of Grand Bank were giving up their forms in that one gust—now while on orchard-trees the struggling blossoms break from the varnished coverings, and in clouds of pink and white float round the boughs that hold their greenness yet in check—and while the lawn lures from yon hemlock hedge the robin, plump and copper-breasted, and the west wind brings mildness and balm—let me attempt the task that also is a pastime.

What though Spring brings not the wonder and the zest of Youth; the hopes, the day-dreams, and the exultations? The animal life whose overflow and waste would far out-measure now our little hoard? The health that made mere physical existence an ample joy; that on the ocean beach

shared with the leaping waves their breezy glee; that in deep woods, or in forsaken clearings, where the charred logs were hid by new verdure, and the shy wood-thrush lighted; or on hills whence counties lay outspread beneath our gaze; or by some rock-girt lake where sandy margins sloped to the mirrored tints of waving trees—could feel no burden in the grasshopper, and no unrest in the long summer day?

Would I consider Youth's fervors fair return for temperate airs that fan sublimer heights than Youth could scale; heights whence the patient vision may see this life's harsh inequalities, its rudimental good and full-blown evil, its crimes and earthquakes and insanities, and all the wrongs and sorrows that perplex us, assume, beneath the eternal calm, the order which can come only from a Love Divine? A love that sees the good beyond the evil, the serial life beyond the eclipsing death—that tracks the spirit through eternities, backward and forward, and in every germ beholds its past, its present, and its future, at every stage beholds it gravitate

where it belongs, and thence new-born emerge into new life and opportunity,

an outcast never from the assiduous Mercy, providing for His teeming universe,

divinely perfect not because complete, but because incomplete, advancing ever beneath the care Supreme?—heights whence the soul, uplifted from all speculative fog,

all darkening doctrine, all confusing fear, can see the drifted plants, can scent the odors, that surely come from that celestial shore to which we tend; however out of reckoning, swept wrong by Error's currents, Passion's storms, the poor tossed bark may be?

Come down, my thoughts! Your subject lies as low as a ground-bird's nest. Why do you try, with wings so feeble and unused, to soar above the clouds and face the stars? Come down from your high venture, and descend to scenes from the heart's common history!

II.

THE FATHER'S STORY.

The little house was full of sunshine. The western windows overlooked the Hudson, where the great city's traffic disturbed the tide. The front received the early light of the east. Here lived three people, who the neighbors said were husband, wife, and daughter. And indeed there was no sign that they were otherwise. Their name was Percival. They lived secluded and saw no society, except some poor

old pensioner who came for food or help. Though, when fair days invited, they would take the omnibus and go to see the paintings at the Academy, or hear the music

at opera or concert. Then, in summer,

a visit to the seaside or the hills

would often entice them.

Percival had reached his seventy-five years, but stood erect as if no touch of age had chilled him yet. He was simple in habit, and studious about how to live

in best conformity with divine laws. He was impulsive, yet taught by trial to question all impulses, affections, appetites,

at Reason's bar. Two goals seemed steadily before him. One was to find the eternal truth, showing the constant right in politics, in social life, in morals. The other was to apply all love and wisdom to the education of his child—of Linda.

Yet, if with anointed eye, you could look on that benign and tranquil face, you might detect the lines which Passion leaves long after its volcano is extinct

and flowers conceal its lava. Percival was older than his wife by twenty years. Yet they were well matched. Though, with her, Time had dealt very gently, leaving her face and rounded form still youthful, and unmarred by one uncomely outline. Time had hardly mingled a thread of silver in her chestnut hair, which was so abundant it needed no deceiving braid. The matron seemed framed for motherhood. Three times she had been a mother. But the children the first six winters of her union brought—a boy and girl—were lost to her at once by a wall's falling on them, as they went, heedless of danger, hand in hand, to school. To either parent the blow was terrible!

But, three years afterward, when Linda came, with her dark azure eyes and golden hair, it was as if a healing angel touched

the parents' wound, and turned their desolation into a present paradise, revealing

two dear ones, beckoning from the spirit-land, and one, detaining them here below with infant grasp—feeble, yet how irresistible!

And so there was great comfort in that household. And those unwhispered longings both had felt at times—that they might pass to other scenes where Love would find its own—were felt no more. For Linda grew in beauty every day.

Beauty not only of the outward form,

sparkling in those dear eyes, and on the wind tossing those locks of gold. But beauty born, in revelations flitting over the face, from the soul's inner symmetry; from love too deep and pure to utter, had she words; from the divine desire to know; to prove all objects brought within her dawning awareness; from playful mirth, not heedless but most apt; from sense of conscience, shown in little things so early; and from infant courtesy

charming and debonair.

The parents said, while the glad tears shone brimming in their eyes, "Oh! lacking love and best experience

are those who tell us that the purity

And that the innocence of childhood is a delusion; or that, so far as they exist, they show the absence of all mind; no impulses

except those of selfish passion moving it! And that, by nature desperately wicked, the child learns good through evil; having no innate ideas, no inborn will, no bias. Here, in this infant, is our confutation! O self-sufficing physiologist,

who, grubbing in the earth, has missed the stars, we ask no other answer to your creed

than this, the answer heaven and earth supply."

Now Linda had seen sixteen summers, and grown to be a fair-haired, winsome maid, in shape and aspect promising to be

a softened repetition of her mother;

and yet some traits from the paternal side gave to the head an intellectual grace and to the liquid eyes a reserved power, brooding awhile in tender gloom, and then flashing emotion, as some lofty thought, some sight of pity, or some generous deed, kindled a ready sympathy whose tears

fell on no barren purpose; for with Linda to feel, to be uplifted, was to act;

her sorest trials being when she found how far the wish to do outran the power. Often would Percival observe his child, and study to divine if in the future

of that organization, when mature,

there should prevail the elements that lead woman to find the crowning charm of life in the affections of a happy marriage, or if with satisfactions of the mind

and the aesthetic faculty, the aims

of art and letters, the pursuits of trade, Linda might find the fresh activities

he craved for her, and which forecasting care might possibly provide.

His means were small,

merged in a life-annuity which gave

all that he held as indispensable

to sanative conditions in a home:

good air, good influences, proper food. By making his old wardrobe do long service he saved the wherewith to get faithful help from the best teachers in instructing Linda; and she was still the object uppermost. The day dawned fair, for Linda it was fair, and they all three could ramble in the Park. If on Broadway the ripe fruit tempted him, Linda was fond of fruit; those grapes will do for Linda. Was the music rich and rare? Linda must hear it. Were the paintings grand? Linda must see them. So the important thought was always Linda; and the mother shared in all this fond parental providence;

for in her tender pride in the dear girl there was no room for any selfish thought, for any jealous balancing of dues.

"My child," said Percival, one summer day, as he brought in a bunch of snow-white roses, ringed with carnations, many-leafed and fragrant, "take it, an offering for your birthday; this is June the twelfth, a happy day for me." "How fresh, how beautiful!" said Linda rising and kissing him on either cheek. "Dear father, you spoil me for all other care, I fear, since none can be like yours."

"Why speak of that?" he with a start exclaimed; "my care must be prolonged till I can see you safely fixed in an assured and happy womanhood.

Why should it not be so? Though sixty-five, how well am I, and strong! No, Linda, no; dream not of other tendance yet awhile; my father lived to eighty, and his father to eighty-five; and I am stronger now

than they were, at my age."

"Live long!" cried Linda, "for whom have I to love me, to befriend, you and my mother gone?"

"Your mother, child? She should outlive me by some twenty years at least. God grant, her sweet companionship may be your strength and light when I'm not here, my matchless little girl, my precious Linda!"

"Ah! how Love magnifies the thing it loves!" Smiling she said: "when I look in the glass, I see a comely Miss; nay, perhaps pretty; That word is the highest praise she can claim,

So far as person is concerned, I fear. Grant her a cheerful temper; that she gets from both her parents. She is dutiful—no wonder, for she is never opposed!

Strangely coincident her way is yours; industrious, but that is her mother's training. Then if you come to gifts of mind—ah me! What can she show? We will not pronounce her dull; but she is not apt or quick; and all she gets is by hard work, by oft-repeated trials, trials with intermissions of despair.

The languages she takes to not unkindly; but mathematics is her scourge, her kill-joy, pressing her like a nightmare. Logic, too, distresses and confuses her poor brain; oh! ask her not for reasons. As for music—music she loves. Would that Love might inspire the genius it reveres so ardently!

Has she no gift for painting? Eye for form and coloring I truly think she has;

And one thing she can do, and do it well; she can group flowers and ferns and autumn leaves, paint their true tints, and render back to nature a not unfaithful copy.

"This is the extent of her achievements! She has labored hard to mould a bust or statue; but the clay lacked the Pygmalion touch beneath her hands. She will never be a female Angelo.

She must come down content to mother Earth, and study out the alphabet which Summer weaves on the sod in fields or bordering woods. Such is your paragon, my simple father! But now, this ordinary little girl,

So seeming frank, (whisper it low!) is yet so deep, so crafty, and so full of wiles, that she has quite persuaded both her parents—in most things sensible, clear-seeing people—that she is just a prodigy indeed!

Not one of goodness merely, but of wit, capacity, and general cleverness!"

"There, that will do, spoilt darling! What a tongue!" Percival said, admiring while he chided. "O the swift time! You are seventeen today; and yet, except your parents and your teachers, friends and companions you have hardly known. It is fit that I should tell you why our life has been thus socially estranged and quiet. Sit down, and let me push the arm-chair up where I can note the changes in your face; for it is a traitor, that sweet face of yours, and has a sign for every fleeting thought.

"But here is our little mother! Come, my dear, and take a seat by Linda; you did help me to graft upon the bitter past a fruit

All sweetness, and your very presence now can take the sting from a too sad remembrance."

The mother placed her hand upon his brow and said: "The water-lily springs from mud; so springs the future from the past." Then he: "My father's death made me, at twenty-one, heir to a fortune which in those slow days was thought sufficient: I had quitted Yale with some slight reputation as a scholar, and, in the first flush of ingenuous youth when brave imagination's rosy hue

Tinges all unknown objects, I was launched into society in this great place;—

Sisterless, motherless, and having seen but little, in my student life, of women.

All mothers with marriageable daughters saw me as their rightful target and set their traps to catch me. Poor, inexperienced young man that I was, I was soon caught—caught in a snare indeed, though not through any mother's clever planning. Young, beautiful, and accomplished, she—my Fate—met me with smiles and doomed me even as she smiled. She was as quick as light, as fluid as molten lead in taking whatever shape was offered, apt to adopt any preference of taste or feeling

that might best flatter. She was friendly and kind, or seemed so, and generous to a fault—but only when she had a part to play. Affectionate—ah, there too she was pretending. As I look calmly back now, she seems to me the pure embodiment of a mind

that understood all the secrets of the heart and was quick to substitute a counterfeit

for the heart's genuine coin and make it pass as real. But she was as void of feeling as the knife that wounds. And so the game was in her hands, and she played it with confident, remorseless skill, even to the bitter end.

"Yet do not think that inner foresight never stirred or spoke. Though it is strangely veiled from consciousness, and its fine voice goes unheard amid the noise of outward things and the pursuit of earthly passion, there is an under-sense, a faculty

entirely independent of our mortal organs,

and limited by neither space nor time. Otherwise, where do those clairvoyant glimpses come from—that vision piercing to the distant future, those sudden warnings of impending ruin—if not from depths of soul that consciousness,

limited as it is in mortal scope, may not explore? Yet there, serenely hidden, or with a conscious being all their own, superior and apart from what we know

in this narrow keep we call our waking state, lie lofty powers growing with our growth, which we pay no attention to. Some may live a whole life and never heed them, nor know they have a soul. Many a plodding anthropologist,

philosopher, logician, scientist,

dismisses them as moonshine. But they are, no less, actual, proven, and in their dignity

and grasp and space-defying attributes, worthy to qualify a deathless spirit

to have the range of an infinity

through an unending period—at once

a promise and a proof of life immortal.

"One night—one mild, sweet night in early June—we two had walked the drawing-room together until ten o'clock. Then I took my leave and walked along the street, a block or more, when suddenly I looked up at a star,

and then a thought I could not fail to heed rushed from the soul's unexplored and solemn depths, crying, 'Back! Go back!' And back I went, as hastily as if it were a matter

of life or death. I did not stop to ring the doorbell, but sprang up, alert and silent, to the veranda of the open window,

drew back a blind without a sound, looked in, and through the waving muslin curtain saw—well, she was seated in a young man's lap, her head upon his shoulder.

"Quick of ear as a hunted hare, she heard me. She started up, ran to the curtain, eagerly drew me in, and said, while joy shone tenderly in her eyes, 'My brother Ambrose, just arrived from Europe!' So swift she was, she did not give me time even for one jealous pang. I took his hand, and saying, 'Anna's brother must be mine,' I bade them both good-night and went my way. So was I fooled—my better angel baffled!

"And yet once more the vivid warning came, flashed like sudden truth from her own eyes. We stood together in a ballroom, when a lady,

unknown to me, came up and regarded me

With strange compassion in her curious glance, And then, with something less divine than pity, Looked down on my betrothed, and moved away. I turned to Anna, but upon her face,

There was a look to startle like a ghost; Defiance, deadly fear, and murderous hate Were all so wildly blended! But it was gone— Gone like a flash before I well could mark it; And in its place there came a luminous smile, So childlike sweet, such type of heavenly candor, It would have served for a Madonna's mouth, To make the pilgrim's adoration easy.

"Who was that lady, Anna?" I inquired. "A Mrs. Lothian," was her reply:

"A lovely person, although somewhat haughty." We returned home soon after, and no more Was said of it.

"The rapid weeks flew by, And Anna plied her powers to charm, but still Not all the subtle glamour of her presence Could bind in sleep my pleading monitor. And so at last I said: 'We both are young: Let us, as earnest of a mutual wish

To share a perfect love, or none at all, Absolve each other here, without condition, From this engagement; and, if three years hence We both are of one heart, then shall we find The means to make it known; of that be sure! Are you in your own loyalty so fixed

As to accept the challenge? Would you prize The love of any man, who could not bear A test so simple?'

"The first word I spoke Made all my meaning plain to her; she shook, But more perhaps with anger than with grief; She turned her face away, and covered it With both her hands, and so remained until I had done speaking; then she rose at once, Her face averted still, (she durst not show it!) And grasped my hand, and, in a husky tone Sheathing her wrath, exclaimed: 'To-morrow, come At twelve—at twelve!' and rushed out of the room.

"Prompt at the hour I went; and in the parlor Sat down expectant; and she entered soon, Clad all in white; upon her face the marks Of passionate tears, and a beseeching sorrow In every look! A desk of ivory,

Take them, and give me mine.'

It is a trial merely of our loves?'

"'A light affair perhaps to you,' she said, 'But death to me. "And so she had her way, and we were married. The next day all Wall Street was stirred up by the news that brave Papa had become famous—not simply as a bankrupt, but as a swindler, escaping prison by the skin of his teeth. 'No matter! Papa has a son-in-law,

A greenhorn, as they say, who lives in a grand house on Fifth Avenue,

And, in his hall, Papa will hang his hat.' And, in all this, rumor only told the truth.

"Six months passed. Repeatedly I asked, 'Where is Brother Ambrose?' He, it seemed, was in such demand by the government that he could rarely be spared for home enjoyment. But at last I did meet Brother Ambrose, and once again I found him—

"Well, the scales dropped from my eyes. I needed no other proof than a quick look I saw the two exchange—forgetting a mirror beside them—to see that I was betrayed. He was no brother. I looked for more proof; but they, imagining I knew more than I did, acted quickly. Before I could find a way to seek a divorce, she got ahead of me, took the initiative herself, and played the victim, trapping me as the guilty party in the eyes of the law.

"It was a plot so skillfully arranged,

All the precautions and schemes

Were so carefully planned in advance; the lies under oath were all so well coordinated; my innocent life was made to seem so guilty; the witnesses were so well rehearsed, so perfect in their roles—in short, it was such a thorough work of art that I did not wonder at the court's decision, which was, for her—divorce and alimony; for me—no freedom, since I was denied the right to marry again. Such was the decree!"

"I'm glad you rejected it as you did!" cried Linda, while her cheeks flushed and hot, indignant tears answered her anger. Then she kissed her father on each cheek and tenderly embraced her mother too; and they, meanwhile, with a slight moisture in their smiling eyes, exchanged a nod. Then Percival said to Linda: "Why, what an utter rebel you would be, you little champion of the higher law! Sit down, and hear me out."

"If that is their justice," cried Linda, unable to contain herself and panting, "who would not reject it and hurl back defiance at all the shallow judges on the bench—at them and their decrees!"

"My little girl," the father said, "the heart's impulsive choice may guide us safely when the act must be born of the instant, but let reason rule when reason may. For some twelve years I lived a wandering life in Europe; not so crushed by my harsh experience but that I

Grew larger as age drew nearer; and at forty a weariness of life came over me,

And I was sick at heart; for many a joy had lost the charm that made it joy. I took a house in London, all for solitude,

And there found what you may not find in Egypt, or on Mont Blanc.

"One day as I was crossing an obscure street, I saw a crowd of workmen gathered around a man on the ground. A rafter from a half-built house had fallen, and he was badly injured. Seeing no one

And you've been jealous, I suppose, at times, of the poor runaway?" "Ay, that I have! Bitterly jealous."

"Jealousy and love were never yet true mates. For jealousy is born of selfish passion, lust, or pride, while love is so divine and pure a thing, it only takes what cannot be withheld. It flies constraint. All that it gives is given, even as the lily renders up its perfume, because it cannot help it. Would it crave return less worthy? Would it be content with a grudged gift? Then it is something else, not love—not love! Ah me! How men and women cozen themselves with words, and let their passions fool them and blind, until they madly hug illusions which some stunning shock like yours puts to the proof, revealing emptiness. Have you a loving heart, and would you feed it on what the swine have left—mock it with lies?" "Speak this to me again, when I am stronger," said Kenrick, smiling faintly. Then I left him, and taking up The Times looked through the list of "Wants." And one amid the many hundred instantly caught my eye. "And now, my dear, this little girl with eager eyes has, for a summer morning, heard enough. The weather is the crown of all that June has of most fair—the year's transcendent day, when the young foliage and the perfect air intoxicate the birds, and put our hearts in harmony with their extravagance

of joy and love. Come, come! To slight this day would be a sin. We'll ramble in the Park, and take our dinner there, and see the flowers, the children, and the swans, and all the places which Linda used to love in babyhood,

when, in her little carriage, like a queen she'd sit, receiving homage from all eyes."

The father had his way; and in the Park they spent the happy time, and felt the charm which harmony complete with Nature brings when loving spirits, unpreoccupied,

Gain by surrender, and grow rich by giving. O sunshine and blue sky and gentle airs! To human happiness, like daily bread,

Your blessings come, until the unthinking heart does not realize the debt we owe your silent powers. If you can give so much, what may not He, of whose omnipotence you are but shadows, have in reserve in his eternities!

III.

THE MOTHER'S STORY.

That evening, when the feast of strawberries had been enjoyed, and the happy three sat down together, Linda asked: "And now, may I not hear the rest?" "Tomorrow, Linda, you shall hear all," said Percival; "but now, that brain of yours must calm itself before you try to sleep; and so, tonight, let us have 'Annie Laurie,' 'Bonnie Doon,' and songs that most offend the refined ear of modern fashion." Linda played and sang for a full half-hour; then, turning on her chair, said, "Now shall mother sing that cradle song you made for me when I was an infant. Mother, imagine you are rocking me to sleep,

As in those far-off days."

Replied the mother: "O the dear days! Yet not more dear than these! For careful Linda brings along with her all of her past; the infant's purity,

The child's trusting love, and now, at last, the maiden's free and quick intelligence! Be ever thus, my Linda; for the pure

In heart shall carry an immortal youth into the great hereafter. That little song—well I remember the delightful time

When it was made up on the spot; when, with my pen, I noted down the words, while, by your crib, your father sat, and you, with little fists drawn tight, would spring and start, as infants will, crowing the while, and chuckling at the words not yet understood, except in the smiles that went with them! It was at the mellow close of an autumn day, and we were staying in a secluded village, where a brook

Babbled beneath our window, and the hum of insects soothed us, while a louder note from the hoarse frog's bassoon would, now and then, break on the cricket's sleepy monotone and startle laughter." Here the matron paused; then sweeping, with a firm, elastic touch, the ivory keys, sang

LINDA'S LULLABY.

I.

Murmur low, little rivulet flowing!

For to sleep our dear Linda is going; all good little lambs be reposing,

For Linda one eyelid is closing.

II.

O frogs! What a noise you are making! O crickets! Now don't keep her waking! Stop barking, you little dog Rover,

Till Linda can get half-seas over.

III.

Little birds, let our word of love reach you—go to bed, go to sleep, I beg you; on her little white coverlet lying,

To sleep our dear Linda is trying.

IV.

Hush! Sing just as softly as may be; sing lullaby, lullaby, baby!

Now to sleep this dear Linda is going—murmur low, little rivulet flowing!

The next day, when the heat kept everyone at home, and they were gathered in the library, where a lazy southern breeze would now and then

Stir the heavy curtains, Percival said, turning to the mother: "Mary, now your story will best complete my own; tell it." She answered: "Let it be so, then; my life is but the tributary stream to yours,

"My parents lived in Liverpool; my father, a prosperous merchant, gave to business his time and active thoughts, and let his wife rule everything else with absolute strictness.

My maiden name was Mary Merivale.

There were eight daughters of us, and of these I was the fourth. We lived in generous style, and did not lack the best society

The city could offer. My careful mother, with eight portionless girls to be settled, fearfully healthy all, and clamorous

And she was the oldest. So they came forward in their order: Julia, and Isabel, and Caroline; until

I was dragged forth from maps and lexicons, slate-pencils and arithmetics, and put as Candidate Number Four upon the list.

In giving opportunity for thought,

Behold the world's ideal of a wife! It is something like this:

She marries young, perhaps in meek submission to parental will, or in hope of support.

Her love for him, if love there ever was, is turned to hatred or indifference.

But is it moral? No; when a man or woman can look up, with the heart of prayer, and say, Forbid it, Heaven, forbid it, self-respect, forbid it, merciful regard for others, that this one should be parent to my child—at that moment the intimate relations of marriage should end, and a release be found!

It may exist with hate; too often it leads its victim blindfold into hateful bonds, under the wild delusion that love leads. Love's bonds are adamant, and love a slave; and yet love's service must be perfect freedom. It craves candor, for love is innocent—but no enforced fidelity, no ties

You have a sister—Mrs. Hammersley—

Julia, I think you said—an elder sister, resident here and in society,

You at her house shall be a visitor,

IV.

PARADISE FOUND.

"You might have made it longer," murmured Linda, who had listened with moist eyes, and to whom the time had seemed inexplicably brief. Then with an arm around each parent's neck, and with a kiss on each parent's cheek, she said: "My lot is as the good God gave it; and I would not have it other than it is.

Could permission from any human lips

Have made me any more a child of God?

Have made me any more your child, my parents?

My right to be and breathe the common air? Could any form of words approving it

Have made us three more intimately near? Have made us three more exquisitely dear? Ah! if it could, our love is not the love I hold it now to be—immortal love!"

With speechless joy and a new pride they gazed into her fair and youthful face, bright with a heavenly glow and tenderness. Then smoothing back her hair, the father said: "An anxious thought comes to us now and then—comes like a cloud: the thought that we have not yet saved anything from our income for Linda. My few small ventures in commerce, made in hopes of profit,

turned out so badly that I saw

"Not with nothing, my father!" Linda cried. "As far back as memory goes, you taught me this: to help myself; to seek in my own mind companionship forever new and glad,

through studies, meditations, and resources which nature, books, and busy life supply. And then you urged me to excel in something—'Better to do one thing thoroughly,' you said, 'than fifty only tolerably well'—

something from which, with loving diligence, I might, if life's circumstances required it, earn a living. And then how carefully you taught me how to deal with slippery men! You taught me my rights, the laws, the very forms by which to guard against neglect or fraud in any business—until I am half a lawyer. You taught me, too, how to protect myself if force should attack me; how to hold a pistol, carry it, fire it—Heaven save me from the need! And when I was a very little girl,

you used to take me around to see the houses as they were built—the clearing of the land, the digging of the cellar, the foundations. You told me that the sand to make the mortar ought to be fresh, and not the sea-shore sand, or else the salt would keep up a certain moisture. And then we would watch the framework and the roofing, and you would explain the purpose and the name of every beam, and make me understand

the qualities of wood, the seasoning of timber, and how the masons, the carpenters, the plasterers, the plumbers, and the slaters should do their work—and when they did it carelessly, and when the woodwork was too near the flue, the flue too narrow, or the draft defective. So that, as you yourself have often said, I am better qualified than half the builders to plan and build a house, and to guard myself from being cheated in the process.

Do not fear for me, my parents. Spend your income without a thought of saving. And besides, even if you had not trained me as well as you have, am I not better—I—than many sparrows? There is a heavenly Father over all!"

"Sweet arguer!" said Percival. "May He and his swift angels love and help our Linda! Your mother and I have tried lately to study how and where we might reduce certain expenses that have been—"

But here the dinner bell broke in, and lighter thoughts—thoughts that only skim the surface of the mind and do not stir its depths—were exchanged as more timely now; for the Percivals

You've spilt the salt, papa; please throw a little over your shoulder. There! That saves a quarrel. So you leave it to me to decide

Dinner was ended, and the gas was lit. The last edition of The Day had been put into his hand to read, when suddenly,

"Kenrick, I see, is dead—Kenrick, our friend. 'Died in Chicago on the seventh instant. Leaves an estate valued at seven millions.'" "Indeed! Our faithful Kenrick—is he dead? Does he leave a wife?" "Probably not, my dear. Three months ago he was a single man.

I had a letter from him, begging me,

if I lacked funds at any time, to draw

I.

O, give me one breath from that land—the land to which all of us go!

Even now, O my soul, are you fanned by the breezes that blow over it.

II.

By the breezes that blow over it!

Though far from the knowledge of sense, you know the shore of that land. There soon you will go with me hence.

III.

There soon you will go with me hence—but where, O my soul, where will it be? In that region, that immense region, shall we see the loved and the lost?

IV.

The loved and the lost shall we see! For Love will make near all that it loves. Type and outcome of Love shall it be—our home in that infinite sphere!

A day's excursion to a favorite spot—a choice nook among the choicest of Long Island, which he playfully called Paradise Found—had often been planned. One day Percival said: "Let us go today!" "No, not today!" cried Linda, with a shudder. "And why not? It is the very day of all the year!

There's an elastic coolness in the air, thanks to the thunder-shower we had last night. A day for out-of-doors! Your reasons, Linda? Tears in your eyes! No, I'll not ask for reasons. We will not go." "Yes, father, let us go. I could not say where my abrupt No came from. It was a sudden whim, and what it meant you know as well as I. Shall we get ready?" "Yes, such a perfect day is rare. It seems to bring heaven nearer to my understanding. Life, life itself is joy enough! To be, to breathe this air, to see that arch of blue, is happiness." "But it is the soul that makes it so. What would it be, my father, without love?" "Yes, without love—love human and divine—there can be no atmosphere of real joy."

Mother and daughter did not need long to put on their simple, neat clothes. A postman handed Percival a letter.

They were just leaving the house to take the carriage that would bring them to the station. They had to travel about twenty miles by rail to reach this paradise of Percival's.

When they were seated on the train and it was moving, Percival took the letter from his pocket. As he read it, a strange expression came over his face. "Now what is it, father?" Linda asked. He sighed in a way that her sharp ear recognized as hiding a pleasant thought. "Poor little Linda!" he said. "And why poor?" she asked. "Because she will not be so rich again in unfulfilled wishes. That grand piano you saw at Chickering's—what was the price?" "Twelve hundred dollars only," she said. "It is yours!" he said. "That painting you admired so much—the one by Church—what did they ask for it?" "Two thousand dollars," she said. "That is cheap. We will take it. Whose carriage was it that caught your eye?" The mother's heart flew to the loved ones who were gone. She sighed, but not to have them back again; that would be too selfish and irreverent a wish. Then, once the first surprise had settled, they calmly discussed their future, though not without natural joy

Ah! A shivering crash! A whirl of splintered wood and twisted iron! Then shrieks and groans of pain....

A broken rail had caused it all. Now there were the killed and wounded to deal with! The sight was ghastly! And happy were those whom Death had taken quickly! Then he died; and, chasing from his face that glare of anguish, came a smile so beatific it was as if angels

Her father's cry was all unheard by Linda, or by her mortal senses all unheard.

V.

LINDA.

The news of the great railroad accident and of the sudden death of Percival,

Coming so soon upon intelligence

Of his rare fortune in the legacy

From Kenrick, occupied the public mind for a full day at least, and then was whelmed in other marvels rushing thick upon it. The mother and the daughter, who still bore the name of Percival, came back from Paris at once, on getting the unlooked-for news. When Linda, after three weeks had elapsed, re-entered, with a swelling heart, the house to her so full of sacred memories,

She was accosted by an officer

Who told her he had put his seal on all the papers, plate, and jewelry belonging to the late Albert Percival,—and asked if in her keeping were a watch and ring, also some money, found upon his person: if so, would she please give them up, and he, who had authority to take them, would

Sign a receipt for all such property,

And then the rightful heir could easily dispose of it, as might seem best to her.

"The rightful heir?" gasped Linda, taking in not readily the meaning of the words,—"Do you not know that I'm the rightful heir and only child of Albert Percival?"

"Pardon me," said the officer, "the child, recognized by the law, is not yourself, but Harriet Percival, the only heir,—for so the court adjudges,—and to her all property, both personal and real,

Must be made over. She, no doubt, will deal kindly in your peculiar case, and make a suitable provision—"

"Hold!" cried Linda, her nostrils' action showing generous blood as clearly as some matchless courser shows it after a mighty race,—"Your business,

But not your comments! And yet, pardon me—I'm being hasty—you meant well. But you would have me hand over the watch and pocketbook found on my father's person and delivered to me, his daughter. I'll only do that when more authority than you have shown compels me, and my lawyer tells me to yield." "Here is my warrant," said the officer, "and my instructions are explicit." Then, the spirit of the gentleman disdaining the action he was sent for, he added: "But the letter of the law shall not make me commit an incivility, perhaps a wrong.

And so, relying on your honesty, I leave you, assured that you'll be ready to answer all that the law can ask. And now, good day!"

Left to her own decisions, Linda sought the best advice at once. And such had been her upbringing that she was not ignorant of who among counselors were most trusted in particular areas. Kindly and patiently

her case was taken up and thoroughly

examined and tested. No hope! No flaw! No case! So craftily had every step been taken, with such precaution and such legal care—so diligently had the net been woven, enclosing Percival and all that was his—

that nothing could be done except delay the payment of the Kenrick legacy

for some six months—when it was all transferred to the supposed child, already rich

through the law's distribution of the sums which Percival had been compelled to pay.

After the legal test, with brave composure Linda surveyed her situation. Enough was left, from the sale of jewels that had been her mother's, for a few months' support with frugal care. Claim to these jewels and the money found on her mother's person had been made too eagerly by the contesting party,

who said that Percival, in dying last, was heir to the effects. But since the claim could only be upheld by proving marriage, the claimants sorrowfully gave it up.

One day as Linda stood with folded hands before her easel, on which lay a painting of autumn flowers grouped with the rarest skill—the blue-fringed gentian, the red cardinal flower, with fern and feathery goldenrod intertwined—a knock startled her, and the opened door revealed a footman dressed in livery,

who, hat in hand, asked if a lady might come up to see the pictures. "Certainly," was the reply. And, panting up the stairs, a lady came whose showy dress

and air of self-assured, aggressive wealth marked her as one well pleased to intimidate those beneath her.

As when by some instinctive sense the dove knows that the hawk, though out of sight and still, is hovering near, even so did Linda feel an enemy draw near. She felt that this woman—who, despite the marks a self-indulgent life leaves on the face, still showed traces of beauty—was she who first had cast the bitterness into that cup of youth which Linda's father was made to taste for so long.

And yet—how strangely, in this mixed web of life, the strands of good cross and weave into the evil!—to that wrong might he have traced a joy surpassing hope: the saving angel who, in Linda's mother, had so enriched his being. He might have traced—mysterious thought!—Linda herself, his child, the crown of every rapture, every hope.

The lady, known as Madame Percival,

Seated herself and turned a piercing look on Linda, who did not flinch, but stood erect, regarding her with a calm and serious look. The lady was the first to lower her eyes. She then, with some embarrassment, remarked: "So! You're an artist! Will you let me see some of your newest paintings?" Linda placed three of her choicest pieces on the easel, and madame raised her eyeglass, looked a moment, said, "Very pretty," and then, breaking through further constraint, began: "You may not know me. My name is Percival. You, I suppose,

Bear the same name by courtesy. That is well. The law at last has taught you, possibly, our relative positions. Of the past

We will say nothing. No hard thought is left against you in my heart. I trust I know the meaning of forgiveness, and what is due to Christian charity. In me, although

The church has but a frail, unworthy child, yet I would help my enemy; remove her

From doubtful paths, and see her fitly placed with her own kindred for proper protection. Hear my proposal now, on your behalf:

If you will go to England, where your aunts and relatives reside—and first will sign a paper promising that you will not return,

And that you never will resume your suit—I will advance your passage-money, and give you five thousand dollars. Will you do it?"

The indignant No, surging in Linda's heart, paused as if language were too weak for it, when, in that pause, the opening of the door disclosed a lady younger than the first, yet not unlike in features, though not blonde, and of a figure small and delicate.

I see a likeness of my father. So!

You, then, are Linda Percival! The child for whom he could abandon me, his first! Come, let me look at you!"—"Nay, Harriet, this should not be. Come with me to the carriage. Come! I command you."—"Pooh! And pray, who cares for your commands? I move not till I please. We are half-sisters, Linda, but I hate you."

"Excuse me," Linda answered quietly,

"But I see no resemblance to my father in you. Your features, form, complexion, all are quite unlike."—"Silence! We've had enough." "What did she say?" cried Harriet. "Do not heed a word of hers. It is unlike any that I've ever seen."

And with the word she took it from the nail and would have put it in her pocket, had not Linda, with sudden grasp, recovered it.

Darker her dark face grew, when Harriet saw herself baffled. Taking out her purse she drew from it a thousand-dollar bill, and said, "Will this procure it?"—"Harriet! You're mad to offer such a sum as that." "Old woman, if you anger me, you'll rue it! I ask you, Linda Percival, if you

"Will you take two thousand dollars for that portrait?" And Linda answered: "I will not take your money. You may have the portrait without a price; I am not without a copy." "Well, I will take it. "There! No more idle questions! Two o'clock? That camel's hair at Stewart's will be sold unless we go this minute. Such a bargain! Come, my dear, come!" And so, cajoling and coaxing, she drew away her daughter, and the door closed quickly on the two. VI.

BY THE SEASIDE.

Borne swiftly to the North Cape of the Bay, still on the wings of steam the travellers went; and tenderly the purple sunset smiled

Upon their journey's end: a little cottage with oaks and pines behind it, and before, high ocean crags, and under them the ocean, uninterrupted as far as sight could reach! Foliage and waves! A combination rare

Of lofty sylvan table-land, and then—no barren strip to mar the interval—

The watery waste, the ever-changing main! Old Ocean, with a diadem of verdure.

Crowning the summit where his reach was stayed! The shore was a line of steep rocks, piled on each other, leaving deep chasms. Into their rifts the foamy waters rushed with a gurgling roar, then flowed back in little streams until the surge drove them furiously in again, shaking the split granite with a thunderous bass. Yet up to the base of the tumbled cliffs the wild grass crept. The sweet-leafed bayberry scented the briny air. The fern, the sumach, the prostrate juniper, the flowering thorn, the blueberry, the clinging blackberry, tangled the fragrant sod. And in their midst the red rose bloomed, wet with the drifted spray. Cut off from the main shore and isolated by the invading, surrounding waves, a rock which time had made an island spread with a small patch of brine-defying herbage. It is known as Norman's Woe, for on this rock, two hundred years ago, Captain Norman, in his good ship from England, was driven and wrecked in a wild storm, and every life was lost.

Stand on the cliff nearby. Southeasterly there are only waves on waves to the horizon. But easterly, less than two miles across, and forming with the coastline where you look the harbor's entrance, stretches Eastern Point. A lighthouse stands at its end. It is a mile of land thrust out like an arm to keep the ocean off, so narrow that beyond its width, due east, you see the Atlantic glittering, hardly made less conspicuous by the intervention. The cottage fare, the refreshing breeze, the grove, the piny odors, and the flowers, rambles at morning and twilight time, sea-bathing, joyous and exhilarating—

Siestas on the rocks, with inhalations of the pure breathings of the ocean tide—soon worked a visible change in both the maidens. Each day their walks grew longer, till at last a ten-mile tramp was no infrequent one.

"And where to today?" asked Rachel one fair morning. "To Eastern Point," said Linda, "with our baskets! For berries, there's no place like Eastern Point. Blackberries, whortleberries, pigeon-pears—all we shall find in abundance!"

And so by what was once the old stage-road close to the shore, and through the woods—though long abandoned except by scenery-hunters, and overgrown with grass and vines and bushes; then leaving on their right the wooded hill named from the rattlesnakes, now obsolete; then by the Cove, and by the bend of shore over Stage-rocks, by little Half-moon beach, across the Cut, the Creek, by the Hotel, and through the village, even to Eastern Point—the maidens went, and had a happy day. And when the setting sun blazed clear and mild, and every little cloud was steeped in crimson, they strolled along the beach to a small wharf on the harbor side, and looked across the stretch of wave to Norman's Woe. And Linda wistfully said: "Heigho! I own I'm tired. And you too, Rachel, you look travel-worn, and hardly good for four miles more of road. Could we but make this short cut over water! What would I give now for a boat to take us to Webber's Cove! O, if some timely oarsman would only come and say, 'Fair demoiselles, my skiff lies yonder, rocking on the tide, and eager to convey you to your home!' Then would I—Rachel!"

"What, Miss Percival?" "Look at those men coming down from the ridge!" "Well, I can see an old man and a young one." "And is that all you have to say about them?" "How should I know anything about them? Ah! I see! Those are the two we met three weeks ago—the day we left New York—met on the train." "Yes, Rachel, and their name is Lothian. They are father and son. Who would have thought they would find their way to Eastern Point?" "Why not, as well as we, Miss Percival? Look! They're going to the wharf, and there beside it, if I'm not much mistaken, lies a boat. The oarsman we wished for! Oh, this is luck! They're going to the boat—he'll row us over. I'll run and ask him. You watch my basket." "Rachel! Stop, Rachel! Shame on you, you forward girl! Don't think of it. Come back! Back, back, I say!"

But Rachel did not hear, or would not listen. She ran straight to the boat, and as the men drew near and stopped—to Linda's great dismay—she went up and spoke to them, and pointed to Norman's Woe. Then she turned back to her companion, and with a gesture that spoke eloquently of thanks for some reply the younger man had made, she seemed to lead the way, and he to follow, along the footpath to the granite bench where Linda sat, embarrassed and wondering. And when they stood before her, Rachel said, "Miss Percival, here's Mr. Lothian."

"He has a boat nearby, and will be glad to give us seats and row us both across." Charles Lothian bowed, and Linda, blushing, said, "Against my orders this little lady approached you, sir, but I will not pretend to regret her success, if you are content." "More than content, I'm very glad," said Charles. "My boat is easily large enough for four, and we are bound, it seems, all the same way. My father and I have taken rooms"

"at Mistress Moore's, not far from where you live. So consider your obligation very slight." "An obligation not the first!" said Linda. "So much the better!" said Charles Lothian. "Come, take my arm, and let me hold your basket. What fine blackberries! I'll taste one." "Why not two? As many as you like?" "Thank you. You've been adventurous, it seems." "Yes, Fortune favors the adventurous."

"See the old proverb proven true today!"

"Praise a good day when it's over. Here's my father. Father, Miss Percival!" The older man bowed and said, "I used to know—" And then, as if stopped by a memory that might be unwelcome, he fell silent, and they all went to the boat. "Please let me take an oar," said Linda. "Can you row?" asked Charles. "A little! My father taught me." Then old Lothian looked at her with a searching glance.

The ocean waves melted into one,

and that stretched level as a marble floor. All winds were hushed, and only sunset tints from purple cloudlets, edged with fiery gold, and a bright crimson fleece the sun had left behind, fell on the liquid plain, dyeing it red. The very pulse of the ocean was now mute.

From the far-off depths, no throb, no swell! Motionless on the coastwise ships, the sails hung limp and white, their very shadows white. The lighthouse windows drank in the kindling red, and flashed and gleamed as if the lamps were lit.

"A heavenly evening!" sighed Linda, full of praise, as the two looked out toward the ocean with their oars ready. Then, keeping time together, they rowed out from the shore. Smiling, but with sad eyes, the father said: "Ah! A little bird flew

the sky it was blue,

But when the moon shone as a crescent in the west, and the faint outline of the darkened part curved thread-like and visible from horn to horn—and Jupiter, supreme among the heavenly bodies,

They felt that God exists, though inconceivable. And while they mutely worshipped, suddenly a change came over Linda's face, and her glazed mortal eyes stopped working. For there, before her in the boat, stood two unbidden but not unwelcome passengers,

Her father and her mother....

The morning sea-fog rose up to the sun like incense and vanished in his beam. The sky's blue promise brightened through the veil. Yet Linda was not altogether happy.

Why was it that Charles Lothian had called once, and once only, after their adventure? Called just to ask her how she found herself, and whether she overtasked herself in rowing? Then the conversation slid off to women's rights, for Lothian held a newspaper which told of some convention, the report of which might raise a smile. One of the lady speakers, it seems, would give her sex the privilege of taking the initiative in wooing,

"Indeed, why not?" cried Linda. "Indeed, you almost take my breath away with your 'Why not, Miss Percival! Why not?'" "Yes, I repeat—if so inclined, why not? For why should woman any more than man play the deceiver, with so much at stake? I know the ready taunt that will rise here: 'Already our girls are none too backward in husband-seeking.' Seeking in what way? Seeking by scheming and management—not by frank, honest means! What food for mockery it would give to shallow men to see a woman court the relationship, intertwined with all the purest happiness she may crave—the ties of wife and mother! Oh, what pointing, sneering, and joking! And yet why should thoughtful, pure, and wisely provident care, that Nature's sacred prompting shall not fail, be one thing for a man, and quite another for her, the woman? Why this flimsy mask? This playing of a part, put on to suit not the heart's need, but custom-bound Fashion? Feigning we must be sought, and never seek? Give her freedom—freedom to seek—and she will not harbor it! Because if woman, equally with man,

UNDER THE PINES.

O pine-trees! bid the busy breeze be still that through your tops roars like the constant surf. Such was the sound I heard in happy days under the pines.

In happy days, when those I loved were near; in happy days, when love was daily food; and joyful childhood, finding it, found joy under the pines.

Again I hear the west wind in your tops; again I smell the scent you give off;

but sound and scent now only bring tears under the pines.

O pine-trees! shall a different joy be mine, one day when I shall seek your fragrant shade? Whisper it faintly, breezes, to my heart under the pines.

"I do not know, nor care," said Linda, switching her horse's ear to start a quicker trot. Another mile of silence! "Look!" cried he; "the lighthouse light salutes us!" "Yes, I see." "Why do you go so fast?" "I'll slacken speed if you desire it. There!" They breathed their horses. Then Lothian said: "Indeed, I hope that we

Shall meet again." "Why not? The world is wide, but I have known a letter in a bottle, flung over in mid-ocean, to be found

and reach its owner. Doubtless, we may meet." "I'm glad to find you confident of that." Silence again! And so they rode along

till they saw Rachel coming from the house to greet them. Charles helped Linda to dismount, held out his hand, and said, "Goodbye, Miss Linda." "Goodbye!" she cheerily answered; "bid your father goodbye for me. And so you go indeed

tomorrow?" "Yes, we may not meet again." "Well, pleasant journey!" "Thank you. Goodbye, Rachel." He rode away, leading her panting horse; and, when the trees concealed him, Linda rushed upstairs, and locked the door, and wept awhile.

As, early the next morning, she looked forth on the blue ocean from the open window, "Now, then, for work!" she cried, and drew her palm across her brow, as if to thrust away

thoughts that too perseveringly came back. She heard a step. It is he! "I hardly hoped, Miss Percival, to find you up so early. Goodbye, once more!" "Goodbye! Don't miss the train." At this a shadow fell on Lothian's face, as with uplifted hat and thwarted smile, he turned away. Then off with hasty stride he walked and struck the bushes listlessly.

"What did I mean by speaking so?" said Linda, with hand outstretched, as if to draw him back. "Poor fellow! He looked sad; but why—but why is he so undemonstrative? And why

could he not ask again for my address, I'd like to know?" Poor Linda! She could preach, but, like her elders, could not always practise.

VII.

FROM LINDA'S DIARY.

I.

Home again! Home? What satire in the word! If home is where the heart is, where is my home? Well, here is my easel; here my old piano; here the memorials of my early days!

Here let me try at least to be content. This din of rolling wheels beneath my window, let it renew for me the ocean's roar!

II.

It is the heart that makes music musical!

My neighbor has a mockingbird. Its song has been as little heeded as the noise of rattling wheels incessant; but today one of its strains brought all Elysium back into my heart. What was it? What was the tie linking it with some inexpressible joy? At length I solve the mystery! Those notes, pensively slow and sadly exquisite,

were what the wood-thrush piped at early dawn after that evening passage in the boat, when stars came out that never more shall set. Oh! sweet and clear the measured cadence fell upon my ear in slumber—and I woke!

I woke, and listened while the first faint flush of day was in the east; while yet the grove showed only purple gloom, and on the beach the tidal waves with intermittent rush broke lazily and lent their mingling chime. And O the unreckoned riches of the soul! The possible beatitudes, of which

a glimpse is given, a transitory glimpse, so rarely in a lifetime! Then it was,

hearing that strain, as if all joy the Past had in its keeping—all the Future held—all love, all adoration, and all beauty—made for a moment the soul's atmosphere, and lifted it to bliss unspeakable.

O splendor fugitive! O transport rare! Transfiguring and glorifying life!

III.

This strange, inexplicable human heart! My lawyer sends me more good news; he writes: "The picture's sale will reach ten thousand copies, and for the first year only! We shall have a big bill to send in; and do not fear but the 'old man' will pay it, every dime. To escape the heavy damages the law

allows for such infringement, he'll be glad to compromise for the amount I fix;

And what I shall force him to surrender will simply be fair copyright on all

Your published works; and this will give you about fifteen thousand dollars, not to mention a fixed interest in future sales."

So writes my lawyer. Now one would suppose that news like this would make me light of heart, spur my ambition; and, as the taste of blood fires a pet tiger, even so the touch of gold would rouse the sacred appetite for gain. But with attainment comes apathy;

And I was somewhat happier, I think,

When life was all a struggle, and the prayer, "Give me my daily bread," had anxious meaning.

IV.

Is it then true that woman's proper sphere is in the affections? That she is out of place when these are blocked, and science, art, or trade has won the dedication of her thought? No! The affections are for all; and he, or she, has most of life who has them most. O, they are not an attribute of sex!

Heart loneliness is loneliness indeed, but not for woman any more than man,

If she were so trained, her active faculties could have a worthy aim.

What worthier aim than the pursuit, the discipline of beauty? He who finds beauty helps to interpret God: for not an irreligious heart can dwell in him who sees and knows the beautiful. I will not believe that one whom Art has chosen for a high priest can be irreverent,

Sordid, unloving; his veil-piercing eye sees not the beauty in life until it sees God and the life beyond; not in a dream of pantheistic revery where all

In all is lost, diluted, and absorbed, and consciousness and personality

Vanish like smoke forever; but all real, distinct, and individual, though all

Eternally dependent on the One!

Who gave the Eye to see, shall He not see? Who gave the Heart to feel, shall He not love? Of infinite knowledge we know a letter, a syllable or two, and thirst for more: is there not One, Teacher at once and Cause, who comprehends all beauty and all science, holding infinity, so that, step by step,

We may advance, and find, in what seems good to Him, our gladness and our being's crown? If this were not so, then what a toy the world would be! And what a mockery these suns and systems! And how like pumping at an empty cistern it would be to live and study and aspire!

Come, then, O Art! and warm me with your smile! Flash on my inward sight your radiant shapes! August interpreter of thoughts divine, whether revealed in sound, or word, or form! Pledge and credential of immortal life! Grand arbiter of truth! Consoler! Come! Come, help even me to seek you and to find you!

V.

Winter is here again; it sees me still at work upon my picture. This presents two vases, filled with flowers, upon a slab. "Which will you choose?" I call it; it is in oil. Three hours a day are all I give to it, so fine the work, so trying to the eyes. Thus I have ample time for teaching Rachel: a good child and affectionate! I have found her aptitude; she has a taste in bonnets, with an inventive skill in ornament.

And so I have her regularly taught

By an accomplished milliner; and Rachel already promises to surpass her teacher.

Had I a fortune, still I would have her feel that she must conquer something worthily; something to occupy her active powers, and yield a fair support, should need require.

VI.

Whom should I meet today but Meredith! My washerwoman, Ellen Blount, is ill,

So ill I fear she never will be well.

It is the old story, every day renewed: a little humble, tender-hearted woman, tied to a husband whom to call a brute would be to insult the quadrupeds!

A man who must have his pipe, his whiskey, and his good dinner, no matter what happens to his wife and children. He could take the small amount she earned from her hard work, and spend it on himself and his drinking companions. When he was out of work, as he often was, then she had to work twice as hard—with irritable words from him as her only reward for all of it. She bore him child after child; but, forced too soon after giving birth to return to the old wash-tub, all her suffering affected the children, and they died, most of them in infancy—

Until only one was left—a little boy, puny and pale, gentle and never complaining, with all his mother's pleading staring from his eyes in hollow, anxious, pitiful appeal.

In this one remaining child, all her love and hope and all that made her life bearable

were at last centered. She had saved a dollar to buy him a pair of overshoes;

but, as she went to get them, Blount waylaid her, learned that she had the money, and forced it from her. Poor Teddy had to go without his shoes. It was when the January thaw had made

the streets reek with mud and melting snow. Poor Teddy wet his feet, caught a cold, and died. "Come soon, mamma," were his last weak words. Blount was a cunning scoundrel; he knew well how far to go, and where and when to stop. Smooth-tongued and plausible, he kept, in his small circle, a certain appearance of respectability; and he could sing in tune for mother church, though he himself would give her few pennies. "Cast off the wretch," was my advice to Ellen. She did not love him; she might as well have tried to love a burden that chafed and wearied her. But custom, social fear, and, above all, those sacramental chains the church

had bound her in, and would keep to the end, prevented the poor, frightened, helpless little woman from freeing herself, by one decisive act, from the foul burden that sucked her life away. So a false sense of duty kept her tied, feeding all that was pitiless in him.

And now she is dying. I had gone today to take some little treats, cream and fruit, and there, administering comfort,

was Meredith.

Hearing his tones of faith,

seeing his saintly look of sympathy,

I felt, since there was no disagreement between us in spirit, that doctrines mattered little. And so I knelt and listened to his prayer. At last he noticed me and recognized me. "Miss Percival!" he cried; "can this be you? But when and why did you return from England?" "I have never been in England, never been out of my native country," I replied.

"But that is inexplicable," he said;

"for I have seen letters, written as if from you, signed with your name, acknowledging receipt of certain sums of money, dated from London." "I have had no money but what I have earned," was my reply; "and who would send me money?" He said: "I have a carriage at the door; I would like to learn more about this; you will not object to taking a seat with me? Thank you; that is right."

Leaving the patient in good hands, we went, and through the noisy streets drove to the Park. Then he drew from me all I had ever known about my parents, and all my history

since I had parted from him; he carefully noted down my address, and gave me his. Then, driving back with me to my lodgings, he left me with a _Benedicite_!

He is rich: has he been sending money, then? What does it all mean? I can find no clue.

VII.

As gently as thistle-down is carried away from the dry stem, Ellen departed yesterday. I heard her dying words; they were:

"I'm coming, Teddy! Bless you, dear Miss Linda!" No priest was by her side, so sudden was her passing. When Blount came in, there was no tenderness in his sleek, gluttonous look, although he tried, behind his handkerchief, to play the mourner. What will he do without a drudge to trample on? Counting himself a privileged lord and master, he'll soon condescend to a new victim, and make some patient waiter a sad loser.

VIII.

"Some patient waiter!" I know someone like that. There was a time when I resolved that if ever I could secure a modest income,

I would marry; and the income is now secure—but where is my resolve? Shall I conclude it's all fate?

Leave it to chance, and take no active step myself to seek what I so hope to find? Accepting it as heaven's fixed decree that man should change his single state at will, but woman be the sport of circumstance, a purposeless and passive accident,

as inert as oysters waiting for a tide,

but not like oysters, sure of what they wait for? "Ah! woman's strength is in passivity," Fastidio says, shaking his wise, wise head, and withering me with a disdainful stare. No! Woman's strength is in developing, in virtuous ways, all that is best in her. No superstitious waiting then for me! No fancy that in coy, alluring arts,

rather than in action, modest and sincere, woman most worthily performs her part. Here I am twenty-five, and all alone

in the wide world; yet having won the right, by my own effort, to shape my lot,

and create ties to cheer this barren waste. How bleak and empty my future, if I stand waiting beside the stream until some Prince—son of Queen Moonbeam by King Will-o'-the-wisp—appears, and jumping from his gilded boat, lays heart and fortune at my idle feet! You languid day-dreams, vanish! Let me act!

But ah! Fastidio says, "A woman's wooing must always be offensive to a man

of any dignity." The dignity

that modest truth can shock is far too frail and sensitive to mate with love of mine, whose earnestness might crush the feeble hand linked in its own. So goodbye, dignity! I shall survive the chill of your rejection. Defiance, not of Nature's law, but Custom's, is what disturbs Fastidio. Does he think that a man's wooing never is offensive to woman's dignity? In either sex

the disaffection is not prompted by

the wooing but the wooer; love can never be an unwelcome tribute to the lover;

though premature freedom, or unwarranted forwardness, may rightly fail to win.

And so I'll run my risk; for I confess—(keep the unuttered secret, sacred page!)—that there is one whom I could love—could die for—would he but—Tears? Well, tears may come from strength as well as weakness. I'll not begrudge him these; I'll not despair while I can shed a tear.

IX.

I've found him—seen him! The Directory gave me his address. He keeps a school, one for young ladies only; and at once my coward heart hit on a good excuse

for calling on him: Would he take a pupil? Rachel, my protégée? Of course he would. A flush of tender, joyful wonder,

I thought, lit up his face at seeing me; then, as it faded, I was grieved to mark how pale and thin and worn with care he looked. I took my leave, promising to return

within a week; and on the outer steps

I met his father. "Turn and walk with me a block or two," I said, and he did. "What's wrong with him?" I asked. "Only hard work. He puts too much conscience into it. He needs help, but he hates debt, so he keeps on doing the work of two or three men. What should I do, Miss Percival, to stop it?" "I don't know—only something must be done, and right away," I said, in a tone that made the old man turn to look at me.

I hailed a bus, and there we parted. What if I write Charles Lothian a letter? No, I won't hide behind a sheet of paper. I'll say what I have to say face to face. This very evening I must call again.

Let a firm will hold up my fainting heart!

X.

And so at eight o'clock the carriage came, and getting in, I drove to Lothian's.

At last I was alone with him once more! He had been sitting at a table heaped

with manuscripts, and these he was correcting. "I'm here to interrupt all this," I said. "You've kept your brain under strain for too long. Why be so careless of your health, your life?" "But what are they to you, Miss Percival?" "That's what I've come to tell you," I said, encouraged by the opening he gave me. He flushed a little—only just a little—replying, "That I'm curious to learn." And then, like someone who in the dark at first moves cautiously but soon runs boldly on, I said: "Reckless gambler that I am, I've come to stake on the throw of a die

much of my present and future peace. Perhaps to shock, repel, and anger you, since I won't offend without warning. I know you guess my purpose, and you shrink from hearing me declare it. But I will,

and in plain, unadorned English.

I'm here to offer you my hand. The heart that should go with it has gone ahead of it and lives with you, so you can claim your own, or gently send it away, to trouble you

never again. If it's unwomanly

to declare this, then I'm unlike my sex,

but not false to my own nature—ah, not false. I must be true or die. I cannot play

a pretender's part, disguising hopes that cling closest to my brooding heart. But say the word, 'I cannot love you,' and the bird that leaves the cage where it pined will sooner try to enter it again than I will return

to speak any complaint of mine within your hearing."

With throbbing heart and burning face I stopped. Twice, three times he tried to stop me, but my words came far too quickly and earnestly for that. And then he listened in resignation. I had seen—or dreamed I had—at first a sacred joy at my confession sparkle in his eyes,

and then an utter sadness follow it,

which chilled me, and I knew I had failed.

"O divine Pity! What will you not brave?" he answered, and there were tears in his eyes. "You bring her here, even to humble herself to rescue me! Too costly a sacrifice!

The Graces and the Loves do not live here; Drudgery is master of this house.

Dear lady, seek the answering bloom elsewhere." A hope flashed up. "Do you suppose," I said, "that any impulse less supreme than love—love bold enough to venture, but pure—could bring me here? That Pity could do this?"

"I believe all," he answered, "all you say. But do not ask me to whisper more than this: the circumstances that surround me,

and which no one knows—not even my father knows—shut me out utterly from any hope

of marriage or of love. A wretch in prison might better dream of marrying than I. But O sweet lady, rashly generous—

around whom, a protecting atmosphere,

Floats Purity, and sends her messengers with flaming swords to guard each avenue from unholy thoughts and base approaches—You who have made an act I deemed uncomely seem beautiful and gracious—do not doubt my memory of your worth shall be the same, only expanded, lifted up, and touched

With light as dear as sunset radiance

To summer trees after a thunder-storm."

And there was silence then between us two. Thought of myself was lost in thought for him. What was my wreck of joy, compared with his? Health, youth, and competence were mine, and he was staking all of his to save another. If my winged hopes fell fluttering to the ground, regrets and disappointments were forgotten in the reflection, He, then, is unhappy! "Good by!" at length I said, giving my hand: "Even as I was believed, will I believe. You do not deal in hollow compliment;

And we shall meet again if you're content. The good time will return—and I'll return!" "If you return, the good time will return and stay as long as you remain," said he.

XI.

It is as I supposed: an obstacle

Which his assumption of his father's debts has raised before him unexpectedly!

I did not let a day go by before

I saw the elder Lothian, and he,

Distressed by what I told him of a secret, applied himself to hunting up a key

To the mysterious grief: at last he got it, though not by means that I could justify. In Charles's private escritoire he found a memorandum that explained it all.

Among the obligations overlooked,

In settling up the firm's accounts, was one of fifty thousand dollars, payable

To an estate, the representatives

Of which were six small children and a widow, dependent now on what they could derive of income from this debt; and manfully Charles shoulders it, although it crushes him; and hopes to keep his father ignorant. I can command one quarter of the sum

Already—but the rest? That staggers me. And yet why should I falter? Look at him! Let his example be my high incentive.

I'll be his helpmate, and he shall not know it. Poor Charles! I'll toil for him—to him devote all that I have of energy and skill,

All I acquire. Ambition shall not mount less loftily for having Love to help it. Come forth, my easel! All thy work has been girl's play till now; now will I truly venture. I've a new object now—to rescue him! And he shall never know his rescuer

From lips of mine—no, though I die for it, with the sweet secret undisclosed—my heart glad in the love he never may requite!

VIII.

FROM MEREDITH'S DIARY.

I.

Incalculably selfish and corrupt,

Well may man need a sacrifice divine

To expiate infinity of sin.

Few but a priest can know the fearful depth of human wickedness. At times I shrink faint and amazed at what I have to learn: and then I wonder that the Saviour said His yoke is easy and his burden light. Ah! how these very murmurs at my lot

Show that not yet into my heart has crept that peace of God which passeth understanding!

II.

Among my hearers lately there has been a lady all attention to my words:

Thrice have I seen that she was deeply moved; and to confession yesterday she came.

Let me here call her Harriet. She is

By education Protestant, but wavers,

Feeling the ground beneath her insecure, and would be led unto the rock that is higher than she. A valuable convert;

Not young; in feeble health; taxed for two millions; and she would found, out of her ample means, a home for orphans and neglected children. Heaven give me power to lead the stray one safe into the only fold; securing thus

Aid for the church, salvation for herself!

III.

A summons took me to her house today. Her mother and her stepfather make up the household with Harriet. I refrain

from putting real names on paper here. Let me call the man Denison. He is somewhat younger than his wife, a lady advanced in years, but her heart is still wholly set on the frivolities of fashion.

I see the situation at a glance:

a mercenary marriage on the part

of Denison, whose hungry eyes are fixed upon the daughter's property. The mother is under his evil influence, and expects the daughter to die soon without a will, thus leaving all to them. And Harriet is not quite so dull but she can penetrate Denison's motive and her mother's hope! A sad state for an invalid who feels

that any hour may be her last! Today

Harriet confessed, for she has been alarmed by some bad symptoms lately. As she urged it, I sent word to the bishop, and he came. She was formally confirmed, and taken unto the bosom of the Church. May her poor toiling spirit find repose there!

IV.

Another summons! In the drawing-room,

whom should I meet but Denison? His stare had something vicious in it, but we bowed, and he remarked: "I hear that Harriet, caught in your Catholic net, is turning saint. No foul play, priest! She is not in a condition to make a will, or give away her money. Remember that, and do not waste your words." My color rose, and the brute Adam in me would, uncontrolled, have surely knocked him down. But I cast off temptation and replied: "Sir, I am responsible to God, not man." I left him and passed on to Harriet.

I found her greatly moved. An interview she had been having with her mother caused the agitation. "Take me hence!" she cried. "I will not remain another day or hour

under this roof. I tell you, I am not safe with these two, watching, dogging, maddening me." She rang the bell and said to the servant: "My carriage, and that quickly!" Then to me: "I will show them that I am mistress of my fortune and of myself. Call on me in an hour

at the Fifth Avenue Hotel, for there

henceforth I make my home." And there

I called, as she had ordered, and we met in her own parlor. "What I wish," said she, "is to give all I have, without reserve, for the foundation that I have planned. I will send directions to my lawyer, and the papers shall be prepared at once." "Before you do it, let me learn more of you and yours," said I. "Who was your father?" Then, to my surprise, I learned that he was one whom I had met some years before—in his death-hour had met. "But you have a sister?" I suddenly asked. Surprised, she answered: "A half-sister—yes—I have seen her only once. For many years I lived in Europe. She is in England now, and married happily. On three occasions I have sent her money." "Do you correspond?" "Not often. Here are letters from her, full of thanks for all I have given her." "In your will shall you remember her?" "If you advise it." "Then I advise a liberal bequest.

And now I must attend a sufferer

who waits my help." "Father, I would confess." "Daughter, be quick: I listen." Harriet then gave a sad recital of a trial

and a divorce, and (but reluctantly)

told of a terrible suspicion, born

A servant once made a remark about how unlike her father she was. But she could never get her mother to confirm the distressing story. "Tell her," I said, "that you mean to leave your sister a handsome legacy." She promised to do this. Then, saying I would call the following day, I hurried off to see poor Ellen Blount.

V.

A new surprise! There, by the patient's bed, I came upon Linda, Harriet's half-sister—or at least she is reputed to be, but here is a doubt. I questioned her, and now I am satisfied that treason and forgery have been at work, defeating Harriet's sisterly intention.

Moreover, the harrowing suspicion,

awakened by a servant's gossip overheard, is in all probability the truth!

And if we accept it as true, what can I

advise but that Harriet completely surrender all of her fortune to the real child

and proper heir of Albert Percival?

But ah! The fortune is now devoted to the Church! Here is a divided duty. I must lay

the case before a higher power than mine.

VI.

I have had a long discussion with the bishop. I placed before him all the facts, beginning with my own presence at the death of Linda's parents; her father's letter received that day, communicating news

of Kenrick's large bequest; the father's effort, while dying, to convey in legal form

to his child Linda all this property;

the failure of the effort; his death; and all I knew of subsequent events.

And the good bishop, after careful thought, replied: "Some way the mother must be brought to full confession. Of her guilt there is no doubt!" I told him I had charged the daughter to tell her mother of the legacy

designed for Linda. This, perhaps, might wring a confession from the guilty one. He seemed to think it not unlikely, and remarked: "When that is obtained, there is but a single course for you to urge on Harriet. For, my son, I need not tell a Christian gentleman, not to say priest, that we must decide this peculiar case precisely as we would

if the Church had no interest in it.

Let Harriet at once give up—convey,

not merely bequeath—all she has to Linda. Until she does this, her soul will be in peril. When she does this, she shall be made the ward of Holy Church and cared for to the end." I kissed his hand and left. How his high thoughts poured around my path a flood of divine light! Why did I hesitate, since he could make the path of duty so directly clear?

VII.

Harriet's intimation to her mother

that she should leave a good part of her wealth to her half-sister brought things to a crisis. Today my visit found the two together. Harriet, in an agony of tears,

cried to me as I entered: "It is all true! God! She confesses it—confesses it!

She confesses, too, that she never sent the money, and that the letters were all forgeries! And she thinks, by this confession, to secure my fortune for herself! Ah! Can this woman be, then, my mother?"

At this the woman, crimson with rage at being thus exposed, exclaimed, "Unnatural daughter—" But before her wrath could vent itself, she fell in convulsions with a groan. Medical assistance was obtained at once. Then Denison came in, aghast at what had happened, for he knew his wife's estate was all in lands and houses, and would, if she should die, be Harriet's, since the old lady superstitiously

had still put off the making of a will. All help was vain, and drugs were powerless. Paralysis had struck the heated brain, driving consciousness from mortal hold. It reappeared not in one outward sign, and before midnight life had left the clay.

VIII.

Meek and submissive as a little child

Harriet is like that now; she has no will except what the Church imposes as the divine will. "Your fortune, nearly doubled by this death, must all," I said, "be now transferred to Linda." "Let it be done," she cried, "before I sleep!" And it was done tonight—securely done—since I was acting as Linda's representative.

Tomorrow I must bring her the good news.

IX.

After the storm, the rainbow, child of light! Such is the transition as I turn to Linda! I found her hard at work upon a picture. She heard my news with wonder at Heaven's ways. Shocked at the tragic death, she did not hide her satisfaction at the tardy act

That brought the restitution of her own.

She asked three things. One was that I would at once place a certain person in possession of a large sum, without letting him find out from whom it came. Another was to have this great change in her fortunes kept a secret for as long as she might wish. The third and last was that she might be allowed to wait on Harriet with a sister's loving care. All of which I readily promised should be done, so far as my poor human will could arrange. Then Linda said: "Tell Harriet that her scheme for others' welfare shall not wholly fail; that in your hands I will place a sum sufficient to plant at least the seed of what she planned."

X.

I have taken my last look at Harriet:

She died in Linda's arms, and was comforted with all the Church could give of heavenly hope. Slowly and imperceptibly does Time

work out the dreadful problem of our sins! Not often do we see it solved as here

in plain results that anyone who runs may read. The sinner's punishment is not always

shown in this world. May the Eternal Mercy cleanse us from secret faults, and, while we mark another's foulness, not blind us to our own!

IX.

BESIDE THE LAKE.

The August sun from a clear blue sky shone on Lake Saranac. The south wind stirred mildly the encircling woods, which threw down a purple shadow on the liquid smoothness that glassed the eastern border, while the west lay bared to light.

Wild, virgin nature everywhere! Except that here and there a partial clearing, made by the sportsman's axe for summer tents, dented the massive verdure, and revealed a little slope of bank, dotted with stumps and brown with slender aromatic leaves shed from the pine, the hemlock, and the fir in layers that gave a soft and slippery carpet.

Near one of these small openings where the breeze crept resinous and cool from evergreens behind them, while the sun blazed bright before—where the whiteness of a spacious tent contrasted with the pine-trees' vapory depth of hue, and beside which, on a staff, the nation's flag flung out its crimson with protecting pride—a wife and husband reclined, looking down less on the glorious lake than on the glory that, through a gauzy veil, played round the head of a reposing infant, golden-tressed,

asleep upon a deer-skin at their feet, while a huge dog kept watchful guard beyond: for there lay little Mary Merivale.

Boats on the lake showed that this group set apart were part of a well-chosen company.

Here children ran and frolicked on the beach; there an old man, rowed by two guides, stood up with rod and line and reel, while the reel flew swiftly, announcing that a vigorous trout had just then seized the hook. Came the loud cry—"Look, Charles! Look, Linda! See me land him now! Don't touch him with your scoop, men! I can fetch him,"—in tones not unfamiliar to our ears.

And there, six boats swept by, from which the voices of merry children and their older friends—mothers and fathers, teachers, faded aunts, dyspeptic uncles, wonderfully cured

all by this tonic, Adirondack air—

came musical and loud: a strange collection, selected by Rachel (now the important queen of all this sanitary festivity)

from her acquaintance in the public schools; where her quick sympathies had carried her straight to the overworked, the poor, the ailing, among the families of her associates,

when Linda planned this happy enterprise of a grand camping-out for one whole month. The blind aunt and the grandmother, of course, high and important persons, Rachel's helpers, graced the occasion; for the ancient dame had lived in such a region in her youth, and in all woodland craft was proudly wise: declaring that this taste of life would add some ten years to her eighty-five, at least.

On went the boats, all large and safely manned, in competition not too venturesome.

Then, from a rocky outlook on the hill, there came a gush of music from a band, employed to cheer with timely melody

this strange encampment in the wilderness. Listen! Every voice is hushed as down the lake the breathing clarions accordantly send

the tune of "Love Not" to each eager ear! The very infant, in its slumber, smiled as if a dream of some old paradise

had been awakened by the rapture.

"Look at the child!" cried Linda; "mark that smile! All heaven reflected in a dew-drop! See!" "And all the world grasped in that little fist—at least as we esteem the world!" cried Charles. "And yet," said Linda, "it is a glorious world: see how those families enjoy themselves!" "And who created all this happiness?"

The husband said—"who, after God, but Linda? Who spends her money, not in raising piles of cold and costly marble for her pride—not in great banquets for the rich and gay who need them not, and laugh at those who give—where, at one feast, enough is spent to make all these poor people radiant for a month—but in exhilarations coming from

shared joy and health and life—the happiness that is found in making others happy."

"All selfishness!" cried Linda; "selfishness! I seek my happiness, and others theirs; only my tastes are different; more plebeian, perhaps, they would say; but, husband mine, reflect! You, too, I fear, are lacking in refinement: would this have been, had you not acquiesced in all these vulgar whims, and found contentment, like me, in giving pleasure to the needy? And tell me—passing to another point—where would have been the monarch of this joy, that little child—that foretaste of bliss such as the angels taste—had I recoiled from daring as I did, even when I knew he I most wished to win would think me bold?"

"Ah! little wife," cried Charles, "I have half a mind to tell you what I have never told you yet. Yes, I will tell you all, although it may end the complacent thought that Linda did it—did it by simply daring to propose!

Know, then, that I kept a constant track of you, even while I seemed to shun you. I could kneel before your memory in my heart,

when you regarded me as shy and cold.

And, while held back by poverty,

I saw, supreme among my hopes, only Linda! Before we left the seaside I had learned, through gossip of my worthy landlady,

Where would you go, returning to New York? I found your house; I passed it more than once when your study-lamp shone like a beacon. The very night before you called upon me to ask if I would take Rachel as my pupil—how kind of you to patronize my school!—I sought an anodyne for my despair

in watching for your shadow on the curtain.

Discovery of that unexpected debt,

owed by my father, killed the last faint hope which I had cherished. And our interview—your daring offer of this little hand—only made me eager to equal you

in self-renouncing generosity.

And so I frankly told you what I told: that love and marriage were not in my lot.

Ten days passed, and then from utter gloom I sprang to cheerful light. My father's partner, the man named Judd, who robbed us all one day, had a moment of remorse and sent

a hundred thousand dollars back to us. Why do you smile?

Go on. It is worth a smile.

That very day I cleared myself from debt. That very day I asked for Linda's hand. That very day she gave it willingly.

And the next month saw us two made one. And so it would have been, if you, my dear, had made no sign and waited patiently. But ah! what luck was mine! After two days, the news arrived that Linda was an heiress. An heiress! Think of it—and I had said never, no, never would I wed an heiress! But it was too late for scruples; I was married—caught in the trap I always meant to shun!

Then Linda, mischief in her smile, exclaimed: "O simple Charles! The innocent dear man! Who doubts that a woman ought to hold her tongue and wait till he, the preordained, appears? That hundred thousand dollars—you are sure it was from your father's partner, from Judd?"

Of course it was—from Judd, and no one else! Who could have sent the money, if not Judd? No doubt it came from Judd! My father said it was conscience-money, restored by Judd, who had become a deacon in the Church. Why did you ask me whether I was sure

the hundred thousand dollars came from Judd? What are you smiling at, provoking Linda? O, you are all so quick, so clever, you men! And women are so dull and credulous,

so easily duped when left to go alone! What you would prove is that my daring step in being first to make a declaration

was needless, since priority in love

was yours, and your intention would have brought the same result about without my seeking. Know then, the money was not yours until I had got the news of my recovered fortune. From me the money came, and only from me.

And all that story of a Judd turned deacon, grown penitent and making restitution, was a mere myth invented by your father, lest you might hesitate to take the money. Now if I had not sought you as I did,

and if I had not put you to the test,

and if I had not learned your secret grief, we might have lived till we were gray and bent before a step of yours had brought us nearer. "Outflanked! I admit it, and I give it up!" cried Charles, all flushing with astonishment. "But how I will scold that ancient fisherman, my graceless father, for deceiving me! See him stand there, as if with a clear conscience, throwing the line for innocent, fat trout! With that grave face, saying the money came from Judd—from Deacon Judd! I'll deacon him!"

"What! You regret it, do you, Master Charles? You would straighten the crooked ways that brought you where you are, and undo the past? To think that a woman has wooed you, to think that a woman has won you, is too humbling and too scandalous for you to bear! It is an indignity too hard to endure!

Oh! Well, sir, well; do as you please. But the child goes with its mother; remember that." And here the infant opened its eyes, revealing orbs as blue as the shadows cast on Saranac's blue waters by overhanging woods. Said Lothian, snatching up the smiling wonder and handing it, with kisses, to the mother: "Take all your woman's rights; even this, the best of them. Are we not each richer by sharing such a gift? I will not regret your daring."

This is but a very mild statement of the social fictions under which woman is now suffering in mind, body, and estate; but it is valuable as coming from a witness who hopes that some less radical remedy than female suffrage will be found for existing evils. If the remedy lies with woman herself, as all admit, how can we expect her to act efficiently until she is a modifying force in legislation?

"_Unions no priest, no church can sanctify._"

"The most absurd ideas," says J. A. St. John, "have prevailed on the subject of marriage. Marriage, it is said, is a divine institution, therefore marriages are made in heaven; but the conclusion does not follow at all. The meaning of the first statement is simply that God originally ordained that men and women should be united in wedlock; but that he determined which particular men and women should be united, everyday experience proves to be false. It is admitted on all sides that marriage is intended to bring happiness to those who wed. Now, if it is found that marriage does not bring them happiness, it is undoubted proof that they ought not to have been united, and that the sooner they separate the better. But some people are deterred from accepting this doctrine by misrepresentations of scripture, others by considerations of policy, and others again by an entire indifference to human happiness. "That moment should the intimate relations of marriage end, and a release be found!"

In the United States the action of certain State legislatures, in increasing the facilities for divorce, has been a subject of alarm among people raised under the influences of a more conservative system. It would be difficult to show as yet whether social morality is harmed or helped by the increased freedom. Nothing can be more deceptive and unsatisfactory than the statistics offered on both sides of the question. It is generally admitted, we believe, that in those countries where divorce is most difficult, the number of illegitimate births is largest, and the reputation of married women is most questionable. In the nature of things, since much of the prevalent immorality is secret and hidden, it is impossible to estimate the extent of the evils growing out of illiberal laws in relation to marriage. In any legislation on the subject women should have a voice.

"Unlike the Church, I look on marriage as a civil contract, not a sacrament."

Kenrick here refers, of course, to the Catholic Church. Its theory of marriage—that it is a sacrament and cannot be dissolved once it has been contracted according to the Church's forms—still influences the legislation and social prejudices of Protestant communities regarding their own religious forms of marriage. It was not until the twelfth century, under Pope Innocent III., that divorce was prohibited by both civil and canon law. But under the Catholic system, only a marriage between Catholics is indissoluble. In the case of a marriage between Protestants, the tie is not regarded as binding. A dissolution was actually granted in such a case in 1857, when one of the parties turned Catholic, by the bishop of Rio Janeiro, who pronounced an uncanonical marriage null and void. Modern legislation, in establishing the validity of civil marriages, aimed a severe blow at ecclesiastical privilege.

To Rome, and not to the Bible, we must go for all the authority we can produce for denying that marriage is simply a civil contract. The form binding one man to one woman had its origin outside of the Bible. Up to the time of Charlemagne in the eighth century, polygamy and concubinage were common among Christians and countenanced by the Church. Even Luther seems to have had somewhat lax, though not unscriptural, notions on the subject. When Philip, landgrave of Hesse-Cassel, wanted to take another wife and threatened to get a dispensation from the Pope for the purpose, Luther convoked a synod composed of six of his proselytes. They declared that marriage is merely a civil contract, and that they could find no passage in the Holy Scriptures ordaining monogamy. They consequently signed a decree permitting Philip to take a second wife without repudiating his first.

In that reconstruction of laws threatened by the movement in behalf of female suffrage, it is not probable that the patriarchal institution of polygamy will be regarded otherwise than as debasing to both sexes. But perhaps a greater latitude of divorce will be sought as not inconsistent with public morality. Looking at the question abstractly, and apart from all religious and social prejudice, it certainly seems the height of cruelty and absurdity to compel parties to keep up the relations of man and wife when one of them feels towards the other either a physical repugnance or a moral dislike. The impediments often raised by our courts in the way of divorce are gross relics of barbarism, and will be abolished by a higher legislative morality.

"Whoso," says Milton, "prefers either matrimony or other ordinance before the good of man and the plain exigence of charity, let him profess Papist or Protestant or what he will, he is no better than a pharisee, and understands not the gospel; whom, as a misinterpreter of Christ, I openly protest against." And in another passage he rebukes those who would rest "in the mere element of the text," as favoring "the policy of the Devil to make that gracious ordinance (of marriage) become insupportable, that what with men not daring to venture upon wedlock, and what with men wearied out of it, all inordinate license might abound."

Mr. J. A. St. John, editor of the Prose Works of Milton, remarks in reference to the marriage law as it now stands in England:--

"Having been invented and established by men, it is designed to bear with extreme severity on women, who are daily subjected to wrongs and hardships they would not endure if the relief of divorce were open to them. Those who take a different view talk at length about the encouragement they say would be given to immorality if divorce were made easy. But the opposite is the truth.

"It is on behalf of morals, and for the sake of giving a higher tone to the feelings of society, that the present unnatural system should be abolished. Where what Milton calls an unconjugal mind exists, there must be unconjugal manners; and no one needs to be told what these lead to. Where marriage is indissoluble, people presume upon that fact to transgress its laws, which they would not do if it were legally practicable to obtain immediate redress.

"However, there is a great reluctance in mankind to innovate in legislation; and they generally prefer to be miserable according to rule rather than free and happy on a new principle.... Whenever it clearly appears that man and wife can no longer live together in peace and harmony, their separation would be far more beneficial to themselves and favorable to morals than their compulsory union. Milton's notions of married life are highly flattering to women, whom he evidently contemplates as the equal companions of men."

"_Is against nature._"

A curious instance of the boldness with which glaring errors are pressed into the service of criticism is presented in some remarks in the _N. Y. Nation_. "There is probably," it says, "no incident of woman's condition which is more clearly natural than her passivity in all that relates to marriage. In waiting to be wooed, she not only complies with one of the conventional proprieties, but obeys what appears to be a law of sex, _not amongst human beings only, but among all animals_."

These remarks have been adopted by many American journalists, and have been accepted perhaps by many readers as settling the whole question with scientific accuracy and force, so far as analogies drawn from the habits of the lower animals can settle it. But if the critic, while buttering his daily bread or putting cream into his daily coffee, had acquainted himself with the habits of the useful animal to which he is indebted, he would never have been guilty of so prodigious a blunder. So far from passively "waiting to be wooed," the cow, when the sexual impulse is awakened, will disturb the whole neighborhood by her bellowings. Should the critic reply that this is because she is kept in an unnatural state of restraint, such a reply would only add additional force to the contradiction of the argument which he would offer.

Other examples in abundance, in confutation of his assumption, could no doubt be furnished. But even if that assumption were true, we might sometimes be led to rather awkward results if we were to take the habits of the lower animals as authoritative. Certain animals have not infrequently an eccentric habit of destroying their offspring. Some of our Chinese brethren, borrowing a hint perhaps from the brute creation, are said to think it no sin to kill such female children as they have no use for. We hope that no enterprising critic will recommend such a solution as this of the woman problem.
