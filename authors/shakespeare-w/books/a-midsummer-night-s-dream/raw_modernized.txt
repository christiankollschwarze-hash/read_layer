###TITLE### A Midsummer Night's Dream
###AUTHOR### William Shakespeare

###CHAPTER### Act I

SCENE I. Athens. A room in the Palace of Theseus

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, Philostrate and Attendants.

THESEUS.

Now, fair Hippolyta, our wedding hour

draws near. Four happy days bring in another moon; but oh, how slowly this old moon wanes, I think! She delays my desires, like a stepmother or a widow,

who makes a young man wait endlessly for his inheritance.

HIPPOLYTA.

Four days will quickly pass into night; four nights will quickly dream away the time; and then the moon, like a silver bow

newly bent in heaven, shall look upon the night of our celebrations.

THESEUS.

Go, Philostrate,

stir up the young people of Athens to festivities; awaken the lively and quick spirit of mirth; send melancholy off to funerals;

that pale companion has no place in our ceremony.

[Exit Philostrate.]

Hippolyta, I courted you with my sword,

and won your love by doing you harm;

but I will marry you in a different manner,

with ceremony, with triumph, and with celebration.

Enter Egeus, Hermia, Lysander and Demetrius.

EGEUS.

Happiness to Theseus, our renowned Duke!

THESEUS.

Thanks, good Egeus. What news do you bring?

EGEUS.

I come full of frustration, with a complaint

against my child, my daughter Hermia.

Step forward, Demetrius. My noble lord,

this man has my consent to marry her.

Step forward, Lysander. And, my gracious Duke, this man has bewitched my child's heart. You, you, Lysander, you have given her poems, and exchanged love-tokens with my child. You have sung at her window by moonlight, with a pretending voice, verses of pretended love; and stolen the impression of her imagination with bracelets of your hair, rings, trinkets, fancy gifts, knick-knacks, trifles, nosegays, sweets—messengers of strong influence on unhardened youth. With cunning you have stolen my daughter's heart, turned her obedience (which is due to me) into stubborn harshness. And, my gracious Duke, if she will not here before your grace consent to marry Demetrius,

I demand the ancient privilege of Athens:

as she is mine, I may dispose of her;

which shall be either to this gentleman

or to her death, according to our law

immediately provided for that case.

THESEUS.

What do you say, Hermia? Be advised, fair maid. To you your father should be as a god;

one who created your beauties, yes, and one to whom you are but as a shape in wax

imprinted by him, and within his power

to leave the figure as it is, or disfigure it.

Demetrius is a worthy gentleman.

HERMIA.

So is Lysander.

THESEUS.

In himself he is.

But in this matter, lacking your father's approval, the other must be considered the worthier.

HERMIA.

I wish my father could see with my eyes.

THESEUS.

Rather your eyes must see with his judgment.

HERMIA.

I beg your Grace to pardon me.

I do not know by what power I am made bold, nor how it may affect my modesty

to plead my thoughts in such a presence here: but I beg your Grace that I may know the worst that may happen to me in this case, if I refuse to wed Demetrius.

THESEUS.

Either to die the death, or to renounce

forever the company of men.

Therefore, fair Hermia, question your desires, know your youth, examine well your passions, whether, if you do not yield to your father's choice, you can endure the clothing of a nun,

to be forever shut up in a shady cloister,

to live a childless sister all your life,

chanting faint hymns to the cold fruitless moon. Thrice-blessed are they who master their passions so as to undergo such a maiden pilgrimage,

but more earthly happy is the rose distilled than that which, withering on the virgin thorn, grows, lives, and dies, in single blessedness.

HERMIA.

So will I grow, so live, so die, my lord, before I will give up my virginity

to his lordship, whose unwanted yoke

my soul does not consent to be ruled by.

THESEUS.

Take time to think; and by the next new moon—the day that seals the bond between my love and me

for everlasting fellowship—

on that day either prepare to die

for disobedience to your father’s will,

or else to marry Demetrius, as he wishes,

or on Diana’s altar to vow

forever a life of austerity and singleness.

DEMETRIUS.

Relent, sweet Hermia; and, Lysander, give up your flawed claim to my certain right.

LYSANDER.

You have her father’s love, Demetrius.

Let me have Hermia’s. You marry him.

EGEUS.

Scornful Lysander, true, he has my love; and what is mine my love will give to him. And she is mine, and all my right to her I transfer to Demetrius.

LYSANDER.

I am, my lord, as well born as he,

as wealthy; my love is greater than his; my fortunes are in every way as highly ranked,

if not higher, than Demetrius’s;

and, which is more than all these boasts can be, I am loved by beautiful Hermia.

Why should I not then pursue my right? Demetrius, I’ll declare it to his face,

made love to Nedar’s daughter, Helena,

and won her soul; and she, sweet lady, dotes, devoutly dotes, dotes in idolatry,

upon this stained and unfaithful man.

THESEUS.

I must confess that I have heard as much, and meant to speak with Demetrius about it; but, being too full of my own affairs,

my mind lost track of it.—But, Demetrius, come, and come, Egeus; you shall go with me.

I have some private instruction for you both.— For you, fair Hermia, prepare yourself to fit your desires to your father’s will, or else the law of Athens delivers you up

(which by no means may we lessen)

to death, or to a vow of single life.

Come, my Hippolyta. How are you, my love? Demetrius and Egeus, come along;

I must employ you in some business

for our wedding, and speak with you about something that closely concerns yourselves.

EGEUS.

With duty and desire we follow you.

[Exeunt all but Lysander and Hermia.]

LYSANDER.

How now, my love? Why is your cheek so pale? How is it that the roses there fade so fast?

HERMIA.

Likely for lack of rain, which I could well give them from the storm of my eyes.

LYSANDER.

Ah me! From all that I could ever read, could ever hear by tale or history,

the course of true love never did run smooth. But either it was different in rank—

HERMIA.

O curse! Too high to be bound to one so low.

LYSANDER.

Or else mismatched in respect of years—

HERMIA.

O spite! Too old to be engaged to one so young.

LYSANDER.

Or else it depended on the choice of friends—

HERMIA.

O hell! To choose love by another’s eyes!

LYSANDER.

Or, if there were a mutual sympathy in choice,

war, death, or sickness did lay siege to it, making it as momentary as a sound,

swift as a shadow, short as any dream,

brief as the lightning in the blackened night that, in a flash, reveals both heaven and earth, and, before a man has power to say, “Behold!” the jaws of darkness devour it up:

so quickly bright things come to ruin.

HERMIA.

If then true lovers have ever been crossed,

it stands as a decree of destiny.

Then let us teach our trial patience,

because it is a customary cross,

as due to love as thoughts and dreams and sighs, wishes and tears, poor fancy’s followers.

LYSANDER.

A good argument; therefore, hear me, Hermia. I have a widowed aunt, a dowager

of great income, and she has no child. Her house is seven leagues from Athens, and she regards me as her only son.

There, gentle Hermia, may I marry you.

And the sharp Athenian law cannot follow us there. So if you love me, slip out of your father’s house tomorrow night. And in the wood, a league outside the town (where I once met you with Helena

to celebrate a May morning),

there I will wait for you.

HERMIA.

My good Lysander!

I swear to you by Cupid’s strongest bow, by his best arrow with the golden head,

by the innocence of Venus’ doves,

by that which binds souls and makes love prosper, and by that fire which burned the Carthage queen when the false Trojan was seen under sail, by all the vows that men have ever broken (more in number than women have ever spoken),

in that same place you have appointed for me, tomorrow I will truly meet with you.

LYSANDER.

Keep your promise, love. Look, here comes Helena.

Enter Helena.

HERMIA.

God speed, fair Helena! Where are you going?

HELENA.

Do you call me fair? Then take that “fair” back. Demetrius loves your fairness. O happy fair one! Your eyes are lodestars, and your tongue’s sweet voice is more musical than a lark to a shepherd’s ear, when wheat is green and hawthorn buds appear. Sickness is catching. O, if only beauty were too,

I would catch yours, fair Hermia, before I go. My ear would catch your voice, my eye your eye, my tongue would catch your tongue’s sweet melody. If the world were mine, and Demetrius excluded, I would give the rest to be transformed into you. O, teach me how you look, and with what skill you sway the motion of Demetrius’ heart!

HERMIA.

I frown at him, yet he still loves me.

HELENA.

O, if only your frowns could teach my smiles such power!

HERMIA.

I curse him, yet he gives me love.

HELENA.

O, if only my prayers could stir such affection!

HERMIA.

The more I hate him, the more he follows me.

HELENA.

The more I love him, the more he hates me.

HERMIA.

His foolishness, Helena, is no fault of mine.

HELENA.

None but your beauty; if only that fault were mine!

HERMIA.

Take comfort: he shall no more see my face. Lysander and I will flee this place. Before I saw Lysander,

Athens seemed like a paradise to me.

O, then, what graces dwell in my love, that he has turned a heaven into hell!

LYSANDER.

Helen, we will reveal our plan to you.

Tomorrow night, when the moon beholds

her silver face in the watery mirror,

decorating the bladed grass with liquid pearl (a time that still conceals lovers’ flights), we have planned to steal through Athens’ gates.

HERMIA.

And in the wood where often you and I

used to lie upon faint primrose beds, emptying our hearts of their sweet secrets, there my Lysander and I shall meet. And from there we shall turn our eyes away from Athens, to seek new friends and unfamiliar company. Farewell, sweet playfellow. Pray for us, and may good luck grant you your Demetrius!

Keep your word, Lysander. We must starve our eyes of lovers’ food until deep midnight tomorrow.

LYSANDER.

I will, my Hermia.

[Exit Hermia.]

Helena, farewell.

May Demetrius dote on you, as you dote on him!

[Exit Lysander.]

HELENA.

How much happier some people can be than others!

Throughout Athens I am thought to be as fair as she is. But what does that matter? Demetrius does not think so; he will not see what everyone but him knows. And just as he errs, doting on Hermia’s eyes, so I err, admiring his qualities.

Love can transform base and worthless things, which have no substance, into shape and dignity.

Love doesn't look with the eyes, but with the mind; and that is why winged Cupid is painted blind. Nor does love's mind have any sense of judgment. Wings and no eyes represent careless haste. And that is why love is said to be a child, because in choosing he is so often deceived. Just as playful boys break their promises in games, so the boy Love breaks his promises everywhere.

For before Demetrius looked on Hermia's eyes, he rained down oaths that he was only mine; and when this hail felt some warmth from Hermia, he dissolved, and showers of oaths melted away. I will go tell him of fair Hermia's flight. Then tomorrow night he will go to the wood

to pursue her; and for this information

if I receive thanks, it is a costly reward.

But in this I mean to enrich my suffering,

to have his sight there and back again.

[Exit Helena.]

SCENE II. The Same. A Room in a Cottage

Enter Quince, Snug, Bottom, Flute, Snout and Starveling.

QUINCE.

Is all our company here?

BOTTOM.

You would do best to call them all together, man by man, according to the script.

QUINCE.

Here is the list of every man's name who is considered fit throughout all Athens to perform in our play before the Duke and Duchess on his wedding day at night.

BOTTOM.

First, good Peter Quince, say what the play is about; then read the names of the actors; and so come to the point.

QUINCE.

Indeed, our play is The Most Lamentable Comedy and Most Cruel Death of Pyramus and Thisbe.

BOTTOM.

A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a cheerful one. Now, good Peter Quince, call forth your actors by the list. Gentlemen, spread yourselves out.

QUINCE.

Answer as I call you. Nick Bottom, the weaver.

BOTTOM.

Ready. Name what part I am for, and proceed.

QUINCE.

You, Nick Bottom, are assigned to play Pyramus.

BOTTOM.

What is Pyramus—a lover, or a tyrant?

QUINCE.

A lover who kills himself most nobly for love.

BOTTOM.

That will require some tears in the true performance of it. If I do it, let the audience look to their eyes. I will stir up storms; I will grieve to some degree. To the rest—yet my main preference is for a tyrant. I could play Hercules excellently, or a part to rant and rage in, to tear everything apart.

The raging rocks

And shivering shocks

Shall break the locks

Of prison gates,

And Phoebus' chariot

Shall shine from far,

And make and ruin

The foolish Fates.

This was magnificent. Now name the rest of the players. This is Hercules' style, a tyrant's style; a lover is more sorrowful.

QUINCE.

Francis Flute, the bellows-mender.

FLUTE.

Here, Peter Quince.

QUINCE.

Flute, you must take on the role of Thisbe.

FLUTE.

What is Thisbe? A wandering knight?

QUINCE.

It is the lady that Pyramus must love.

FLUTE.

No, truly, do not make me play a woman. I have a beard coming in.

QUINCE.

That makes no difference. You shall play it in a mask, and you may speak as softly as you like.

BOTTOM.

And if I may hide my face, let me play Thisbe too. I'll speak in a wonderfully tiny voice: "Thisne, Thisne!"—"Ah, Pyramus, my lover dear! Your Thisbe dear! And lady dear!"

QUINCE.

No, no, you must play Pyramus; and, Flute, you play Thisbe.

BOTTOM.

Well, proceed.

QUINCE.

Robin Starveling, the tailor.

STARVELING.

Here, Peter Quince.

QUINCE.

Robin Starveling, you must play Thisbe's mother. Tom Snout, the tinker.

###CHAPTER### Snout

Here, Peter Quince.

QUINCE.

You, Pyramus’ father; myself, Thisbe’s father; Snug, the joiner, you, the lion’s part. And I hope here is a play fitted.

###CHAPTER### Snug

Do you have the lion's part written out? Please, if you do, give it to me, because I am slow at memorizing.

QUINCE.

You can do it on the spot, because it is nothing but roaring.

BOTTOM.

Let me play the lion too. I will roar so well that it will do any man's heart good to hear me. I will roar so well that I will make the Duke say, "Let him roar again, let him roar again."

QUINCE.

If you do it too fiercely, you would frighten the Duchess and the ladies so much that they would shriek; and that would be enough to get us all hanged.

###CHAPTER### All

That would get us all hanged, every one of us.

BOTTOM.

I agree, friends, that if you frighten the ladies out of their wits, they would have no choice but to hang us. But I will soften my voice so much that I will roar as gently as any baby dove; I will roar like a nightingale.

QUINCE.

You can play no part but Pyramus, because Pyramus is a sweet-faced man; a handsome man, as you might see on a summer's day; a most lovely gentleman-like man. Therefore you must play Pyramus.

BOTTOM.

Well, I will take on the part. What beard would be best for me to wear in it?

QUINCE.

Why, whatever you like.

BOTTOM.

I will perform it in either your straw-colored beard, your orange-tan beard, your deep purple beard, or your French-crown-colored beard, your perfect yellow.

QUINCE.

Some of your French crowns have no hair at all, and then you will play bare-faced. But, gentlemen, here are your parts, and I must beg you, ask you, and urge you to learn them by tomorrow night; and meet me in the palace wood, a mile outside the town, by moonlight; there we will rehearse, because if we meet in the city, we will be followed by company, and our plans will be found out. In the meantime I will make a list of props that our play needs. Please do not fail me.

BOTTOM.

We will meet, and there we may rehearse most boldly and courageously. Work hard, be perfect; goodbye.

QUINCE.

At the Duke's oak we meet.

BOTTOM.

Enough. Keep your promise, or give up the part.

[They exit.]

ACT II

SCENE I. A wood near Athens

Enter a Fairy at one door, and Puck at another.

PUCK.

Hello there, spirit! Where are you wandering?

###CHAPTER### Fairy

Over hill, over dale,

Through bush, through brier,

Over park, over pale,

Through flood, through fire,

I do wander everywhere,

Swifter than the moon's sphere;

And I serve the Fairy Queen,

To dew her orbs upon the green.

The tall cowslips are her attendants; in their gold coats you can see spots.

Those are rubies, fairy gifts.

In those freckles live their perfumes. I must go seek some dew-drops here,

And hang a pearl in every cowslip's ear. Farewell, you lout of spirits; I'll be gone. Our Queen and all her elves will come here soon.

PUCK.

The King holds his revels here tonight; take care the Queen does not come within his sight, for Oberon is extremely fierce and angry,

Because she has as her attendant a lovely boy, stolen from an Indian king; she never had so sweet a changeling.

And jealous Oberon would have the child

Be a knight of his train, to roam the wild forests: but she forcibly withholds the beloved boy, crowns him with flowers, and makes him all her joy. And now they never meet in grove or green, by clear fountain, or glittering starlight brightness, without quarreling; so that all their elves creep into acorn cups for fear, and hide themselves there.

###CHAPTER### Fairy

Either I am mistaken about your shape and nature, or else you are that sly and mischievous spirit called Robin Goodfellow. Are you not he

who frightens the village girls, skims the milk, and sometimes works at the hand-mill, and makes the breathless housewife churn without result, and sometimes makes the drink fail to produce any froth, misleads night-wanderers, and laughs at their trouble? Those who call you Hobgoblin and sweet Puck, you do their work, and they shall have good luck. Are you not he?

PUCK.

You speak correctly;

I am that merry wanderer of the night.

I joke with Oberon and make him smile,

when I trick a fat, bean-fed horse by neighing like a young filly;

and sometimes I hide in a gossip's bowl

in the very likeness of a roasted crab apple,

and when she drinks, I bob against her lips and pour the ale down her withered neck. The wisest old woman, telling the saddest tale, sometimes mistakes me for a three-foot stool; then I slip out from under her bottom, down she topples, and cries "tailor," and falls into a cough; and then the whole choir hold their hips and laugh, and grow merry, and sneeze, and swear a merrier hour was never spent there.

But make room, fairy. Here comes Oberon.

###CHAPTER### Fairy

And here is my mistress. I wish he were gone!

Enter Oberon at one door, with his Train, and Titania at another, with hers.

OBERON.

We meet unhappily by moonlight, proud Titania.

TITANIA.

What, jealous Oberon! Fairies, skip away from here; I have sworn off his bed and his company.

OBERON.

Wait, reckless wanton; am I not your lord?

TITANIA.

Then I must be your lady; but I know

When you have stolen away from fairyland, and sat all day in the shape of Corin,

Playing on pipes of straw, and speaking verses of love to amorous Phillida. Why are you here,

Come from the farthest steep of India,

But that, indeed, the bouncing Amazon, your booted mistress and your warrior love, must be wedded to Theseus; and you come

To give their bed joy and prosperity?

OBERON.

How can you, for shame, Titania, cast doubt on my faithfulness with Hippolyta,

Knowing I know of your love for Theseus?

Did you not lead him through the glimmering night from Perigenia, whom he had ravished?

And make him break his faith with fair Aegles, with Ariadne and Antiopa?

TITANIA.

These are the inventions of jealousy:

And never, since the beginning of midsummer, have we met on hill, in dale, forest, or meadow, by paved fountain, or by rushy brook,

Or on the beached margin of the sea,

To dance our ringlets to the whistling wind, but with your quarrels you have disturbed our sport. Therefore the winds, piping to us in vain, as if in revenge, have sucked up from the sea contagious fogs; which, falling on the land, have made every paltry river so proud

That they have overflowed their banks. The ox has therefore stretched his yoke in vain, the ploughman lost his sweat, and the green corn has rotted before its youth attained a beard. The sheepfold stands empty in the drowned field, and crows are fattened on the diseased flock; the nine-men's-morris is filled up with mud, and the intricate mazes in the lush green, for lack of being walked upon, are indistinguishable. The human mortals lack their winter here. No night is now blessed with hymn or carol. Therefore the moon, the governess of floods, pale in her anger, washes all the air,

So that rheumatic diseases abound.

And through this disorder we see

The seasons alter: hoary-headed frosts

Fall in the fresh lap of the crimson rose; and on old Hiems' thin and icy crown

A fragrant chaplet of sweet summer buds

Is, as if in mockery, set. The spring, the summer, the fruitful autumn, angry winter, change their accustomed garments; and the bewildered world, by their produce, now knows not which is which. And this same offspring of evils comes

From our quarrel, from our dissension;

We are their parents and origin.

OBERON.

Then fix it. It lies in your power.

Why should Titania cross her Oberon?

I only ask for a little changeling boy

To be my page.

TITANIA.

Set your heart at rest;

The fairyland will not buy the child from me.

His mother was a votress of my order,

And in the spiced Indian air, by night,

She often gossiped by my side; and sat with me on Neptune's yellow sands, watching the embarked traders on the flood, when we have laughed to see the sails conceive, and grow big-bellied with the wanton wind; which she, with pretty and gliding gait, following (her womb then rich with my young squire), would imitate, and sail upon the land,

To fetch me trifles, and return again,

As from a voyage, rich with merchandise. But she, being mortal, died giving birth to that boy; and for her sake I raise her boy,

And for her sake I will not part with him.

OBERON.

How long do you intend to stay within this wood?

TITANIA.

Perhaps until after Theseus's wedding day. If you will patiently dance in our circle and watch our moonlight celebrations, come with us. If not, avoid me, and I will stay away from your favorite places.

OBERON.

Give me that boy, and I will go with you.

TITANIA.

Not for your fairy kingdom. Fairies, away! We will quarrel outright if I stay any longer.

[Exit Titania with her Train.]

OBERON.

Well, go your way. You shall not leave this grove until I torment you for this insult.—

My gentle Puck, come here. You remember how I once sat upon a high cliff,

And heard a mermaid on a dolphin's back

Producing such sweet and harmonious sound that the rough sea grew calm at her song, and certain stars shot wildly from their orbits to hear the sea-maid's music.

PUCK.

I remember.

OBERON.

At that very time I saw (though you could not), flying between the cold moon and the earth, Cupid fully armed. He took careful aim

At a beautiful vestal virgin, enthroned in the west,

And released his love-arrow sharply from his bow, as if it should pierce a hundred thousand hearts. But I could see young Cupid's fiery arrow quenched in the pure beams of the watery moon; and the royal priestess passed on,

In maiden meditation, untouched by love.

Yet I noticed where Cupid's arrow fell: it fell upon a little western flower,

Once milk-white, now purple with love's wound, and maidens call it love-in-idleness.

Fetch me that flower—the herb I showed you once. The juice of it, laid on sleeping eyelids, will make either a man or a woman fall madly in love

With the next living creature that he or she sees. Fetch me this herb, and be back here before the sea monster can swim a league.

PUCK.

I'll put a belt around the earth

In forty minutes.

[Exit Puck.]

OBERON.

Once I have this juice,

I'll watch Titania when she is asleep,

And drop the liquid into her eyes.

The next thing she sees when she wakes—whether it is a lion, bear, wolf, or bull,

A meddling monkey, or a busy ape—

She shall pursue it with the soul of love. And before I remove this charm from her sight (as I can remove it with another herb),

I'll make her hand over her page to me.

But who comes here? I am invisible,

And I will overhear their conversation.

Enter Demetrius, Helena following him.

DEMETRIUS.

I do not love you, so do not pursue me. Where are Lysander and fair Hermia?

The one I'll kill, the other is killing me. You told me they had stolen into this wood, and here I am, going mad within this wood because I cannot find Hermia.

Go away, get out of here, and follow me no more.

HELENA.

You draw me to you, you hard-hearted magnet,

But yet you do not draw iron, for my heart

Is as true as steel. Give up your power to draw, and I shall have no power to follow you.

DEMETRIUS.

Do I entice you? Do I speak kindly to you?

Or rather, do I not tell you in the plainest truth

That I do not, and cannot, love you?

HELENA.

And even for that, I love you all the more. I am your spaniel; and, Demetrius,

The more you beat me, the more I will fawn on you. Treat me only as your spaniel—kick me, strike me, neglect me, lose me; only allow me, unworthy as I am, to follow you.

What worse place can I beg for in your love—and yet a place of high respect to me—than to be used as you use your dog?

DEMETRIUS.

Do not tempt the hatred of my spirit too much, for I feel sick when I look at you.

HELENA.

And I feel sick when I do not look at you.

DEMETRIUS.

You compromise your modesty too much

To leave the city and put yourself

Into the hands of one who does not love you, to trust the opportunities of night

And the dangerous influence of a deserted place,

With the rich worth of your virginity.

HELENA.

Your goodness is my privilege: for that reason

It is not night when I do see your face, Therefore I think I am not in the night; Nor does this wood lack worlds of company, For you, in my opinion, are all the world. Then how can it be said I am alone

When all the world is here to look on me?

DEMETRIUS.

I’ll run from you and hide myself in the bushes, And leave you to the mercy of wild beasts.

HELENA.

The wildest beast does not have such a heart as you. Run when you will, the story shall be changed; Apollo flies, and Daphne holds the chase; The dove pursues the griffin, the gentle deer Makes speed to catch the tiger. Useless speed, When cowardice pursues and bravery flies!

DEMETRIUS.

I will not stay for your questions. Let me go, Or if you follow me, do not believe

But I shall do you harm in the wood.

HELENA.

Yes, in the temple, in the town, the field, You do me harm. Shame on you, Demetrius!

Your wrongs do set a scandal on my sex.

We cannot fight for love as men may do.

We should be wooed, and were not made to woo.

[Exit Demetrius.]

I’ll follow you, and make a heaven of hell, To die upon the hand I love so well.

[Exit Helena.]

OBERON.

Farewell, nymph. Before he leaves this grove, You shall flee from him, and he shall seek your love.

Enter Puck.

Do you have the flower there? Welcome, wanderer.

PUCK.

Yes, there it is.

OBERON.

Please give it to me.

I know a bank where the wild thyme blows, Where oxlips and the nodding violet grows, Quite over-canopied with lush woodbine, With sweet musk-roses, and with eglantine. There sleeps Titania some time of the night, Lulled in these flowers with dances and delight; And there the snake sheds her enameled skin, Weed wide enough to wrap a fairy in.

And with the juice of this I’ll streak her eyes, And make her full of hateful fantasies.

Take some of it, and seek through this grove: A sweet Athenian lady is in love

With a disdainful youth. Anoint his eyes; But do it when the next thing he sees

May be the lady. You shall know the man By the Athenian garments he has on.

Do it with some care, that he may prove More fond of her than she is of her love: And be sure to meet me before the first cock crow.

PUCK.

Fear not, my lord, your servant shall do so.

[Exeunt.]

SCENE II. Another part of the wood

Enter Titania with her Train.

TITANIA.

Come, now a round dance and a fairy song;

Then for the third part of a minute, away; Some to kill canker-worms in the musk-rose buds; Some to war with bats for their leathery wings, To make my small elves coats; and some keep back The clamorous owl, that nightly hoots and wonders At our clever spirits. Sing me now asleep; Then to your duties, and let me rest.

Fairies sing.

FIRST FAIRY.

You spotted snakes with double tongue, Thorny hedgehogs, be not seen;

Newts and blind-worms do no wrong,

Come not near our Fairy Queen:

CHORUS.

Philomel, with melody,

Sing in our sweet lullaby:

Lulla, lulla, lullaby; lulla, lulla, lullaby. Never harm, nor spell, nor charm,

Come our lovely lady near;

So good night, with lullaby.

FIRST FAIRY.

Weaving spiders, come not here;

Hence, you long-legged spinners, hence. Beetles black, approach not near;

Worm nor snail do no offense.

CHORUS.

Philomel with melody, etc.

SECOND FAIRY.

Away now! Now all is well.

One stand sentinel at a distance.

[Exeunt Fairies. Titania sleeps.]

Enter Oberon.

OBERON.

What you see when you wake,

[Squeezes the flower on Titania’s eyelids.]

Take it for your true love;

Love and languish for his sake.

Be it lynx, or cat, or bear,

Leopard, or boar with bristled hair,

In your eye that shall appear

When you wake, it is your dear one.

Wake when some vile thing is near.

[Exit.]

Enter Lysander and Hermia.

LYSANDER.

Fair love, you are faint from wandering in the wood. And, to tell the truth, I have forgotten our way. We will rest here, Hermia, if you think it good, and wait for the comfort of the day.

HERMIA.

Let it be so, Lysander. Find yourself a bed,

for I will rest my head upon this bank.

LYSANDER.

One patch of grass shall serve as pillow for us both; one heart, one bed, two bosoms, and one vow.

HERMIA.

No, good Lysander; for my sake, my dear, lie further off, do not lie so near.

LYSANDER.

Oh, take the true meaning, sweet, of my innocence! Love understands the meaning in love’s conversation. I mean that my heart is knit to yours, so that we can make only one heart of it: two bosoms bound together with an oath,

so then two bosoms and a single vow.

Then do not deny me a place to sleep by your side;

for lying so, Hermia, I do not lie.

HERMIA.

Lysander riddles very prettily.

Now much shame on my manners and my pride, if Hermia meant to say Lysander lied!

But, gentle friend, for love and courtesy, lie further off, in human modesty.

Such separation as may well be said

becomes a virtuous bachelor and a maid.

So far be distant; and good night, sweet friend. May your love never change until your sweet life ends!

LYSANDER.

Amen, amen, to that fair prayer say I;

and then may my life end when I end my loyalty!

Here is my bed. May sleep give you all his rest!

HERMIA.

With half that wish may the wisher’s eyes be closed!

[They sleep.]

Enter Puck.

PUCK.

Through the forest I have gone,

but no Athenian found I,

on whose eyes I might test

this flower’s power in stirring love.

Night and silence! Who is here?

He wears the clothes of Athens:

this is he, my master said,

who despised the Athenian maid;

and here the maiden, sleeping sound,

on the damp and dirty ground.

Pretty soul, she dared not lie

near this loveless man, this discourteous one. Churl, upon your eyes I throw

all the power this charm possesses;

when you wake, let love forbid

sleep to sit upon your eyelid.

So awake when I am gone;

for I must now go to Oberon.

[Exit.]

Enter Demetrius and Helena, running.

HELENA.

Stay, even if you kill me, sweet Demetrius.

DEMETRIUS.

I command you, go away, and do not haunt me like this.

HELENA.

Oh, will you leave me in the dark? Do not do so.

DEMETRIUS.

Stay, at your own peril; I will go alone.

[Exit Demetrius.]

HELENA.

Oh, I am out of breath from this foolish chase! The more I beg, the less favor I find. Happy is Hermia, wherever she lies,

for she has blessed and attractive eyes. How did her eyes become so bright? Not with salt tears. If so, my eyes are washed more often than hers. No, no, I am as ugly as a bear,

for beasts that meet me run away in fear. Therefore it is no wonder that Demetrius

flies from my presence like I am a monster.

What wicked and deceiving mirror of mine made me compare myself with Hermia’s starry eyes? But who is here? Lysander, on the ground! Dead or asleep? I see no blood, no wound. Lysander, if you live, good sir, awake.

LYSANDER.

[Waking.] And I would run through fire for your sweet sake. Transparent Helena! Nature shows her art,

that through your bosom makes me see your heart. Where is Demetrius? Oh, how fitting a word

is that vile name to perish on my sword!

HELENA.

Do not say so, Lysander, do not say so.

What though he loves your Hermia? Lord, what of it? Yet Hermia still loves you. Then be content.

LYSANDER.

Content with Hermia? No, I repent

the tedious minutes I have spent with her. Not Hermia, but Helena I love.

Who will not change a raven for a dove?

A man’s will is guided by his reason, and reason says you are the worthier young woman. Things that grow are not ripe until their proper season; so I, being young, until now was not ripe for reason. And now, reaching the point of human judgment, reason becomes the guide of my will,

and leads me to your eyes, where I read love’s stories, written in love’s richest book.

HELENA.

Why was I born to be mocked so cruelly? When did I ever deserve this scorn from you? Is it not enough, is it not enough, young man, that I never did, no, nor never can,

deserve a kind look from Demetrius’s eye, but you must mock my inadequacy?

Truly, you do me wrong, truly you do, by wooing me in such a disdainful manner.

But farewell; I must confess, I thought you were a lord of truer gentleness. Oh, that a lady refused by one man

should therefore be insulted by another!

[Exit.]

LYSANDER.

She does not see Hermia. Hermia, sleep there, and may you never come near Lysander again! For just as too much of the sweetest things brings the deepest disgust to the stomach; or as the heresies that men abandon

are hated most by those they deceived; so you, my excess and my heresy,

be hated by all, but most of all by me!

And, all my powers, direct your love and strength to honor Helen, and to be her knight!

[Exit.]

HERMIA.

[Starting.] Help me, Lysander, help me! Do your best to pull this crawling serpent from my chest! Ah me, for pity! What a dream this was!

Lysander, look how I tremble with fear. I dreamed a serpent ate my heart away,

and you sat smiling at his cruel feast.

Lysander! What, gone? Lysander! Lord! What, out of hearing? Gone? No sound, no word? Alas, where are you? Speak, if you can hear; speak, for the love of all! I nearly faint with fear. No? Then I clearly see you are not near. I will find either death or you immediately.

[Exit.]

###CHAPTER### Act Iii

SCENE I. The Wood.

The Queen of Fairies still lying asleep.

Enter Bottom, Quince, Snout, Starveling, Snug and Flute.

BOTTOM.

Are we all met?

QUINCE.

Right on time; and here's a wonderfully convenient place for our rehearsal. This green patch shall be our stage, this hawthorn bush our dressing room; and we will perform it just as we will before the Duke.

BOTTOM.

Peter Quince?

QUINCE.

What do you say, good fellow Bottom?

BOTTOM.

There are things in this comedy of Pyramus and Thisbe that will never go over well. First, Pyramus must draw a sword to kill himself; which the ladies cannot stand. How do you answer that?

###CHAPTER### Snout

By our Lady, a terrible fear.

STARVELING.

I believe we must leave the killing out, when all is done.

BOTTOM.

Not at all; I have a plan to make everything right. Write me a prologue, and let the prologue seem to say we will do no harm with our swords, and that Pyramus is not killed indeed; and for even more assurance, tell them that I, Pyramus, am not Pyramus but Bottom the weaver. This will put them out of fear.

QUINCE.

Well, we will have such a prologue; and it shall be written in eight and six.

BOTTOM.

No, make it two more; let it be written in eight and eight.

###CHAPTER### Snout

Won't the ladies be afraid of the lion?

STARVELING.

I'm afraid of it, I assure you.

BOTTOM.

Gentlemen, you should think carefully about this: bringing a lion among ladies—God protect us!—is a most dreadful thing. For there is not a more frightening wild creature than your lion alive; and we ought to take this seriously.

###CHAPTER### Snout

So another prologue is needed to tell them he is not a lion.

BOTTOM.

No, you must say his name, and half his face must be visible through the lion's neck. And he himself must speak through it, saying this, or something to the same effect: "Ladies," or "Fair ladies, I would wish you," or "I would request you," or "I would entreat you, not to fear, not to tremble: my life for yours. If you think I come here as a lion, it would be a pity for my life. No, I am no such thing; I am a man like other men." And there, indeed, let him say his name, and tell them plainly that he is Snug the joiner.

QUINCE.

Well, it shall be so. But there are two difficult things: that is, to bring the moonlight into a room, for you know, Pyramus and Thisbe meet by moonlight.

###CHAPTER### Snout

Will the moon be shining on the night we perform our play?

BOTTOM.

A calendar, a calendar! Look in the almanac; find out moonshine, find out moonshine.

QUINCE.

Yes, it will shine that night.

BOTTOM.

Why, then you can leave a window of the great chamber where we're performing open, and the moon can shine in through the window.

QUINCE.

Yes; or else someone must come in with a thorn bush and a lantern, and say he comes to represent the character of Moonshine. Then there is another thing: we must have a wall in the great chamber, because Pyramus and Thisbe, according to the story, talked through a crack in a wall.

###CHAPTER### Snout

You can never bring in a wall. What do you say, Bottom?

BOTTOM.

Some man or other must play Wall. And let him have some plaster, or some clay, or some rough-cast about him, to represent wall; and let him hold his fingers like this, and through that crack Pyramus and Thisbe shall whisper.

QUINCE.

If that can be done, then all is well. Come, sit down, every one of you, and rehearse your parts. Pyramus, you begin: when you have spoken your speech, go into that thicket; and so everyone according to his cue.

Enter Puck behind.

PUCK.

What coarse country bumpkins are swaggering here, so near the cradle of the Fairy Queen?

What, a play in the making? I'll be a spectator; and an actor too perhaps, if I see reason.

QUINCE.

Speak, Pyramus.—Thisbe, stand forward.

PYRAMUS.

_Thisbe, the flowers of odious savours sweet_

QUINCE.

Odours, odours.

PYRAMUS.

_. . . odours savours sweet.

So has your breath, my dearest Thisbe dear. But listen, a voice! Stay here just a little while, and soon I will come to you._

[_Exit._]

PUCK.

A stranger Pyramus than ever played here!

[_Exit._]

THISBE.

Must I speak now?

QUINCE.

Yes, indeed, you must, for you must understand he goes only to check a noise that he heard, and he is to come back again.

THISBE.

_Most radiant Pyramus, most lily-white of complexion, of colour like the red rose on triumphant brier, most lively young man, and also most lovely Jew, as true as the truest horse, that yet would never tire, I'll meet you, Pyramus, at Ninny's tomb._

QUINCE.

Ninus' tomb, man! Why, you must not speak that yet. That you answer to Pyramus. You speak all your part at once, cues and all.—Pyramus, enter! Your cue is past; it is 'never tire.'

THISBE.

O, _As true as the truest horse, that yet would never tire._

Enter Puck and Bottom with an ass's head.

PYRAMUS.

_If I were handsome, Thisbe, I would be only yours._

QUINCE.

O monstrous! O strange! We are haunted. Pray, masters, fly, masters! Help!

[_Exeunt Clowns._]

PUCK.

I'll follow you. I'll lead you in a circle, through bog, through bush, through thicket, through brier; sometimes I'll be a horse, sometimes a hound, a hog, a headless bear, sometimes a fire; and neigh, and bark, and grunt, and roar, and burn, like horse, hound, hog, bear, fire, at every turn.

[_Exit._]

BOTTOM.

Why do they run away? This is a trick of theirs to make me afraid.

Enter Snout.

###CHAPTER### Snout

O Bottom, you are changed! What do I see on you?

BOTTOM.

What do you see? You see an ass-head of your own, do you?

[_Exit Snout._]

Enter Quince.

QUINCE.

Bless you, Bottom! Bless you! You are transformed.

[_Exit._]

BOTTOM.

I see their trickery. This is to make an ass of me, to frighten me, if they could. But I will not move from this place, do what they can. I will walk up and down here, and I will sing, so they shall hear I am not afraid.

[_Sings._]

The blackbird, so black in color,

With orange-tawny beak,

The thrush with his note so true, The wren with little feather.

TITANIA.

[_Waking._] What angel wakes me from my flowery bed?

BOTTOM.

[_Sings._]

The finch, the sparrow, and the lark, The plain-song gray cuckoo,

Whose note full many a man does mark, And dares not answer no.

For, indeed, who would set his wits against so foolish a bird? Who would call a bird a liar, though he cry "cuckoo" never so much?

TITANIA.

I pray you, gentle mortal, sing again.

My ear is much in love with your note.

So is my eye captivated by your shape;

And your fair virtue's force compels me, on first view, to say, to swear, I love you.

BOTTOM.

I think, mistress, you should have little reason for that. And yet, to tell the truth, reason and love keep little company together nowadays. The more the pity that some honest neighbors will not make them friends. Nay, I can joke on occasion.

TITANIA.

You are as wise as you are beautiful.

BOTTOM.

Not so, neither; but if I had wit enough to get out of this wood, I have enough to serve my own turn.

TITANIA.

Do not desire to go out of this wood.

You shall remain here whether you will or no. I am a spirit of no common rank.

The summer still waits upon my state; And I do love you: therefore, go with me. I will give you fairies to attend on you; And they shall fetch you jewels from the deep, And sing, while you sleep on pressed flowers. And I will purify your mortal grossness so That you shall move like an airy spirit.— Peaseblossom! Cobweb! Moth! and Mustardseed!

Enter four Fairies.

PEASEBLOSSOM.

Ready.

COBWEB.

And I.

MOTH.

And I.

MUSTARDSEED.

And I.

ALL.

Where shall we go?

TITANIA.

Be kind and courteous to this gentleman; Hop in his walks and frolic in his eyes; Feed him with apricots and dewberries,

With purple grapes, green figs, and mulberries; Steal the honey-bags from the bumblebees, And for night-candles, crop their waxen thighs, And light them at the fiery glow-worm's eyes, To have my love to bed and to arise;

And pluck the wings from painted butterflies, To fan the moonbeams from his sleeping eyes. Nod to him, elves, and do him courtesies.

PEASEBLOSSOM.

Hail, mortal!

COBWEB.

Hail!

MOTH.

Hail!

MUSTARDSEED.

Hail!

BOTTOM.

I beg your worships' pardon, heartily.—I beseech your worship's name.

COBWEB.

Cobweb.

BOTTOM.

I shall desire more acquaintance with you, good Master Cobweb. If I cut my finger, I shall make bold with you.—Your name, honest gentleman?

PEASEBLOSSOM.

Peaseblossom.

BOTTOM.

I pray you, commend me to Mistress Squash, your mother, and to Master Peascod, your father. Good Master Peaseblossom, I shall desire more acquaintance with you too.—Your name, I beseech you, sir?

MUSTARDSEED.

Mustardseed.

BOTTOM.

Good Master Mustardseed, I know your patience well. That same cowardly giant-like ox-beef has devoured many a gentleman of your house. I promise you, your kindred has made my eyes water before now. I desire more acquaintance with you, good Master Mustardseed.

TITANIA.

Come, wait on him; lead him to my bower. The moon, I think, looks with a watery eye, and when she weeps, every little flower weeps too, lamenting some enforced chastity.

Tie up my love’s tongue; bring him silently.

[Exeunt.]

SCENE II. Another part of the wood

Enter Oberon.

OBERON.

I wonder if Titania is awake;

and what it was that next came into her sight, which she must dote on to an extreme degree.

Enter Puck.

Here comes my messenger. How now, mad spirit? What mischief is happening now in this haunted grove?

PUCK.

My mistress is in love with a monster.

Near to her private and consecrated bower, while she was in her dull and sleeping hour, a crew of patches, rude mechanicals,

that work for bread in Athenian shops, had met together to rehearse a play

intended for great Theseus’ wedding day. The shallowest blockhead of that barren sort, who played Pyramus in their entertainment,

left his scene and went into a thicket. When I took him at this advantage,

I fixed an ass’s head on his head.

Soon his Thisbe must be answered,

and out comes my actor. When they spy him, as wild geese that see the creeping fowler, or russet-pated choughs, many in a flock,

rising and cawing at the gun’s report,

separate themselves and madly sweep the sky, so at his sight his fellows fly away,

and at our stamp, here over and over one falls; he cries murder, and calls for help from Athens. Their senses thus weak, lost with their fears, thus strong, made senseless things begin to do them wrong; for briers and thorns snatch at their clothing; some sleeves, some hats, from those who yield, everything is caught. I led them on in this distracted fear,

and left sweet Pyramus transformed there. When in that moment, so it came to pass, Titania woke, and immediately loved an ass.

OBERON.

This turns out better than I could have planned. But have you yet latched the Athenian’s eyes with the love-juice, as I told you to do?

PUCK.

I took him sleeping—that is finished too—and the Athenian woman by his side,

so that, when he woke, she must of necessity be seen by him.

Enter Demetrius and Hermia.

OBERON.

Stand close. This is the same Athenian.

PUCK.

This is the woman, but not the same man.

DEMETRIUS.

Oh, why do you rebuke him who loves you so?

Lay breath so bitter on your bitter foe.

HERMIA.

Now I only scold, but I should treat you worse, for I fear you have given me cause to curse. If you have slain Lysander in his sleep, being up to your ankles in blood, plunge into the deep, and kill me too.

The sun was not so true unto the day

as he was to me. Would he have stolen away

from sleeping Hermia? I would as soon believe this whole earth may be bored through, and that the moon may creep through the center and so displease her brother’s noontide with the Antipodes. It cannot be but that you have murdered him. A murderer should look so, so dead, so grim.

DEMETRIUS.

So should the murdered look, and so should I, pierced through the heart with your stern cruelty. Yet you, the murderer, look as bright, as clear, as yonder Venus in her glimmering sphere.

HERMIA.

What does this have to do with my Lysander? Where is he? Ah, good Demetrius, will you give him to me?

DEMETRIUS.

I would rather give his carcass to my hounds.

HERMIA.

Out, dog! Out, cur! You drive me past the bounds of a maiden’s patience. Have you slain him, then? From now on, never be numbered among men!

Oh, once tell the truth; tell the truth, even for my sake! Would you have dared to look upon him while he was awake, and have you killed him sleeping? Oh, brave touch! Could not a worm, an adder, do so much?

An adder did it; for with a double tongue than yours, you serpent, never did an adder sting.

DEMETRIUS.

You are wasting your passion on a mistaken idea: I am not guilty of Lysander's blood;

Nor is he dead, as far as I can tell.

HERMIA.

I beg you, tell me then that he is well.

DEMETRIUS.

And if I could, what would I get for it?

HERMIA.

The privilege of never seeing me again.

And from your hated presence I will leave now:

See me no more, whether he is dead or not.

[Exit.]

DEMETRIUS.

There is no following her in this fierce mood. So I will stay here for a while. Sorrow's heaviness grows heavier

For the debt that bankrupt sleep owes to sorrow; which now it will pay in some small measure, if I rest here a little.

[Lies down.]

OBERON.

What have you done? You have made a complete mistake, and put the love-juice on some true lover's eyes. From your error it must follow

That some true love is turned false, and not a false one turned true.

PUCK.

Then fate rules over all: for every one man who keeps faith, a million fail, breaking oath after oath.

OBERON.

Go through the woods swifter than the wind, and make sure you find Helena of Athens.

She is lovesick and pale in the face, with sighs of love that cost her fresh blood dearly. By some illusion bring her here; I will charm his eyes before she appears.

PUCK.

I go, I go; look how I go,

Swifter than an arrow from the Tartar's bow.

[Exit.]

OBERON.

Flower of this purple dye,

Hit with Cupid's archery,

Sink into the apple of his eye.

When he sees his love,

Let her shine as gloriously

As the Venus of the sky.

When you wake, if she is nearby,

Beg of her for remedy.

Enter Puck.

PUCK.

Captain of our fairy band,

Helena is here at hand,

And the youth I mistook,

Is pleading for a lover's reward.

Shall we watch their foolish spectacle?

Lord, what fools these mortals be!

OBERON.

Stand aside. The noise they make

Will cause Demetrius to wake.

PUCK.

Then two at once will woo one.

That must be sport indeed;

And those things please me best

That happen absurdly.

Enter Lysander and Helena.

LYSANDER.

Why should you think that I woo in mockery? Scorn and derision never come in tears.

Look, when I vow, I weep; and vows born that way show their truth in their origin.

How can these things seem like mockery to you, when they carry the badge of faith to prove them true?

HELENA.

You show your cunning more and more. When truth kills truth, what a devilish-holy battle! These vows belong to Hermia: will you abandon her? Weigh your oath to her against your oath to me, and you weigh nothing: your vows to her and me, put in two scales, will weigh the same; and both as light as lies.

LYSANDER.

I had no judgment when I swore to her.

HELENA.

Nor do you have any now, in my opinion, when you abandon her.

LYSANDER.

Demetrius loves her, and he does not love you.

DEMETRIUS.

[Waking.] O Helen, goddess, nymph, perfect, divine! To what, my love, shall I compare your eyes? Crystal is muddy beside them. O how ripe and tempting

Your lips, those kissing cherries, grow! That pure congealed white, the snow of high Taurus, fanned by the eastern wind, turns to a crow when you hold up your hand. O, let me kiss this princess of pure white, this seal of bliss!

HELENA.

O spite! O hell! I see you are all determined

To gang up on me for your amusement.

If you were civilized and knew courtesy,

You would not do me so much injury.

Can you not hate me, as I know you do,

Without joining together in spirit to mock me too? If you were men, as you appear to be men, you would not treat a gentle lady so;

To vow, and swear, and overpraise my qualities, when I am sure you hate me in your hearts. You both are rivals, and love Hermia;

And now both rivals, to mock Helena.

A fine exploit, a manly enterprise,

To make tears come to a poor girl's eyes with your mockery! No one of noble character

would insult a maiden like this, and push

a poor soul's patience to the limit, just to amuse yourself.

LYSANDER.

You are unkind, Demetrius; do not be like this.

For you love Hermia; this you know I know. And here, with all good will, with all my heart, I give up my claim to Hermia's love in your favor. And leave your claim to Helena to me,

whom I do love and will love until my death.

HELENA.

Mockers never wasted more useless words.

DEMETRIUS.

Lysander, keep your Hermia; I do not want her.

If I ever loved her, all that love is gone. My heart only stayed with her as a guest, and now it has returned home to Helena,

there to remain.

LYSANDER.

Helena, it is not so.

DEMETRIUS.

Do not insult a faith you do not know, unless you want to pay dearly for it.

Look, here comes your love; there is your dear one.

Enter Hermia.

HERMIA.

Dark night takes away the eye's power, but makes the ear more sharp. What it takes from sight, it gives back doubly to hearing.

You are not found by my eye, Lysander; my ear, I thank it, brought me to your voice. But why did you unkindly leave me like that?

LYSANDER.

Why should he stay, when love urges him to go?

HERMIA.

What love could urge Lysander from my side?

LYSANDER.

Lysander's love, which would not let him stay—fair Helena, who makes the night more golden

than all those fiery circles and eyes of light. Why do you seek me? Could this not make you know that the hatred I felt for you made me leave you so?

HERMIA.

You do not speak as you think; it cannot be.

HELENA.

Look, she is one of this conspiracy!

Now I see they have all three joined together to create this cruel joke at my expense. Hurtful Hermia, most ungrateful girl!

Have you conspired, have you plotted with these men, to torment me with this foul mockery?

Is all the closeness that we two have shared, the sisters' vows, the hours that we have spent, when we scolded the swift-footed time

for parting us—oh, is all forgotten?

All our school-days' friendship, childhood innocence? We, Hermia, like two skilled creators,

have with our needles made both one flower, both on one sampler, sitting on one cushion, both singing one song, both in one key, as if our hands, our sides, voices, and minds, had been joined as one. So we grew together, like a double cherry, seeming parted, but yet a union in separation,

two lovely berries grown on one stem;

so, with two seeming bodies, but one heart; two of the first, like coats in heraldry, belonging to one alone, and crowned with one crest. And will you tear our old love apart, to join with men in scorning your poor friend? It is not friendly, it is not maidenly.

Our whole sex, as well as I, may scold you for it, though I alone feel the injury.

HERMIA.

I am astonished at your passionate words.

I do not scorn you; it seems that you scorn me.

HELENA.

Have you not set Lysander, as in mockery,

to follow me, and praise my eyes and face? And made your other love, Demetrius,

who even just now kicked me with his foot, call me goddess, nymph, divine and rare, precious, heavenly? Why does he speak this to her he hates? And why does Lysander deny your love, so rich within his soul, and offer me, indeed, affection,

except by your urging, by your consent? What though I am not as favored as you, so hung upon with love, so fortunate,

but most miserable, to love and not be loved?

This you should pity rather than despise.

HERMIA.

I do not understand what you mean by this.

HELENA.

Yes, do it. Keep it up, fake sad looks, make faces at me when I turn my back, wink at each other; keep this sweet joke going. This game, if played well, will be worth recording. If you had any pity, grace, or manners, you would not make me the subject of such mockery.

But farewell. It is partly my own fault, which death or absence will soon fix.

LYSANDER.

Stay, gentle Helena; hear my excuse.

My love, my life, my soul, fair Helena!

HELENA.

Oh, excellent!

HERMIA.

Sweet, do not scorn her so.

DEMETRIUS.

If she cannot persuade, I can force.

LYSANDER.

You can force no more than she can persuade. Your threats have no more strength than her weak prayers. Helen, I love you, by my life I do.

I swear by that life, which I will lose for you, to prove him false who says I do not love you.

DEMETRIUS.

I say I love you more than he can.

LYSANDER.

If you say so, step aside and prove it too.

DEMETRIUS.

Quick, come.

HERMIA.

Lysander, where is all this leading?

LYSANDER.

Away, you Ethiopian!

DEMETRIUS.

No, no. He will

seem to break loose. Pretend you would follow, but do not come. You are a tame man, go!

LYSANDER.

Get off me, you cat, you burr! Vile thing, let go, or I will shake you from me like a serpent.

HERMIA.

Why have you grown so rude? What change is this, sweet love?

LYSANDER.

Your love? Out, tawny Tartar, out!

Out, loathsome medicine! O hated potion, away!

HERMIA.

Are you not joking?

HELENA.

Yes, truly, and so are you.

LYSANDER.

Demetrius, I will keep my word with you.

DEMETRIUS.

I wish I had your bond; for I see

a weak bond holds you. I will not trust your word.

LYSANDER.

What, should I hurt her, strike her, kill her dead? Although I hate her, I will not harm her so.

HERMIA.

What, can you do me greater harm than hate? Hate me? Why? Oh me! What news, my love? Am I not Hermia? Are you not Lysander?

I am as fair now as I was before.

Since night you loved me; yet since night you left me. Why then, you left me—oh, the gods forbid!—in earnest, shall I say?

LYSANDER.

Yes, by my life.

And I never wish to see you again.

Therefore be out of hope, of question, of doubt. Be certain, nothing is truer; it is no joke

that I hate you and love Helena.

HERMIA.

Oh me! You juggler! You canker-blossom!

You thief of love! What, have you come by night and stolen my love's heart from him?

HELENA.

Fine, indeed!

Have you no modesty, no maiden shame,

no touch of bashfulness? What, will you tear impatient answers from my gentle tongue? Shame, shame, you counterfeit, you puppet, you!

HERMIA.

Puppet! Why so? Yes, that is how the game goes. Now I see that she has made comparison between our heights; she has boasted of her height; and with her person, her tall person, her height, indeed, she has won him over. And have you grown so high in his esteem

because I am so dwarfish and so low?

How low am I, you painted maypole? Speak. How low am I? I am not yet so low

but that my nails can reach your eyes.

HELENA.

I beg you, though you mock me, gentlemen, do not let her hurt me. I was never quarrelsome;

I have no gift at all for shrewishness;

I am a true maid in my cowardice;

do not let her strike me. You perhaps may think, because she is somewhat shorter than myself, that I can match her.

HERMIA.

Shorter! Listen, again.

HELENA.

Good Hermia, do not be so bitter with me. I always did love you, Hermia,

did ever keep your secrets, never wronged you, except that, in love for Demetrius,

I told him you had slipped away into this wood. He followed you; I followed him because I love him. But he scolded me and sent me away, and threatened to hit me, kick me, even kill me. And now, if you will just let me go in peace,

I will carry my foolishness back to Athens,

And follow you no further. Let me go.

You see how silly and how foolish I am.

HERMIA.

Well then, go. Who is stopping you?

HELENA.

A foolish heart that I leave behind here.

HERMIA.

What! With Lysander?

HELENA.

With Demetrius.

LYSANDER.

Do not be afraid; she shall not harm you, Helena.

DEMETRIUS.

No, sir, she shall not, even if you take her side.

HELENA.

Oh, when she is angry, she is sharp and fierce. She was a spitfire when she went to school, and though she is small, she is ferocious.

HERMIA.

“Little” again! Nothing but “low” and “little”? Why do you let her mock me like this? Let me get at her.

LYSANDER.

Get away from here, you dwarf;

You tiny thing, made from stunted knot-grass; you bead, you acorn.

DEMETRIUS.

You are too eager

To help someone who scorns your efforts. Leave her alone. Do not speak of Helena;

Do not take her side; for if you show even

The slightest sign of love toward her, you will pay for it.

LYSANDER.

Now she is not holding me back.

Now follow, if you dare, to test whose claim, yours or mine, is stronger in Helena.

DEMETRIUS.

Follow! No, I will go with you, side by side.

[Exit Lysander and Demetrius.]

HERMIA.

You, miss, all this trouble is because of you. No, do not go back.

HELENA.

I will not trust you,

Nor stay any longer in your spiteful company.

Your hands are quicker than mine for a fight. But my legs are longer, so I can run away.

[Exit.]

HERMIA.

I am stunned, and do not know what to say.

[Exit, pursuing Helena.]

OBERON.

This is your carelessness: you keep making mistakes, or else you are doing your mischief on purpose.

PUCK.

Believe me, king of shadows, I made a mistake.

Did you not tell me I would know the man by the Athenian clothes he was wearing?

And so my actions are blameless in this much: I did anoint an Athenian’s eyes. And I am glad it turned out this way,

Because I find their quarreling entertaining.

OBERON.

You see these lovers looking for a place to fight. Hurry, Robin, darken the night. Cover the starry sky soon

With heavy fog, as black as the river Acheron,

And lead these angry rivals so far astray

That one never comes into the other’s path.

Sometimes speak in Lysander’s voice, then provoke Demetrius with bitter insults; and sometimes rant like Demetrius.

And lead them away from each other in this way, until sleep that looks like death creeps over their brows with heavy legs and bat-like wings. Then crush this herb into Lysander’s eye; its juice has this healing power: to remove all error from his sight and make his eyes see normally again. When they next wake, all this mockery

Will seem like a dream and an empty vision; and the lovers will return to Athens, bound together in a union that will last until death. While I set you to this task,

I will go to my queen and beg for her Indian boy; and then I will release her enchanted eye

From the monster’s sight, and all things shall be at peace.

PUCK.

My fairy lord, this must be done quickly, for night’s swift dragons are cutting through the clouds at full speed; and over there shines the dawn’s messenger,

At whose approach, ghosts wandering here and there flock home to churchyards. All the damned spirits that are buried at crossroads and in rivers have already gone to their wormy beds;

For fear that daylight should look upon their shame, they willingly exile themselves from light, and must forever keep company with dark-browed night.

OBERON.

But we are spirits of another sort.

I have often played with the morning's love; and, like a forester, I may walk through the groves even to the eastern gate, all fiery-red, which opens on Neptune with fair blessed beams and turns his salt-green streams into yellow gold. But nevertheless, hurry, make no delay. We may still accomplish this business before day.

[_Exit Oberon._]

PUCK.

Up and down, up and down,

I will lead them up and down.

I am feared in field and town.

Goblin, lead them up and down.

Here comes one.

Enter Lysander.

LYSANDER.

Where are you, proud Demetrius? Speak now.

PUCK.

Here, villain, with my sword drawn and ready. Where are you?

LYSANDER.

I will be with you right away.

PUCK.

Follow me then to more open ground.

[_Exit Lysander as following the voice._]

DEMETRIUS.

Lysander, speak again.

You runaway, you coward, have you fled? Speak. In some bush? Where do you hide your head?

PUCK.

You coward, are you bragging to the stars, telling the bushes that you are looking for a fight, and yet you will not come? Come, coward, come, you child! I'll whip you with a rod. He is disgraced who draws a sword on you.

DEMETRIUS.

Yes, are you there?

PUCK.

Follow my voice; we will not test our courage here.

[_Exeunt._]

Enter Lysander.

LYSANDER.

He goes ahead of me and still dares me on; when I come where he calls, then he is gone. The villain is much lighter-footed than I. I followed fast, but faster he did fly,

so that I have fallen on the dark uneven path,

and here I will rest. Come, you gentle day! [_Lies down._] For if you only show me your grey light once, I'll find Demetrius and revenge this insult.

[_Sleeps._]

Enter Puck and Demetrius.

PUCK.

Ho, ho, ho! Coward, why don't you come?

DEMETRIUS.

Stay and face me, if you dare; for I know well you run before me, shifting from place to place, and dare not stand, nor look me in the face. Where are you?

PUCK.

Come here; I am here.

DEMETRIUS.

No, then, you mock me. You shall pay dearly for this if I ever see your face by daylight.

Now go your way. Weariness forces me to stretch out my full length on this cold bed. Expect a visit from me when day approaches.

[_Lies down and sleeps._]

Enter Helena.

HELENA.

O weary night, O long and tedious night, shorten your hours! Shine, comforts, from the east, so that I may go back to Athens by daylight,

away from these people who hate my poor company. And sleep, which sometimes closes up sorrow's eye, steal me away for a while from my own company.

[_Sleeps._]

PUCK.

Yet only three? Come one more.

Two of both kinds makes up four.

Here she comes, bad-tempered and sad.

Cupid is a mischievous lad

to make poor females mad like this.

Enter Hermia.

HERMIA.

Never so weary, never so full of woe,

splattered with the dew and torn by briers, I can no further crawl, no further go;

my legs can keep no pace with my desires. Here I will rest until the break of day. Heaven shield Lysander, if they mean to fight!

[_Lies down._]

PUCK.

On the ground

sleep sound.

I'll apply

to your eye,

gentle lover, a remedy.

When you wake,

you will take

true delight

in the sight

of your former lady's eye.

And the well-known country proverb,

that every man should take his own, shall be shown in your waking:

Jack shall have Jill;

nothing shall go ill;

the man shall have his mare again, and all shall be well.

###CHAPTER### Act Iv

SCENE I. The Wood

Lysander, Demetrius, Helena and Hermia still asleep.

Enter Titania and Bottom; Peaseblossom, Cobweb, Moth, Mustardseed and other Fairies attending; Oberon behind, unseen.

TITANIA.

Come, sit down upon this flowery bed, while I caress your lovely cheeks,

And stick musk-roses in your sleek smooth head, and kiss your fair large ears, my gentle joy.

BOTTOM.

Where's Peaseblossom?

PEASEBLOSSOM.

Ready.

BOTTOM.

Scratch my head, Peaseblossom. Where's Monsieur Cobweb?

COBWEB.

Ready.

BOTTOM.

Monsieur Cobweb, good monsieur, take up your weapons and kill me a red-hipped bumblebee on the top of a thistle; and, good monsieur, bring me the honey-bag. Do not strain yourself too much in the action, monsieur; and, good monsieur, be careful the honey-bag does not break; I would hate to have you drenched with a honey-bag, signior. Where's Monsieur Mustardseed?

MUSTARDSEED.

Ready.

BOTTOM.

Give me your fist, Monsieur Mustardseed. Please, no need for bowing, good monsieur.

MUSTARDSEED.

What do you want?

BOTTOM.

Nothing, good monsieur, but to help Cavalier Cobweb to scratch. I must go to the barber's, monsieur, for I think I am marvellously hairy about the face; and I am such a tender ass, if my hair so much as tickles me, I must scratch.

TITANIA.

What, would you like to hear some music, my sweet love?

BOTTOM.

I have a reasonably good ear for music. Let us have the tongs and the bones.

TITANIA.

Or say, sweet love, what you desire to eat.

BOTTOM.

Truly, a peck of provender; I could munch your good dry oats. I think I have a great desire for a bundle of hay: good hay, sweet hay, has no equal.

TITANIA.

I have a bold fairy who shall seek the squirrel's hoard, and fetch you fresh nuts.

BOTTOM.

I would rather have a handful or two of dried peas. But, I beg you, let none of your people disturb me; I have a strong desire to sleep coming over me.

TITANIA.

Sleep then, and I will wind you in my arms. Fairies, be gone, and go every way away.

So does the woodbine gently entwine the sweet honeysuckle; the female ivy likewise

Enrings the barky fingers of the elm.

O, how I love you! How I dote on you!

[_They sleep._]

Oberon advances. Enter Puck.

OBERON.

Welcome, good Robin. Do you see this sweet sight? I now begin to pity her foolish infatuation.

For, meeting her lately behind the wood, seeking sweet favours for this hateful fool, I scolded her and quarrelled with her: for she had then crowned his hairy temples with a wreath of fresh and fragrant flowers; and that same dew, which sometimes on the buds used to swell like round and lustrous pearls, now stood within the pretty little flowers' eyes, like tears that mourned their own disgrace. When I had taunted her as much as I pleased,

And she in mild words begged my patience, I then asked her for her changeling child; which she gave me at once, and sent her fairy to carry him to my bower in fairyland.

And now I have the boy, I will undo

This hateful defect of her eyes.

And, gentle Puck, take this transformed scalp from off the head of this Athenian youth, so that when he wakes along with the others,

They may all return to Athens again,

And think no more of this night's events except as the fierce disturbance of a dream.

But first I will release the Fairy Queen.

[_Touching her eyes with an herb._]

Be as you were accustomed to be;

See as you were accustomed to see.

Dian's bud over Cupid's flower

Has such force and blessed power.

Now, my Titania, wake, my sweet queen.

TITANIA.

My Oberon, what visions I have seen!

I thought I was in love with an ass.

OBERON.

There lies your love.

TITANIA.

How did these things come to happen?

Oh, how my eyes hate the sight of his face now!

OBERON.

Be quiet for a moment.—Robin, take off this donkey's head. Titania, call for music; and make these five sleep more deeply than ordinary sleep.

TITANIA.

Music, ho, music, such as charms people to sleep.

PUCK.

Now when you wake, look around with your own foolish eyes.

OBERON.

Play, music.

[_Soft music plays._]

Come, my queen, take my hand,

And rock the ground where these sleepers lie. Now you and I are friends again,

And tomorrow at midnight we will solemnly

Dance in Duke Theseus's house in triumph, and bless it with all good fortune.

There the pairs of faithful lovers will be married, along with Theseus, all in joy.

PUCK.

Fairy king, listen and pay attention.

I hear the morning lark.

OBERON.

Then, my queen, in solemn silence,

Let us move lightly after the shadows of night.

We can circle the globe quickly,

Faster than the wandering moon.

TITANIA.

Come, my lord, and during our flight,

Tell me how it happened this night

That I was found sleeping here

With these mortals on the ground.

[_They exit. Horns sound from offstage._]

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, Egeus, and their attendants.

THESEUS.

Go, one of you, find the forester;

For now our morning ritual is done;

And since we have the early part of the day, my love shall hear the music of my hounds. Release them in the western valley; let them go. Hurry, I say, and find the forester.

[_An attendant exits._]

We will, fair queen, go up to the mountain's top, and listen to the musical confusion

Of hounds and echo joining together.

HIPPOLYTA.

I was with Hercules and Cadmus once,

When in a wood in Crete they hunted the bear with hounds from Sparta. I never heard

Such fine barking; for besides the groves, the skies, the fountains, every nearby region seemed to be one single united cry. I never heard such a musical discord, such sweet thunder.

THESEUS.

My hounds are bred from the Spartan kind, with the same flews and sandy coloring; and their heads have ears that sweep away the morning dew; they have crooked knees and loose skin under their throats like Thessalian bulls; they are slow in pursuit, but their voices are matched like bells, each one under another. A more tuneful cry

Was never called to, nor encouraged with a horn, in Crete, in Sparta, nor in Thessaly.

Judge for yourself when you hear them.—But wait, what nymphs are these?

EGEUS.

My lord, this is my daughter here asleep, and this is Lysander; this is Demetrius;

This is Helena, old Nedar's Helena:

I am amazed to find them here together.

THESEUS.

No doubt they got up early to observe

The rites of May; and, hearing of our plans, came here in honor of our celebration.

But tell me, Egeus; is this not the day

That Hermia should give her answer about her choice?

EGEUS.

It is, my lord.

THESEUS.

Go, tell the huntsmen to wake them with their horns.

Horns sound, and shouts are heard from offstage. Demetrius, Lysander, Hermia, and Helena wake and jump up.

Good morning, friends. Saint Valentine's Day is past. Are these wood-birds only now beginning to pair up?

LYSANDER.

Pardon me, my lord.

He and the rest kneel to Theseus.

THESEUS.

I ask you all to stand up.

I know you two are rival enemies.

How does this gentle peace come into the world, that hatred is so far from jealousy

That you sleep beside your enemy and fear no hostility?

LYSANDER.

My lord, I shall answer in confusion,

Half asleep, half awake; but even now, I swear, I cannot truly say how I came here.

But, as I think (for I truly want to speak truthfully), and now I remember, this is how it is:

I came here with Hermia. Our intention

Was to leave Athens, where we might be without the danger of Athenian law.

EGEUS.

Enough, enough, my lord; you have heard enough. I demand the law, the law upon his head.

They would have stolen away, they would have, Demetrius, and by doing so defeated both you and me:

You speak of your wife, and I of my consent,

Of my consent that she should be your wife.

DEMETRIUS.

My lord, fair Helen told me of their secret plan, of their intention to come here to this wood. And I in fury followed them,

Fair Helena in fondness following me.

But, my good lord, I do not know by what power—but by some power it is—my love for Hermia has melted like the snow, and seems to me now

Like the memory of some worthless trinket

Which I adored in my childhood.

And all the faith, the virtue of my heart, the object and the pleasure of my eye, is only Helena. To her, my lord,

I was engaged before I ever saw Hermia.

But like a sickness I loathed this food. But now, as in health, I have come back to my natural taste. Now I wish for it, love it, long for it,

And will forever be true to it.

THESEUS.

Fair lovers, you are fortunately met.

We will hear more of this story soon. Egeus, I will overrule your will;

For in the temple, shortly, along with us,

These couples shall be eternally joined.

And since the morning is now somewhat spent, our planned hunting shall be set aside. Away with us to Athens. Three and three, we shall hold a feast in great solemnity.

Come, Hippolyta.

[Exeunt Theseus, Hippolyta, Egeus and Train.]

DEMETRIUS.

These things seem small and indistinct, like far-off mountains turned into clouds.

HERMIA.

I think I see these things with divided eyes, when everything seems double.

HELENA.

So do I.

And I have found Demetrius like a jewel, my own, and not my own.

DEMETRIUS.

Are you sure

That we are awake? It seems to me

That we are still sleeping, still dreaming. Do you not think the Duke was here, and told us to follow him?

HERMIA.

Yes, and my father.

HELENA.

And Hippolyta.

LYSANDER.

And he did tell us to follow him to the temple.

DEMETRIUS.

Why, then, we are awake. Let us follow him, and along the way let us tell each other our dreams.

[Exeunt.]

BOTTOM.

[Waking.] When my cue comes, call me, and I will answer. My next line is “Most fair Pyramus.” Heigh-ho! Peter Quince! Flute, the bellows-mender! Snout, the tinker! Starveling! God’s my life! Stolen away from here, and left me asleep! I have had a most extraordinary vision. I have had a dream, beyond the wit of man to say what dream it was. A man is but an ass if he tries to explain this dream. I thought I was—there is no man who can tell what. I thought I was, and I thought I had—but a man is but a patched fool if he offers to say what I thought I had. The eye of man has not heard, the ear of man has not seen, man’s hand is not able to taste, his tongue to conceive, nor his heart to report, what my dream was. I will get Peter Quince to write a ballad of this dream. It shall be called “Bottom’s Dream,” because it has no bottom; and I will sing it at the end of a play, before the Duke. Perhaps, to make it more pleasing, I shall sing it at her death.

[Exit.]

It is not possible. You do not have a man in all Athens able to perform Pyramus but him.

No, he simply has the best wit of any craftsman in Athens.

QUINCE.

Yes, and the best appearance too, and he is a very paragon for a sweet voice.

FLUTE.

You must say paragon. A paramour is, God bless us, a thing of naught.

###CHAPTER### Act V

SCENE I. Athens. An Apartment in the Palace of Theseus

Enter Theseus, Hippolyta, Philostrate, Lords and Attendants.

HIPPOLYTA.

It is strange, my Theseus, what these lovers speak of.

THESEUS.

More strange than true. I can never believe these old fables, nor these fairy toys. Lovers and madmen have such feverish brains, such shaping fantasies, that they perceive

more than cool reason ever comprehends.

The lunatic, the lover, and the poet

are made entirely of imagination.

One sees more devils than vast hell can hold; that is the madman. The lover, just as frantic, sees Helen's beauty in a gypsy's face.

The poet's eye, rolling in a fine frenzy, glances from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven; and as imagination gives form to

the shapes of unknown things, the poet's pen turns them into shapes, and gives to airy nothing a local home and a name.

Strong imagination has such tricks,

that if it would only imagine some joy, it understands some bringer of that joy. Or in the night, imagining some fear,

how easily is a bush supposed to be a bear?

HIPPOLYTA.

But when all the story of the night is told over, and all their minds are transformed so together, it testifies to more than fancy's images,

and grows to something of great constancy; but, however, strange and admirable.

Enter lovers: Lysander, Demetrius, Hermia and Helena.

THESEUS.

Here come the lovers, full of joy and mirth. Joy, gentle friends, joy and fresh days of love accompany your hearts!

LYSANDER.

More than to us

wait in your royal walks, your table, your bed!

THESEUS.

Come now; what masques, what dances shall we have, to wear away this long age of three hours between our after-supper and bed-time?

Where is our usual manager of mirth?

What revels are in hand? Is there no play to ease the anguish of a torturing hour? Call Philostrate.

PHILOSTRATE.

Here, mighty Theseus.

THESEUS.

Say, what entertainment do you have for this evening? What masque? What music? How shall we beguile the lazy time, if not with some delight?

PHILOSTRATE.

Here is a list of how many entertainments are ready. Make choice of which your Highness will see first.

[_Giving a paper._]

THESEUS.

[_Reads_] 'The battle with the Centaurs, to be sung by an Athenian eunuch to the harp.'

We will have none of that. I have told that to my love in glory of my kinsman Hercules.

'The riot of the tipsy Bacchanals,

tearing the Thracian singer in their rage?' That is an old device, and it was played when I came from Thebes last as a conqueror. 'The thrice three Muses mourning for the death of learning, lately deceased in beggary.'

That is some satire, keen and critical,

not fitting with a nuptial ceremony.

'A tedious brief scene of young Pyramus

and his love Thisbe; very tragical mirth.' Merry and tragical? Tedious and brief?

That is hot ice and wondrous strange snow. How shall we find the concord of this discord?

PHILOSTRATE.

There is a play, my lord, some ten words long, which is as brief as I have known a play; but by ten words, my lord, it is too long, which makes it tedious. For in all the play there is not one word apt, one player fitted. And tragical, my noble lord, it is.

For Pyramus therein does kill himself,

which, when I saw rehearsed, I must confess, made my eyes water; but more merry tears the passion of loud laughter never shed.

THESEUS.

What are they that do play it?

PHILOSTRATE.

Hard-handed men that work in Athens here, who never laboured in their minds till now; and now have toiled their unpractised memories with this same play against your nuptial.

THESEUS.

And we will hear it.

PHILOSTRATE.

No, my noble lord,

It is not for you. I have heard it all the way through, and it is nothing, nothing in the world—unless you can find amusement in their efforts, which are extremely strained and rehearsed with painful care to do you service.

THESEUS.

I will hear that play.

For nothing can ever be wrong

when simplicity and duty offer it.

Go, bring them in, and take your places, ladies.

[Exit Philostrate.]

HIPPOLYTA.

I do not like to see wretchedness overburdened, and duty perishing in its service.

THESEUS.

Why, gentle sweet, you shall see no such thing.

HIPPOLYTA.

He says they can do nothing in this kind.

THESEUS.

The kinder we are, then, to give them thanks for nothing. Our amusement shall be to accept what they get wrong. And what poor duty cannot accomplish, noble respect accepts for its effort, not its merit. Where I have gone, great scholars have intended to greet me with premeditated welcomes. Where I have seen them shiver and look pale, pause in the middle of sentences, choke on their practiced words in their fear, and in the end break off dumbly, not paying me a welcome. Trust me, sweet, out of this silence I still picked a welcome. And in the modesty of fearful duty I read as much as from the rattling tongue of bold and audacious eloquence. Love, therefore, and tongue-tied simplicity speak the most to my understanding.

Enter Philostrate.

PHILOSTRATE.

If it please your grace, the Prologue is ready.

THESEUS.

Let him come forward.

Flourish of trumpets. Enter the Prologue.

###CHAPTER### Prologue

If we offend, it is with our good will.

You should think that we come not to offend, but with good will. To show our simple skill, that is the true beginning of our end.

Consider then, we come but in spite.

We do not come as intending to please you. Our true intent is not for your delight. You should regret being here. The actors are at hand, and by their show, you shall know all that you are likely to know.

THESEUS.

This fellow does not stand upon points.

LYSANDER.

He has ridden his prologue like a rough colt; he does not know the stop. A good moral, my lord: it is not enough to speak, but to speak true.

HIPPOLYTA.

Indeed he has played on this prologue like a child on a recorder; a sound, but not in control.

THESEUS.

His speech was like a tangled chain; nothing broken, but all disordered. Who is next?

Enter Pyramus and Thisbe, Wall, Moonshine and Lion as in dumb show.

###CHAPTER### Prologue

Ladies and gentlemen, perhaps you are puzzled by this performance; but keep wondering until the truth makes everything clear. This man is Pyramus, if you want to know.

This beautiful lady is certainly Thisbe.

This man, with lime and rough plaster, plays the Wall—that vile wall which separated these lovers. And through the Wall's crack, poor souls, they are happy to whisper. Let no one be surprised at that. This man, with lantern, dog, and thorn bush, plays Moonshine. For, if you want to know, by moonlight these lovers thought it no shame to meet at Ninus's tomb—there, there to woo. This fearsome beast (whose name is Lion) scared away, or rather frightened, the faithful Thisbe when she came first at night.

And as she fled, she dropped her cloak, which the vile Lion stained with his bloody mouth. Soon comes Pyramus, a sweet and handsome youth, and finds his faithful Thisbe's cloak stained with blood. At that, with his blade—with a bloody, blameful blade—he bravely pierced his boiling, bloody breast. And Thisbe, waiting in the mulberry shade,

drew his dagger and died. For all the rest, let Lion, Moonshine, Wall, and the two lovers tell it fully while they remain here.

[Exeunt Prologue, Pyramus, Thisbe, Lion, and Moonshine.]

THESEUS.

I wonder if the lion is going to speak.

DEMETRIUS.

No wonder, my lord. One lion may speak when so many donkeys do.

WALL.

In this same play it happens

that I, one Snout by name, play a wall. And such a wall as I would have you think had a cracked hole or chink in it, through which the lovers, Pyramus and Thisbe, often whispered very secretly.

This clay, this rough plaster, and this stone show that I am that same wall. The truth is so. And this is the crack, right and left, through which the fearful lovers are to whisper.

THESEUS.

Could you ask lime and hair to speak better?

DEMETRIUS.

It is the cleverest partition I have ever heard speak, my lord.

THESEUS.

Pyramus approaches the wall. Silence.

Enter Pyramus.

PYRAMUS.

O grim-looking night! O night with a hue so black! O night, which always exists when day does not! O night, O night, alas, alas, alas,

I fear my Thisbe's promise is forgotten!

And you, O wall, O sweet, O lovely wall, that stands between her father's land and mine—you wall, O wall, O sweet and lovely wall, show me your chink to peer through with my eyes.

[Wall holds up his fingers.]

Thanks, courteous wall. May Jove protect you well for this! But what do I see? I see no Thisbe.

O wicked wall, through whom I see no joy, cursed be your stones for deceiving me like this!

THESEUS.

The wall, I think, being capable of feeling, should curse back.

PYRAMUS.

No, in truth, sir, he should not. "Deceiving me" is Thisbe's cue. She is supposed to enter now, and I am supposed to spot her through the wall. You will see it will happen exactly as I told you. Here she comes.

Enter Thisbe.

THISBE.

O wall, you have often heard my moans, for separating my fair Pyramus and me.

My cherry lips have often kissed your stones—your stones bound together with lime and hair.

PYRAMUS.

I see a voice. Now I will go to the chink,

to spy if I can hear my Thisbe's face.

Thisbe?

THISBE.

You are my love, I think.

PYRAMUS.

Think what you will, I am your lover's darling, and like Limander I am still faithful.

THISBE.

And I am like Helen, until the fates kill me.

PYRAMUS.

Not even Shafalus was so true to Procrus.

THISBE.

As Shafalus was to Procrus, so am I to you.

PYRAMUS.

O kiss me through the hole in this vile wall.

THISBE.

I kiss the wall's hole, not your lips at all.

PYRAMUS.

Will you meet me right away at Ninny's tomb?

THISBE.

Come life, come death, I will come without delay.

WALL.

So, Wall, I have played my part; and now that it is done, Wall goes away.

[_Exit Wall, Pyramus and Thisbe._]

THESEUS.

Now the wall between the two neighbours is down.

DEMETRIUS.

There is no help for it, my lord, when walls are so stubborn as to listen without warning.

HIPPOLYTA.

This is the silliest stuff I have ever heard.

THESEUS.

The best in this kind are only shadows; and the worst are no worse, if imagination improves them.

HIPPOLYTA.

Then it must be your imagination, and not theirs.

THESEUS.

If we imagine no worse of them than they imagine of themselves, they may pass for excellent men. Here come two noble beasts in, a man and a lion.

Enter Lion and Moonshine.

LION.

You, ladies, you whose gentle hearts fear the smallest monstrous mouse that creeps on the floor, may now perhaps both quake and tremble here, when a rough lion roars in wildest rage. Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am a fierce lion, and not a lioness;

For if I should come as a lion in anger

Into this place, it would be a pity for my life.

THESEUS.

A very gentle beast, and with a good conscience.

DEMETRIUS.

The very best at being a beast, my lord, that I ever saw.

LYSANDER.

This lion is a real fox for his valour.

THESEUS.

True; and a goose for his discretion.

DEMETRIUS.

Not so, my lord, for his valour cannot carry his discretion, and the fox carries the goose.

THESEUS.

His discretion, I am sure, cannot carry his valour; for the goose does not carry the fox. It is well; leave it to his discretion, and let us listen to the moon.

MOONSHINE.

This lantern represents the horned moon.

DEMETRIUS.

He should have worn the horns on his head.

THESEUS.

He is not a crescent, and his horns are invisible within the circle.

MOONSHINE.

This lantern represents the horned moon; I myself seem to be the man in the moon.

THESEUS.

This is the greatest error of all the rest; the man should be put inside the lantern. How else is he the man in the moon?

DEMETRIUS.

He dares not go in there because of the candle, for you see, it is already in need of snuffing.

HIPPOLYTA.

I am weary of this moon. I wish he would change!

THESEUS.

It appears by his small light of discretion that he is waning; but still, in courtesy, in all reason, we must wait out the time.

LYSANDER.

Proceed, Moon.

MOON.

All that I have to say is to tell you that the lantern is the moon; I am the man in the moon; this thorn-bush is my thorn-bush; and this dog is my dog.

DEMETRIUS.

Why, all these should be inside the lantern, for all these are in the moon. But silence; here comes Thisbe.

Enter Thisbe.

THISBE.

This is old Ninny's tomb. Where is my love?

LION.

Oh!

[_The Lion roars, Thisbe runs off._]

DEMETRIUS.

Well roared, Lion.

THESEUS.

Well run, Thisbe.

HIPPOLYTA.

Well shone, Moon. Truly, the moon shines with good grace.

[_The Lion tears Thisbe's cloak, and exits._]

THESEUS.

Well shaken, Lion.

DEMETRIUS.

And then came Pyramus.

LYSANDER.

And so the lion vanished.

Enter Pyramus.

PYRAMUS.

Sweet Moon, I thank you for your sunny beams; I thank you, Moon, for shining now so bright; for by your gracious golden, glittering gleams, I trust to see the truest Thisbe.

But wait! Oh spite!

But look, poor knight,

What dreadful sorrow is here!

Eyes, do you see?

How can it be?

O dainty duck! O dear!

Your cloak so good,

What, stained with blood?

Approach, you fierce Furies!

O Fates, come, come;

Cut thread and end;

Destroy, rush, conclude, and kill!

THESEUS.

This passion, and the death of a dear friend, would almost make a man look sad.

HIPPOLYTA.

Curse my heart, but I pity the man.

PYRAMUS.

Oh, Nature, why did you create lions? Since a vile lion has robbed my dear of her virginity here. Which is—no, no—which was the fairest lady that lived, that loved, that delighted, that looked with joy. Come, tears, overwhelm me!

Out, sword, and wound

The breast of Pyramus;

Yes, that left breast,

Where the heart beats:

Thus I die, thus, thus, thus.

Now I am dead,

Now I have departed;

My soul is in the sky.

Tongue, lose your light!

Moon, take your flight!

Now die, die, die, die, die.

[He dies. Exit Moonshine.]

DEMETRIUS.

No die, but an ace, for him; for he is but one.

LYSANDER.

Less than an ace, man; for he is dead, he is nothing.

THESEUS.

With the help of a surgeon he might yet recover and prove to be an ass.

HIPPOLYTA.

How is it that Moonshine is gone before Thisbe comes back and finds her lover?

THESEUS.

She will find him by starlight.

Enter Thisbe.

Here she comes, and her passion ends the play.

HIPPOLYTA.

I think she should not use a long one for such a Pyramus. I hope she will be brief.

DEMETRIUS.

A speck of dust will tip the balance as to which is better, Pyramus or Thisbe: he for a man, God protect us; she for a woman, God bless us!

LYSANDER.

She has already spotted him with those sweet eyes.

DEMETRIUS.

And thus she laments, namely—

THISBE.

Asleep, my love?

What, dead, my dove?

O Pyramus, arise,

Speak, speak. Quite dumb?

Dead, dead? A tomb

Must cover your sweet eyes.

These lily-white lips,

This cherry nose,

These yellow cowslip cheeks,

Are gone, are gone!

Lovers, make lament;

His eyes were green as leeks.

O Sisters Three,

Come, come to me,

With hands as pale as milk;

Lay them in blood,

Since you have cut

With shears his thread of silk. Tongue, not a word:

Come, trusty sword,

Come, blade, stain my breast with blood;

And farewell, friends.

Thus Thisbe ends.

Adieu, adieu, adieu.

[She dies.]

THESEUS.

Moonshine and Lion are left to bury the dead.

DEMETRIUS.

Yes, and Wall too.

BOTTOM.

No, I assure you; the wall is down that separated their fathers. Will it please you to see the epilogue, or to hear a Bergomask dance between two of our company?

THESEUS.

No epilogue, I beg you; for your play needs no excuse. Never make excuses; for when the players are all dead there is no one to blame. Indeed, if the one who wrote it had played Pyramus, and hanged himself in Thisbe's garter, it would have been a fine tragedy; and so it is, truly; and very notably performed. But come, your Bergomask; leave your epilogue alone.

[Here a dance of Clowns.]

The iron tongue of midnight has struck twelve. Lovers, to bed; it is almost fairy time.

I fear we shall sleep past the coming morning as much as we have stayed awake this night. This obviously clumsy play has well beguiled the slow passage of night. Sweet friends, to bed. We shall hold this celebration for a fortnight

In nightly revels and new merriment.

[They exit.]

Enter Puck.

PUCK.

Now the hungry lion roars,

And the wolf howls at the moon;

While the heavy ploughman snores,

All worn out with weary toil.

Now the burnt-out logs do glow,

While the screech-owl, screeching loud, Puts the wretch who lies in misery

In mind of a burial shroud.

Now it is the time of night

When the graves, all gaping wide,

Each one lets forth its spirit,

To glide along the churchyard paths.

And we fairies, who do run

By the triple Hecate's team

From the presence of the sun,

Following darkness like a dream,

Now are merry; not a mouse

Shall disturb this hallowed house. I am sent with broom before,

To sweep the dust behind the door.

Enter Oberon and Titania with their Train.

OBERON.

Through the house give glimmering light, By the dead and drowsy fire.

Every elf and fairy spirit

Hop as lightly as a bird from a briar, and follow me in this song.

Sing and dance it nimbly.

TITANIA.

First rehearse your song by heart,

With a warbling note for each word.

Hand in hand, with fairy grace,

We will sing and bless this place.

[Song and Dance.]

OBERON.

Now, until daybreak,

Let each fairy wander through this house.

We will go to the best bridal bed,

Which will be blessed by us;

And the children conceived there

Will always be fortunate.

So shall all three couples

Always be true in love;

And the flaws of nature

Will not appear in their children.

No mole, harelip, or scar,

Nor any unnatural mark, such as are

Looked down upon at birth,

Will be upon their children.

With this consecrated field-dew,

Let every fairy go on their way,

And bless each separate room,

Through this palace, with sweet peace; and blessed be the owner of it.

It will always rest in safety.

Trip away. Do not delay;

Meet me all at daybreak.

[Exeunt Oberon, Titania and Train.]

PUCK.

If we shadows have offended you,

Just think of this, and all is fixed:

That you have only been sleeping here

While these visions appeared.

And this weak and idle theme,

Yielding no more than a dream,

Gentle people, do not criticize.

If you pardon us, we will improve.

And, as I am an honest Puck,

If we have had undeserved luck

Now in escaping the serpent’s tongue,

We will make amends before long;

Otherwise, call Puck a liar.

So, good night to you all.

Give me your hands, if we are friends, and Robin will make amends.

[Exit.]
