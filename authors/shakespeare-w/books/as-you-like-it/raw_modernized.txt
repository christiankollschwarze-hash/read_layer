###TITLE### As You Like It
###AUTHOR### William Shakespeare

###CHAPTER### Act I

SCENE I. An Orchard near Oliver’s house

Enter Orlando and Adam.

ORLANDO.

As I remember, Adam, it was like this: my father left me only a poor thousand crowns in his will, and, as you say, he commanded my brother, on his blessing, to raise me well. And there begins my sadness. My brother Jaques he keeps at school, and everyone speaks highly of his progress. As for me, he keeps me at home like a peasant, or, to put it more accurately, he leaves me here at home with no proper care. For can you call it "keeping" for a gentleman of my birth, when it is no different from stalling an ox? His horses are raised better. Besides being well fed, they are taught their training, and riders are paid well to do it. But I, his brother, gain nothing under him except growth. His animals on his dunghills are as much indebted to him as I am for that. Besides this nothing that he so generously gives me, his attitude seems to take away the something that nature gave me. He lets me eat with his servants, denies me the place of a brother, and, as much as he can, undermines my gentility with my upbringing. This is what grieves me, Adam. And the spirit of my father, which I think lives within me, begins to rebel against this servitude. I will no longer endure it, though I do not yet know a wise way to avoid it.

Enter Oliver.

ADAM.

Here comes my master, your brother.

ORLANDO.

Step aside, Adam, and you will hear how he berates me.

[_Adam retires._]

OLIVER.

Now, sir, what are you doing here?

ORLANDO.

Nothing. I am not taught to make anything.

OLIVER.

What are you ruining then, sir?

ORLANDO.

Indeed, sir, I am helping you ruin what God made: a poor, unworthy brother of yours, through idleness.

OLIVER.

Indeed, sir, find better work, and be gone for a while.

ORLANDO.

Shall I keep your pigs and eat husks with them? What prodigal's portion have I spent that I should come to such poverty?

OLIVER.

Do you know where you are, sir?

ORLANDO.

Oh, sir, very well: here in your orchard.

OLIVER.

Do you know who you are before, sir?

ORLANDO.

Yes, better than the one I stand before knows me. I know you are my eldest brother, and by the gentle bond of blood you should know me as such. The custom of nations grants you superiority because you are the first-born. But that same tradition does not take away my blood, even if there were twenty brothers between us. I have as much of my father in me as you do, though I admit your being born before me makes you closer to his honor.

OLIVER.

What, boy!

ORLANDO.

Come, come, elder brother, you are too inexperienced in this.

OLIVER.

Will you lay hands on me, villain?

ORLANDO.

I am no villain. I am the youngest son of Sir Rowland de Boys. He was my father, and he is three times a villain who says such a father fathered villains. If you were not my brother, I would not take this hand from your throat until this other hand had pulled out your tongue for saying so. You have insulted yourself.

ADAM.

[_Coming forward._] Good masters, be patient. For the memory of your father, be at peace.

OLIVER.

Let me go, I say.

ORLANDO.

I will not until I choose to. You shall hear me. My father commanded you in his will to give me a good education. You have trained me like a peasant, obscuring and hiding from me all gentleman-like qualities. The spirit of my father grows strong in me, and I will no longer endure it. Therefore allow me such training as may suit a gentleman, or give me the poor inheritance my father left me in his will. With that I will go buy my fortunes.

OLIVER.

And what will you do? Beg when that is spent? Well, sir, go inside. I will not be troubled with you for long. You shall have some part of what you want. Please leave me.

ORLANDO.

I will not offend you more than is necessary for my own good.

OLIVER.

Go with him, you old dog.

ADAM.

Is “old dog” my reward? Most true, I have lost my teeth in your service. God be with my old master. He would not have spoken such a word.

[_Exeunt Orlando and Adam._]

OLIVER.

Is it even so? Are you beginning to grow upon me? I will cure your insolence, and yet give no thousand crowns either. Holla, Dennis!

Enter Dennis.

DENNIS

Did you call, sir?

OLIVER.

Was not Charles, the Duke’s wrestler, here to speak with me?

DENNIS.

Please, sir, he is here at the door and insists on seeing you.

OLIVER.

Call him in.

[_Exit Dennis._]

This will be a good way to handle it, and tomorrow is the wrestling.

Enter Charles.

CHARLES.

Good morning to your worship.

OLIVER.

Good Monsieur Charles. What is the new news at the new court?

CHARLES.

There is no news at the court, sir, but the old news. That is, the old Duke is banished by his younger brother the new Duke, and three or four loyal lords have gone into voluntary exile with him. Their lands and revenues enrich the new Duke, so he gladly lets them wander.

OLIVER.

Can you tell if Rosalind, the Duke’s daughter, is banished with her father?

CHARLES.

Oh, no. For the Duke’s daughter, her cousin, loves her so much—they have been raised together since their cradles—that she would have followed her into exile or died to stay behind her. She is at the court and is no less beloved by her uncle than his own daughter, and never have two ladies loved as they do.

OLIVER.

Where will the old Duke live?

CHARLES.

They say he is already in the Forest of Arden, and many merry men are with him. There they live like the old Robin Hood of England. They say many young gentlemen flock to him every day and pass the time carelessly, as they did in the golden world.

OLIVER.

What, you wrestle tomorrow before the new Duke?

CHARLES.

Indeed I do, sir, and I came to inform you of a matter. I am given to understand, sir, secretly, that your younger brother Orlando intends to come in disguise against me to try a fall. Tomorrow, sir, I wrestle for my reputation, and anyone who escapes me without some broken limb will have done well. Your brother is but young and tender, and for your love I would be reluctant to hurt him, as I must for my own honour if he comes in. Therefore, out of my love to you, I came here to inform you, so that either you might stop him from his intention, or accept such disgrace as he will run into, since it is a thing of his own seeking and altogether against my will.

OLIVER.

Charles, I thank you for your love to me, which you will find I will most kindly repay. I myself had notice of my brother’s purpose here, and have by underhand means tried to dissuade him from it, but he is resolute. I will tell you, Charles, he is the most stubborn young fellow in France, full of ambition, an envious rival of every man’s good qualities, a secret and villainous plotter against me, his own natural brother. Therefore use your discretion. I would as soon you broke his neck as his finger. And you had best look to it, for if you do him any slight disgrace, or if he does not greatly distinguish himself against you, he will plot against you by poison, entrap you by some treacherous device, and never leave you until he has taken your life by some indirect means or other. For I assure you—and almost with tears I speak it—there is not one so young and so villainous living today. I speak only brotherly of him, but should I lay him open to you as he truly is, I would have to blush and weep, and you would look pale and wonder.

CHARLES.

I am heartily glad I came here to you. If he comes tomorrow I will give him his payment. If he ever walks alone again I will never wrestle for a prize again. And so, God keep your worship.

[_Exit._]

OLIVER.

Farewell, good Charles. Now I will go stir up this gambler. I hope I will see the end of him, because my soul—though I do not know why—hates nothing more than him. Yet he is gentle, never formally educated and still learned, full of noble plans, loved enchantingly by all sorts of people, and indeed so much in the heart of the world, and especially of my own people, who know him best, that I am completely undervalued. But it will not be so for long; this wrestler will settle everything. Nothing remains but for me to encourage the boy to go there, which I will now do.

[Exit.]

SCENE II. A Lawn before the Duke's Palace

Enter Rosalind and Celia.

CELIA.

I beg you, Rosalind, my sweet cousin, be cheerful.

ROSALIND.

Dear Celia, I show more cheerfulness than I actually feel, and would you still have me be merrier? Unless you could teach me to forget a banished father, you must not teach me how to remember any extraordinary pleasure.

CELIA.

In this I see you do not love me with the full weight that I love you. If my uncle, your banished father, had banished your uncle, the Duke my father, so long as you were still with me, I could have taught my love to accept your father as my own. So would you, if the truth of your love for me were as rightly balanced as mine is for you.

ROSALIND.

Well, I will forget the condition of my situation to rejoice in yours.

CELIA.

You know my father has no child but me, nor is likely to have any; and truly, when he dies you will be his heir, for what he has taken away from your father by force, I will give back to you in affection. By my honor I will! And when I break that oath, let me turn into a monster. Therefore, my sweet Rose, my dear Rose, be cheerful.

ROSALIND.

From now on I will, cousin, and think up some amusements. Let me see—what do you think of falling in love?

CELIA.

Indeed, please do, to make sport of it; but love no man in earnest, nor any further in sport either than you can come away with honor and only a modest blush.

ROSALIND.

What shall our amusement be, then?

CELIA.

Let us sit and mock the good housewife Fortune from her wheel, so that her gifts may from now on be given out equally.

ROSALIND.

I wish we could do so, for her benefits are mightily misplaced, and the generous blind woman makes the most mistakes in her gifts to women.

CELIA.

It is true, for those she makes beautiful she scarcely makes virtuous, and those she makes virtuous she makes very unattractive.

ROSALIND.

No, now you are moving from Fortune's domain to Nature's. Fortune rules over worldly gifts, not over the features given by Nature.

Enter Touchstone.

CELIA.

No? When Nature has made a beautiful creature, may she not by Fortune fall into the fire? Though Nature has given us wit to mock Fortune, has not Fortune sent in this fool to cut off the argument?

ROSALIND.

Indeed, there is Fortune too hard for Nature, when Fortune makes Nature's fool the cutter-off of Nature's wit.

CELIA.

Perhaps this is not Fortune's work either, but Nature's, who sees our natural wits are too dull to reason about such goddesses, and has sent this fool as our whetstone; for always the dullness of the fool is the whetstone of the wits. How now, wit, where are you wandering?

TOUCHSTONE.

Mistress, you must come away to your father.

CELIA.

Were you made the messenger?

TOUCHSTONE.

No, by my honor, but I was told to come for you.

ROSALIND.

Where did you learn that oath, fool?

TOUCHSTONE.

There was a certain knight who swore by his honor that the pancakes were good, and swore by his honor that the mustard was bad. Now, I insist the pancakes were bad and the mustard was good, yet the knight was not lying under oath.

CELIA.

How do you prove that, with all your great knowledge?

ROSALIND.

Yes, go on, let your wisdom speak freely.

TOUCHSTONE.

Both of you step forward now. Stroke your chins and swear by your beards that I am a rascal.

CELIA.

By our beards, if we had them, you are.

TOUCHSTONE.

By my rascality, if I had it, then I would be. But if you swear by something that does not exist, you are not lying under oath. The knight was no more lying when he swore by his honor, because he never had any; or if he did have some, he had sworn it all away long before he ever saw those pancakes or that mustard.

CELIA.

Please, who is it that you mean?

TOUCHSTONE.

Someone that old Frederick, your father, loves.

CELIA.

My father's love is enough to give him honor. Enough! Say no more about him. You will be whipped for criticizing people one of these days.

TOUCHSTONE.

It is a pity that fools may not speak wisely about what wise men do foolishly.

CELIA.

By my faith, you speak the truth. For since the little wisdom that fools have was silenced, the little foolishness that wise men have makes a great display. Here comes Monsieur Le Beau.

Enter Le Beau.

ROSALIND.

With his mouth full of news.

CELIA.

Which he will force on us the way pigeons feed their young.

ROSALIND.

Then we shall be stuffed full of news.

CELIA.

All the better; we shall be more valuable at market. Good day, Monsieur Le Beau. What is the news?

LE BEAU.

Fair princess, you have missed much good entertainment.

CELIA.

Entertainment! Of what kind?

LE BEAU.

What kind, madam? How shall I answer you?

ROSALIND.

As your wit and luck allow.

TOUCHSTONE.

Or as the fates decide.

CELIA.

Well said. That was laid on thick.

TOUCHSTONE.

No, if I do not keep my rank—

ROSALIND.

You lose your old smell.

LE BEAU.

You amaze me, ladies. I would have told you about good wrestling, which you have missed seeing.

ROSALIND.

Tell us anyway how the wrestling went.

LE BEAU.

I will tell you the beginning, and if it pleases you ladies, you may see the end, for the best is yet to come. And here, where you are, they are coming to perform it.

CELIA.

Well, the beginning is dead and buried.

LE BEAU.

An old man comes with his three sons—

CELIA.

I could match this beginning with an old tale.

LE BEAU.

Three fine young men of excellent build and presence.

ROSALIND.

With notices on their necks: "Be it known unto all men by these presents."

LE BEAU.

The eldest of the three wrestled with Charles, the Duke's wrestler. Charles threw him in a moment and broke three of his ribs, so there is little hope of life in him. He did the same to the second, and so to the third. Over there they lie, with the poor old man their father making such pitiful mourning over them that all the onlookers weep with him.

ROSALIND.

Alas!

TOUCHSTONE.

But what is the entertainment, monsieur, that the ladies have missed?

LE BEAU.

Why, this that I am speaking of.

TOUCHSTONE.

So men may grow wiser every day. This is the first time I ever heard that breaking ribs was entertainment for ladies.

CELIA.

Or I, I promise you.

ROSALIND.

But is there anyone else who longs to hear this broken music in his sides? Is there yet another who dotes on rib-breaking? Shall we see this wrestling, cousin?

LE BEAU.

You must if you stay here, for this is the place appointed for the wrestling, and they are ready to perform it.

CELIA.

Over there, surely, they are coming. Let us now stay and see it.

Flourish. Enter Duke Frederick, Lords, Orlando, Charles and Attendants.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Come on. Since the young man will not be persuaded, let the danger be on his own boldness.

ROSALIND.

Is that the man over there?

LE BEAU.

That is he, madam.

CELIA.

Alas, he is too young. Yet he looks like he might succeed.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Well now, daughter and cousin? Have you crept here to see the wrestling?

ROSALIND.

Yes, my lord, if you will give us permission.

DUKE FREDERICK.

You will take little pleasure in it, I can tell you, there is such a mismatch between the men. Out of pity for the challenger's youth I would gladly discourage him, but he will not be persuaded. Speak to him, ladies; see if you can change his mind.

CELIA.

Call him over here, good Monsieur Le Beau.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Do so; I will not be present.

[Duke Frederick steps aside.]

LE BEAU.

Monsieur the challenger, the Princess calls for you.

ORLANDO.

I attend them with all respect and duty.

ROSALIND.

Young man, have you challenged Charles the wrestler?

ORLANDO.

No, fair princess. He is the general challenger. I come only as others do, to test the strength of my youth against him.

CELIA.

Young gentleman, your spirits are too bold for your years. You have seen cruel proof of this man's strength. If you saw yourself with your own eyes or knew yourself with your own judgment, the fear of your undertaking would advise you to a more equal contest. We beg you, for your own sake, to embrace your own safety and give up this attempt.

ROSALIND.

Do, young sir. Your reputation shall not therefore be undervalued. We will make it our request to the Duke that the wrestling might not go forward.

ORLANDO.

I beg you, do not punish me with your harsh thoughts, though I confess I am much guilty for denying such fair and excellent ladies anything. But let your fair eyes and gentle wishes go with me to my trial. If I am defeated, there is only one shamed who was never favored; if killed, only one dead who is willing to be so. I shall do my friends no wrong, for I have none to mourn me; the world no injury, for in it I have nothing. Only in the world I fill up a place, which may be better supplied when I have made it empty.

ROSALIND.

The little strength that I have, I wish it were with you.

CELIA.

And mine to add to hers.

ROSALIND.

Fare you well. I pray heaven I am mistaken about you.

CELIA.

May your heart's desires be with you.

CHARLES.

Come, where is this young gallant who is so eager to lie with his mother earth?

ORLANDO.

Ready, sir; but his intention has a more modest purpose.

DUKE FREDERICK.

You shall attempt only one fall.

CHARLES.

No, I assure your grace, you will not need to persuade him to a second, since you have so strongly persuaded him from a first.

ORLANDO.

You mean to mock me afterward; you should not have mocked me before. But come on.

ROSALIND.

Now, Hercules give you speed, young man!

CELIA.

I wish I were invisible, so I could catch the strong fellow by the leg.

[Orlando and Charles wrestle.]

ROSALIND.

O excellent young man!

CELIA.

If I had a thunderbolt in my eye, I can tell who would go down.

[Charles is thrown. Shout.]

DUKE FREDERICK.

No more, no more.

ORLANDO.

Yes, I beg your grace. I am not yet well winded.

DUKE FREDERICK.

How are you, Charles?

LE BEAU.

He cannot speak, my lord.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Carry him away.

[Charles is carried off by Attendants.]

What is your name, young man?

ORLANDO.

Orlando, my lord, the youngest son of Sir Rowland de Boys.

DUKE FREDERICK.

I wish you had been son to some other man. The world esteemed your father honorable,

But I found him always my enemy.

You would have better pleased me with this deed had you come from another family.

But goodbye to you. You are a fine young man. I wish you had told me you had a different father.

[_Duke Frederick, Le Beau, and Lords exit._]

CELIA.

Cousin, if I were my father, would I do this?

ORLANDO.

I am prouder to be Sir Rowland’s son,

His youngest son, and would not trade that title to be Frederick’s adopted heir.

ROSALIND.

My father loved Sir Rowland as his own soul,

And everyone else felt the same way my father did.

If I had known beforehand that this young man was his son,

I would have begged him with tears before he had risked himself like this.

CELIA.

Gentle cousin,

Let us go thank him and encourage him.

My father’s harsh and jealous nature

Stabs me at heart.—Sir, you have earned this well. If you keep your promises in love

As faithfully as you have already done more than you promised,

Your lady will be happy.

ROSALIND.

Sir,

[_Giving him a chain from her neck._]

Wear this for me—one who is out of favor with Fortune, who would give more but has no means to do so.—Shall we go, cousin?

CELIA.

Yes.—Farewell, good gentleman.

ORLANDO.

Can I not even say, thank you? My better qualities

Are all thrown down, and what stands here is just a practice dummy, a mere lifeless block.

ROSALIND.

He calls us back. My pride fell along with my fortunes. I will ask him what he wants.—Did you call, sir?—Sir, you have wrestled well and defeated

More than your enemies.

CELIA.

Will you come, cousin?

ROSALIND.

I am coming with you.—Farewell.

[_Rosalind and Celia exit._]

ORLANDO.

What feeling hangs these weights upon my tongue? I cannot speak to her, yet she invited conversation. O poor Orlando, you are defeated.

Either Charles or something weaker overcomes you.

Enter Le Beau.

LE BEAU.

Good sir, as a friend I advise you

To leave this place. Although you have earned high praise, true applause, and love,

The Duke is now in such a mood

That he misinterprets everything you have done.

The Duke is temperamental; what he really is

is easier for you to imagine than for me to describe.

ORLANDO.

I thank you, sir; and please tell me this:

Which of the two was the Duke’s daughter

Who was here at the wrestling?

LE BEAU.

Neither is his daughter, if we judge by manners, but in fact the smaller one is his daughter.

The other is the daughter of the banished Duke,

And she is kept here by her usurping uncle

To keep his daughter company. Their affection for each other

Is stronger than the natural bond of sisters.

But I can tell you that recently this Duke

Has taken a dislike to his gentle niece, based on no other reason

Except that the people praise her for her virtues and pity her for her good father’s sake;

And, I swear on my life, his malice against the lady

Will soon break out. Sir, farewell. In the future, in a better world than this,

I hope to know you better and earn more of your friendship.

ORLANDO.

I am much obliged to you; farewell!

[_Le Beau exits._]

So I must go from the smoke into the fire,

From a tyrant Duke to a tyrant brother.

But heavenly Rosalind!

[_He exits._]

SCENE III. A Room in the Palace

Enter Celia and Rosalind.

CELIA.

Why, cousin, why, Rosalind! Cupid have mercy! Not a word?

ROSALIND.

Not one to throw at a dog.

CELIA.

No, your words are too precious to be wasted on dogs. Throw some of them at me. Come, cripple me with your arguments.

ROSALIND.

Then there would be two cousins laid up—one crippled by arguments and the other mad without any.

CELIA.

But is all this for your father?

ROSALIND.

No, some of it is for my child’s father. Oh, how full of thorns this working-day world is!

CELIA.

They are just burrs, cousin, thrown on you in holiday fun. If we do not walk on the beaten paths, our very petticoats will catch them.

ROSALIND.

I could shake them off my coat; these burrs are in my heart.

CELIA.

Cough them away.

ROSALIND.

I would try, if I could cough and have him.

CELIA.

Come, come, wrestle with your feelings.

ROSALIND.

Oh, they take the side of a better wrestler than myself.

CELIA.

Oh, a good wish upon you! You will try in time, in spite of a fall. But putting these jokes aside, let us talk seriously. Is it possible that so suddenly you should fall into such a strong liking for old Sir Rowland’s youngest son?

ROSALIND.

The Duke my father loved his father dearly.

CELIA.

Does it therefore follow that you should love his son dearly? By that kind of reasoning I should hate him, for my father hated his father dearly; yet I do not hate Orlando.

ROSALIND.

No, truly, do not hate him, for my sake.

CELIA.

Why should I not? Does he not deserve well?

Enter Duke Frederick with Lords.

ROSALIND.

Let me love him for that, and you love him because I do.—Look, here comes the Duke.

CELIA.

With his eyes full of anger.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Mistress, get yourself away with your fastest haste, and leave our court.

ROSALIND.

Me, uncle?

DUKE FREDERICK.

You, cousin.

Within these ten days, if you are found within twenty miles of our public court,

You die for it.

ROSALIND.

I beg your Grace,

Let me know what my fault is. If I understand myself,

Or know my own desires,

If I am not dreaming, or not insane—

As I trust I am not—then, dear uncle,

Never so much as in a thought not yet born

Did I offend your Highness.

DUKE FREDERICK.

All traitors speak this way.

If their innocence could be proven with words,

They are as innocent as grace itself.

Let it be enough for you that I do not trust you.

ROSALIND.

Yet your mistrust cannot make me a traitor.

Tell me what this suspicion is based on.

DUKE FREDERICK.

You are your father’s daughter, that is enough.

ROSALIND.

So I was when your highness took his dukedom; so I was when your highness banished him.

Treason is not inherited, my lord,

Or, if we did inherit it from our relatives,

What is that to me? My father was no traitor.

Then, good my liege, do not mistake me so much

As to think my poverty is treacherous.

CELIA.

Dear sovereign, hear me speak.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Yes, Celia, we kept her here for your sake,

Otherwise she would have wandered with her father.

CELIA.

I did not ask you then to let her stay;

It was your pleasure and your own pity.

I was too young at that time to value her,

But now I know her. If she is a traitor,

Why, so am I. We have always slept together,

Woken at the same moment, studied, played, eaten together, and wherever we went, like Juno’s swans,

We always went paired and inseparable.

DUKE FREDERICK.

She is too cunning for you, and her smoothness, her very silence, and her patience

Speak to the people, and they pity her.

You are a fool. She robs you of your reputation,

And you will seem more bright and more virtuous when she is gone. So do not open your lips.

Firm and irreversible is my judgment

Which I have passed upon her. She is banished.

CELIA.

Pronounce that sentence then on me, my liege. I cannot live out of her company.

DUKE FREDERICK.

You are a fool. You, niece, prepare yourself. If you stay past the time, upon my honour

And by the power of my word, you die.

[_Exeunt Duke Frederick and Lords._]

CELIA.

Oh my poor Rosalind, where will you go?

Will you change fathers? I will give you mine. I ask you, do not be more grieved than I am.

ROSALIND.

I have more reason to be.

CELIA.

You have not, cousin.

Please be cheerful. Do you not know the Duke has banished me, his daughter?

ROSALIND.

That he has not.

CELIA.

No, has he not? Then Rosalind lacks the love

that teaches you that you and I are one.

Shall we be separated? Shall we part, sweet girl? No, let my father find another heir.

So plan with me how we may flee,

where to go, and what to take with us,

and do not try to take your change upon yourself, to bear your griefs alone and leave me out. For, by this heaven, now pale at our sorrows, say what you can, I will go along with you.

ROSALIND.

Why, where shall we go?

CELIA.

To seek my uncle in the Forest of Arden.

ROSALIND.

Alas, what danger will it be for us,

maids as we are, to travel so far?

Beauty attracts thieves sooner than gold.

CELIA.

I will put myself in poor and humble clothes,

and with a kind of brown stain smear my face.

You do the same; so we shall pass along

and never stir up attackers.

ROSALIND.

Would it not be better,

because I am taller than common,

that I dress myself in all points like a man?

A fine short sword upon my thigh,

a boar-spear in my hand, and in my heart—

let whatever hidden woman’s fear lies there remain—we will have a swaggering and warlike outside,

as many other mannish cowards have

who brazen it out with their appearances.

CELIA.

What shall I call you when you are a man?

ROSALIND.

I will have no worse a name than Jove’s own page, and therefore see that you call me Ganymede.

But what will you be called?

CELIA.

Something that has a reference to my state:

no longer Celia, but Aliena.

ROSALIND.

But, cousin, what if we tried to steal

the clownish fool out of your father’s court? Would he not be a comfort on our journey?

CELIA.

He will go along over the wide world with me.

Leave me alone to persuade him. Let us go,

and gather our jewels and our wealth together,

decide the best time and safest way

to hide us from the pursuit that will be made

after my flight. Now we go in contentment

to liberty, and not to banishment.

[Exeunt.]

###CHAPTER### Act Ii

SCENE I. The Forest of Arden

Enter Duke Senior, Amiens and two or three Lords, dressed as foresters.

DUKE SENIOR.

Now, my companions and brothers in exile,

Has not old habit made this life more pleasant than that of artificial pomp? Are not these woods more free from danger than the jealous court?

Here we feel only the penalty of Adam,

The changing of the seasons, as the icy bite

And harsh scolding of the winter's wind,

Which when it bites and blows upon my body

Even until I shrink with cold, I smile and say: "This is no flattery. These are advisers

That feelingly persuade me what I am."

Sweet are the uses of hardship,

Which, like the toad, ugly and poisonous,

Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;

And this our life, free from public crowds,

Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks, sermons in stones, and good in everything.

AMIENS.

I would not change it. Happy is your grace,

That can translate the stubbornness of fortune into so quiet and so sweet a style.

DUKE SENIOR.

Come, shall we go and kill us some venison?

And yet it troubles me that the poor dappled fools,

Being native citizens of this deserted place,

Should in their own territory with forked arrows have their round haunches wounded.

FIRST LORD.

Indeed, my lord,

The melancholy Jaques grieves at that,

And in that regard swears you do more wrong

Than does your brother that has banished you. Today my lord of Amiens and myself

Did steal behind him as he lay stretched out

Under an oak, whose ancient root peeps out

Upon the brook that babbles along this wood;

To which place a poor isolated stag,

That from the hunter's aim had taken a wound,

Did come to languish; and indeed, my lord,

The wretched animal heaved forth such groans

That their release did stretch his leathery hide almost to bursting, and the big round tears

Chased one another down his innocent nose

In pitiful succession. And thus the hairy fool,

Much observed by the melancholy Jaques,

Stood on the very edge of the swift brook, adding to it with his tears.

DUKE SENIOR.

But what said Jaques?

Did he not moralize this spectacle?

FIRST LORD.

O yes, into a thousand comparisons.

First, for his weeping into the stream that had no need of more water: "Poor deer," he said, "you make a will as worldly people do, giving your abundance of more

To that which had too much already." Then, being there alone, left and abandoned by his velvet-covered friends:

"It is right," he said, "thus misery parts the flow of company." Soon a careless herd,

Full from the pasture, jumps along by him

And never stops to greet him. "Yes," said Jaques, "Sweep on, you fat and greasy citizens!

It is just the fashion. Why do you look

Upon that poor and broken bankrupt there?"

Thus most bitterly he pierces through

The body of the country, city, court,

Yes, and of this our life, swearing that we

Are mere usurpers, tyrants, and what's worse, to frighten the animals and to kill them

In their assigned and native dwelling-place.

DUKE SENIOR.

And did you leave him in this contemplation?

SECOND LORD.

We did, my lord, weeping and commenting

Upon the sobbing deer.

DUKE SENIOR.

Show me the place.

I love to confront him in these sullen moods,

For then he's full of substance.

FIRST LORD.

I'll bring you to him right away.

[Exeunt.]

SCENE II. A Room in the Palace

Enter Duke Frederick with Lords.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Can it be possible that no man saw them?

It cannot be! Some villains of my court

Are of consent and allowance in this.

FIRST LORD.

I cannot hear of any that did see her.

The ladies, her attendants of her chamber,

Saw her in bed, and in the morning early

They found the bed emptied of their mistress.

SECOND LORD.

My lord, the rough clown you so often used to laugh at is also missing.

Hesperia, the princess’s gentlewoman, confesses that she secretly overheard your daughter and her cousin praising the skills and charms of the wrestler who recently defeated the muscular Charles.

[85]

Your daughter and her cousin much commend

[87]

That did but lately foil the sinewy Charles;

And she believes that wherever they have gone, that young man is surely with them.

[90] DUKE FREDERICK.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Send for his brother; bring that fine fellow here. If he is absent, bring his brother to me.

I’ll make him find him. Do this at once!

And do not let any search or inquiry fail to bring back these foolish runaways.

[95] [They exit.]

[_Exeunt._]

SCENE III. Before Oliver’s House

Enter Orlando and Adam, meeting.

ORLANDO.

Who’s there?

ADAM.

What, my young master? O my gentle master,

O my sweet master, O you living reminder of old Sir Rowland! Why, what are you doing here?

[104] Why are you virtuous? Why do people love you? And why are you gentle, strong, and valiant? Why were you so foolish as to overcome the strong wrestler of the moody Duke?

Why are you virtuous? Why do people love you? And wherefore are you gentle, strong, and valiant? Why would you be so fond to overcome

[106] Your praise has come home too quickly before you. Do you not know, master, that to some kinds of men their good qualities work only as enemies?

Your praise is come too swiftly home before you. Know you not, master, to some kind of men

[108] No more do yours. Your virtues, gentle master, are holy traitors to you.

No more do yours. Your virtues, gentle master, Are sanctified and holy traitors to you.

O, what a world this is, when what is admirable poisons the person who has it!

[111] ORLANDO.

ORLANDO.

Why, what’s the matter?

ADAM.

O unhappy youth, do not come within these doors! Within this roof lives the enemy of all your good qualities.

[116] Your brother—no, no brother, yet the son—

Your brother—no, no brother, yet the son—

Yet not the son; I will not call him son—

Of him I was about to call his father, has heard your praises, and tonight he intends to burn the lodging where you usually sleep, and you inside it.

[120] If he fails at that, he will find other ways to kill you.

And you within it. If he fail of that,

[122] I overheard him and his schemes.

I overheard him and his practices.

This is no place for you; this house is only a slaughterhouse. Hate it, fear it, do not enter it.

ORLANDO.

Why, Adam, where would you have me go?

ADAM.

It does not matter where, as long as you do not come here.

ORLANDO.

What, would you have me go and beg for food, or with a low and violent sword force a thieving living on the public road?

[131] This I must do, or I do not know what to do.

This I must do, or know not what to do.

Yet this I will not do, no matter how I manage.

I would rather submit myself to the malice of a bloodline turned aside and a bloody brother.

[135] ADAM.

ADAM.

But do not do so. I have five hundred crowns, the thrifty wages I saved under your father, which I stored up to be my support when service should lie lame in my old limbs and neglected age be thrown into corners.

[138]

Which I did store to be my foster-nurse,

[140] Take that, and may He who feeds the ravens, yes, and providently provides for the sparrow, be comfort to my age. Here is the gold.

Take that, and He that doth the ravens feed,

[142] All this I give you. Let me be your servant.

Be comfort to my age. Here is the gold.

Though I look old, yet I am strong and vigorous, for in my youth I never applied hot and rebellious liquors to my blood, nor did I with shameless face pursue the means of weakness and debility.

[145]

Hot and rebellious liquors in my blood,

[147] Therefore my age is like a vigorous winter, frosty but kind. Let me go with you.

The means of weakness and debility.

[149] I’ll do the service of a younger man in all your business and needs.

Frosty but kindly. Let me go with you.

[151] ORLANDO.

In all your business and necessities.

O good old man, how well in you appears the faithful service of the old world, when service worked hard for duty, not for reward.

[154]

The constant service of the antique world,

You are not made for the fashion of these times, where no one will work hard except for promotion, and having gotten it they choke their service up even with the having.

[157]

Where none will sweat but for promotion,

It is not so with you. But, poor old man, you are pruning a rotten tree that cannot yield so much as a blossom in return for all your pains and care.

[160] But come along, we’ll go together, and before we have spent your youthful wages we’ll find some settled humble contentment.

In lieu of all thy pains and husbandry.

[162]

And ere we have thy youthful wages spent

ADAM.

Master, go on, and I will follow you

To the last breath with truth and loyalty.

From seventeen years old until now, almost eighty, I have lived here, but now I live here no more.

At seventeen years old, many people go to seek their fortunes,

But at eighty it is too late by a week.

Yet fortune cannot repay me better

Than to let me die well and not be my master's debtor.

[They exit.]

SCENE IV. The Forest of Arden

Enter Rosalind as Ganymede, Celia as Aliena, and Touchstone.

ROSALIND.

O Jupiter, how weary my spirits are!

TOUCHSTONE.

I would not care about my spirits, if only my legs were not weary.

ROSALIND.

I could find it in my heart to disgrace this man's clothing and cry like a woman, but I must comfort the weaker sex, since a doublet and hose should show themselves brave before a petticoat. Therefore, courage, good Aliena.

CELIA.

Please bear with me; I cannot go any further.

TOUCHSTONE.

For my part, I would rather bear with you than carry you. Yet I would not be bearing any cross if I did carry you, for I think you have no money in your purse.

ROSALIND.

Well, this is the Forest of Arden.

TOUCHSTONE.

Yes, now I am in Arden, the bigger fool I am! When I was at home I was in a better place, but travelers must be content.

Enter Corin and Silvius.

ROSALIND.

Yes, be so, good Touchstone. Look, who comes here? A young man and an old one in serious conversation.

CORIN.

That is the way to make her scorn you still.

SILVIUS.

O Corin, if only you knew how much I love her!

CORIN.

I partly guess, for I have loved before now.

SILVIUS.

No, Corin, being old, you cannot guess,

Though in your youth you were as true a lover as ever sighed upon a midnight pillow.

But if your love were ever like mine—

As I am sure no man ever loved so—

How many most ridiculous actions

Have you been drawn into by your imagination?

CORIN.

Into a thousand that I have forgotten.

SILVIUS.

Oh, then you never loved so deeply!

If you do not remember the slightest foolishness

That love ever made you run into,

You have not loved.

Or if you have not sat as I do now,

Tiring your listener with praise of your mistress,

You have not loved.

Or if you have not broken away from company

Abruptly, as my passion now makes me do,

You have not loved.

O Phoebe, Phoebe, Phoebe!

[Silvius exits.]

ROSALIND.

Alas, poor shepherd, searching for your wound,

I have by hard adventure found my own.

TOUCHSTONE.

And I mine. I remember when I was in love I broke my sword upon a stone and told him to take that for coming at night to Jane Smile. And I remember kissing her laundry bat, and the cow's udders that her pretty chapped hands had milked. And I remember courting a pea pod instead of her. I took two peas from it, and giving them back to her, said with weeping tears, "Wear these for my sake." We true lovers run into strange antics. But as everything is mortal in nature, so all nature in love is mortal in folly.

ROSALIND.

You speak more wisely than you are aware of.

TOUCHSTONE.

No, I shall never be aware of my own wit until I break my shins against it.

ROSALIND.

Jove, Jove, this shepherd's passion

Is very much like my own.

TOUCHSTONE.

And mine, but it has grown somewhat stale with me.

CELIA.

Please, one of you ask that man over there

If he will give us any food for money.

I am fainting almost to death.

TOUCHSTONE.

Hello there, you clown!

ROSALIND.

Peace, fool, he is not your relative.

CORIN.

Who calls?

TOUCHSTONE.

Your betters, sir.

CORIN.

Otherwise they are very wretched.

ROSALIND.

Peace, I say. Good evening to you, friend.

CORIN.

And to you, gentle sir, and to you all.

ROSALIND.

Please, shepherd, if love or gold

Can in this deserted place buy us hospitality,

Take us somewhere we can rest and eat. Here is a young woman, exhausted from travel, who is faint and needs help.

CORIN.

Good sir, I pity her.

And I wish, for her sake more than for my own, that I had the means to help her.

But I am a shepherd for another man,

And I do not shear the sheep I tend.

My master is a grumpy man

And cares little about finding the way to heaven

By doing kind and generous deeds.

Besides, his cottage, his flocks, and his pasture land are now for sale, and at our sheepfold right now,

Because he is away, there is nothing

For you to eat. But whatever there is, come and see, and I will welcome you as warmly as I can.

ROSALIND.

Who is the man who will buy his flock and pasture?

CORIN.

That young fellow you saw here just a little while ago, who cares little about buying anything.

ROSALIND.

Please, if it is honest to do so,

You buy the cottage, pasture, and the flock, and we will give you the money to pay for it.

CELIA.

And we will increase your wages. I like this place, and I could happily spend my time here.

CORIN.

Certainly the property is for sale.

Come with me. If, after hearing about it, you like

The land, the profit, and this kind of life,

I will be your very faithful shepherd,

And buy it with your gold right away.

[They exit.]

SCENE V. Another part of the Forest

Enter Amiens, Jaques and others.

AMIENS.

[Sings.]

Under the greenwood tree,

Who loves to lie with me

And turn his merry note

Unto the sweet bird’s throat,

Come here, come here, come here!

Here shall he see

No enemy

But winter and rough weather.

JAQUES.

More, more, please, more.

AMIENS.

It will make you melancholy, Monsieur Jaques.

JAQUES.

I welcome that. More, please, more. I can suck melancholy out of a song like a weasel sucks eggs. More, please, more.

AMIENS.

My voice is rough. I know I cannot please you.

JAQUES.

I do not want you to please me; I want you to sing. Come, more, another verse. Do you call them verses?

AMIENS.

Whatever you like, Monsieur Jaques.

JAQUES.

No, I do not care about their names. They owe me nothing. Will you sing?

AMIENS.

More at your request than to please myself.

JAQUES.

Well then, if I ever thank any man, I will thank you; but what people call compliments are like the meeting of two baboons. And when a man thanks me heartily, it seems to me that I have given him a penny and he pays me back with beggarly thanks. Come, sing; and those of you who will not sing, hold your tongues.

AMIENS.

Well, I will finish the song. Sirs, set the table meanwhile. The Duke will drink under this tree; he has been looking for you all day.

JAQUES.

And I have been avoiding him all day. He is too argumentative for my company. I think about as many things as he does, but I thank heaven for them and do not boast. Come, warble, come.

AMIENS.

[Sings.]

Who doth ambition shun

And loves to live in the sun,

Seeking the food he eats

And pleased with what he gets,

Come here, come here, come here.

Here shall he see

No enemy

But winter and rough weather.

JAQUES.

I will give you a verse to this tune that I made up yesterday, in spite of my lack of creativity.

AMIENS.

And I will sing it.

JAQUES.

It goes like this:

If it should come to pass

That any man turns into an ass,

Leaving his wealth and ease

To please a stubborn will,

Ducdame, ducdame, ducdame;

Here shall he see

Gross fools like he,

If he will come to me.

AMIENS.

What is that “ducdame?”

JAQUES.

It is a Greek spell to call fools into a circle. I will go sleep if I can; if I cannot, I will curse all the first-born of Egypt.

AMIENS.

And I will go find the Duke; his banquet is prepared.

[They exit separately.]

SCENE VI. Another part of the Forest

Enter Orlando and Adam.

ADAM.

Dear master, I can go no further. Oh, I am dying of hunger! I will lie down here and measure out my grave. Farewell, kind master.

ORLANDO.

Why, what is this, Adam? Is there no greater courage in you? Live a little, take comfort a little, cheer yourself a little. If this wild forest produces anything savage, I will either become food for it or bring it as food for you. Your imagination is closer to death than your strength is. For my sake, be comforted. Keep death at arm's length for a while. I will be with you soon, and if I do not bring you something to eat, I will give you permission to die. But if you die before I come back, you are mocking my efforts. Well said, you look cheerful, and I will be with you quickly. Yet you are lying in the cold air. Come, I will carry you to some shelter, and you shall not die for lack of a dinner if anything alive exists in this wilderness. Cheer up, good Adam!

[They exit.]

SCENE VII. Another part of the Forest

Enter Duke Senior, Amiens and Lords as outlaws.

DUKE SENIOR.

I think he has been transformed into a beast,

For I can find him nowhere in human form.

FIRST LORD.

My lord, he left here only a moment ago;

He was cheerful here, listening to a song.

DUKE SENIOR.

If he, who is made of discord, becomes musical,

We shall soon have chaos in the heavens. Go find him, and tell him I would like to speak with him.

Enter Jaques.

FIRST LORD.

He saves me the trouble by coming himself.

DUKE SENIOR.

Why, what is this, monsieur? What kind of life is this

That your poor friends must beg for your company? What, you look cheerful.

JAQUES.

A fool, a fool! I met a fool in the forest,

A motley fool. A miserable world!

As surely as I live by food, I met a fool,

Who lay down and basked in the sun,

And railed against Lady Fortune in fine words,

In fine, formal words, and yet a motley fool.

"Good morning, fool," I said. "No, sir," he said, "Do not call me fool until heaven has sent me fortune." And then he drew a sundial from his pocket,

And, looking at it with a dull eye,

Said very wisely, "It is ten o'clock.

Thus we may see," he said, "how the world goes. It is only an hour since it was nine,

And after one more hour it will be eleven.

And so from hour to hour we ripen and ripen,

And then from hour to hour we rot and rot,

And thereby hangs a tale." When I heard

The motley fool moralize like this about time,

My lungs began to crow like a rooster,

That fools should be so deeply thoughtful,

And I laughed without stopping

For an hour by his dial. O noble fool!

A worthy fool! Motley is the only thing to wear.

DUKE SENIOR.

What fool is this?

JAQUES.

O worthy fool!—One who has been a courtier, and says that if ladies are only young and fair,

They have the gift to know it. And in his brain, which is as dry as the leftover biscuit

After a voyage, he has strange places crammed with observations, which he lets out

In mangled forms. O that I were a fool!

I am ambitious for a motley coat.

DUKE SENIOR.

You shall have one.

JAQUES.

It is my only request,

Provided that you remove from your better judgments all opinion that grows wildly in them

That I am wise. I must have freedom

As well, as broad a license as the wind,

To blow on whomever I please, for that is what fools have.

And those who are most hurt by my folly,

They most must laugh. And why, sir, must they do so? The "why" is as plain as the way to the parish church.

He whom a fool hits very wisely

Acts very foolishly, although he feels the sting,

If he does not seem unaware of the jab. If not,

The wise man's folly is laid open

Even by the random glances of the fool.

Dress me in my motley. Give me permission

To speak my mind, and I will thoroughly cleanse the foul body of the infected world,

If they will patiently accept my medicine.

DUKE SENIOR.

Shame on you! I know what you would do.

JAQUES.

What, for a mere token, would I do anything but good?

DUKE SENIOR.

The most mischievous foul sin is in scolding sin itself,

For you yourself have been a libertine,

As sensual as the brutish sting itself,

And all the swollen sores and deep-rooted evils

That you have caught with the license of free living, you would pour out into the whole world.

JAQUES.

Why, who cries out against pride

That can accuse any private person by it?

Does it not flow as hugely as the sea

Until the weary means themselves run dry?

What woman in the city do I name

When I say that the city woman bears

The cost of princes on unworthy shoulders?

Who can come forward and say that I mean her,

When such a one as she is just like her neighbor? Or what man of the lowest rank

Can say his fine clothes are not bought at my expense,

Thinking that I mean him, but in doing so he matches

His foolishness to the substance of my speech?

There, then. How then, what then? Let me see where  
My tongue has wronged him. If it speaks the truth about him,  
Then he has wronged himself. If he is innocent,

Why then my accusation flies like a wild goose

Unclaimed by any man. But who comes here?

Enter Orlando with sword drawn.

ORLANDO.

Stop, and eat no more.

JAQUES.

Why, I have eaten none yet.

ORLANDO.

Nor shall you, until necessity is served.

JAQUES.

What kind of rooster could this one come from?

DUKE SENIOR.

Are you made this bold, man, by your distress? Or are you a rude despiser of good manners,

That you seem so empty of civility?

ORLANDO.

You touched my true condition at first. The thorny point  
Of bare distress has taken from me the appearance

Of smooth civility; yet I was raised in civilized lands

And know some good breeding. But stop, I say!

He dies who touches any of this fruit

Until I and my affairs are answered.

JAQUES.

If you will not be answered with reason, I must die.

DUKE SENIOR.

What would you have? Your gentleness shall force  
More than your force move us to gentleness.

ORLANDO.

I am almost dying for food, so let me have it.

DUKE SENIOR.

Sit down and eat, and welcome to our table.

ORLANDO.

Do you speak so gently? Pardon me, I beg you.

I thought that all things here had been savage,  
And therefore I put on the appearance

Of stern command. But whoever you are,

That in this inaccessible wilderness,

Under the shade of melancholy branches,

Lose and neglect the creeping hours of time,

If ever you have looked on better days,

If ever you have been where bells have rung to church,  
If ever you have sat at any good man’s feast,

If ever you have wiped a tear from your eyelids,

And know what it is to pity and be pitied,

Let gentleness be my strong enforcement,

In which hope I blush and hide my sword.

DUKE SENIOR.

It is true that we have seen better days,

And have been called to church by holy bells,  
And sat at good men’s feasts, and wiped our eyes  
Of drops that sacred pity has produced.

And therefore sit down in gentleness,

And take freely whatever help we have

That may be given to your need.

ORLANDO.

Then only hold back your food a little while,

While, like a doe, I go to find my fawn,

And give it food. There is a poor old man

Who after me has limped many a weary step

In pure love. Until he is first satisfied,  
Oppressed with two weak evils, age and hunger,  
I will not touch a bit.

DUKE SENIOR.

Go find him,

And we will waste nothing until you return.

ORLANDO.

I thank you, and may you be blessed for your good comfort.

[Exit.]

DUKE SENIOR.

You see we are not the only ones unhappy.

This wide and universal theatre

Presents more woeful scenes than the stage

Wherein we play.

JAQUES.

All the world’s a stage,

And all the men and women are merely players;

They have their exits and their entrances,

And one man in his time plays many parts,

His acts being seven ages. At first the infant, crying and spitting up in the nurse’s arms;

Then the whining schoolboy, with his satchel

And shining morning face, creeping like a snail unwillingly to school. And then the lover,

Sighing like a furnace, with a sad love song

Written to his mistress’ eyebrow. Then a soldier, full of strange oaths and bearded like the leopard, jealous in honor, sudden and quick to quarrel, seeking the fleeting reputation

Even in the cannon’s mouth. And then the judge, with a fair round belly lined with good chicken,

With severe eyes and a formally trimmed beard,

Full of wise sayings and modern examples;

And so he plays his part. The sixth age shifts into the lean and slippered old man,

With spectacles on his nose and a pouch at his side,

His youthful stockings, well saved, a world too wide for his shrunk shank, and his big manly voice, turning again toward a childish treble, pipes

And whistles in his sound. The last scene of all, that ends this strange eventful history,

Is second childishness and mere oblivion,

Without teeth, without eyes, without taste, without everything.

Enter Orlando carrying Adam.

DUKE SENIOR.

Welcome. Set down your venerable burden,

And let him eat.

ORLANDO.

I thank you most for him.

ADAM.

You certainly should;

I can scarcely speak to thank you for myself.

DUKE SENIOR.

Welcome, fall to. I will not trouble you

As yet to question you about your fortunes.

Give us some music, and good cousin, sing.

SONG.

AMIENS. (Sings.)

Blow, blow, you winter wind,

You are not so unkind

As man’s ingratitude.

Your tooth is not so keen,

Because you are not seen,

Although your breath is rough.

Heigh-ho, sing heigh-ho, unto the green holly. Most friendship is pretending, most loving mere folly. Then, heigh-ho, the holly!

This life is most jolly.

Freeze, freeze, you bitter sky,

That does not bite so near

As benefits forgotten.

Though you freeze the waters,

Your sting is not so sharp

As a friend not remembered.

Heigh-ho, sing heigh-ho, unto the green holly. Most friendship is pretending, most loving mere folly. Then, heigh-ho, the holly!

This life is most jolly.

DUKE SENIOR.

If you were the good Sir Rowland’s son,

As you have whispered faithfully you were,

And as my eye witnesses his likeness

Most truly drawn and living in your face,

Be truly welcome here. I am the Duke

That loved your father. The rest of your fortune, go to my cave and tell me.—Good old man,

You are as truly welcome as your master is.

Support him by the arm. [To Orlando.] Give me your hand, and let me understand all your fortunes.

[Exeunt.]

###CHAPTER### Act Iii

SCENE I. A Room in the Palace

Enter Duke Frederick, Lords and Oliver.

DUKE FREDERICK.

You have not seen him since? Sir, sir, that cannot be.

If I were not more inclined to mercy,

I would not look for an absent target

Of my revenge while you are present. But be careful:

Find your brother wherever he is.

Search for him with a candle. Bring him dead or alive within twelve months, or do not return to seek a living in my territory.

Your lands, and everything you call your own that is worth seizing, I seize into my hands,

Until you can clear yourself by your brother's testimony of what I suspect against you.

OLIVER.

Oh, if only your highness knew my heart in this:

I never loved my brother in my life.

DUKE FREDERICK.

Then you are even more of a villain. Well, push him out the door, and let my officers of such a kind

Seize his house and lands.

Do this quickly, and send him away.

[They exit.]

SCENE II. The Forest of Arden

Enter Orlando with a paper.

ORLANDO.

Hang there, my verse, as witness of my love.

And you, three-times-crowned queen of night, look down with your pure eye, from your pale sphere above, upon your huntress's name that rules my whole life. Oh Rosalind, these trees shall be my books,

And on their bark I will write my thoughts, so that every eye that looks in this forest

Shall see your virtue witnessed everywhere. Run, run, Orlando, carve on every tree

The fair, the pure, and the indescribable woman.

[He exits.]

Enter Corin and Touchstone.

CORIN.

And how do you like this shepherd's life, Master Touchstone?

TOUCHSTONE.

Truly, shepherd, considered on its own, it is a good life; but considered as a shepherd's life, it is worthless. Since it is solitary, I like it very well; but since it is private, it is a very miserable life. Now since it is in the fields, it pleases me well; but since it is not in the court, it is tedious. As it is a simple life, you see, it suits my temperament well; but as there is no more abundance in it, it goes much against my appetite. Do you have any philosophy in you, shepherd?

CORIN.

No more than this: I know that the more one sickens, the worse he feels; and that he who lacks money, means, and contentment is without three good friends; that the nature of rain is to wet, and fire to burn; that good pasture makes fat sheep; and that a great cause of night is lack of the sun; that he who has learned no wisdom by nature or education may complain of good breeding or comes from a very dull family.

TOUCHSTONE.

Such a one is a natural philosopher. Were you ever at court, shepherd?

CORIN.

No, truly.

TOUCHSTONE.

Then you are damned.

CORIN.

No, I hope not.

TOUCHSTONE.

Truly, you are damned, like an ill-roasted egg, all on one side.

CORIN.

For not being at court? Your reason.

TOUCHSTONE.

Why, if you were never at court, you never saw good manners; if you never saw good manners, then your manners must be wicked, and wickedness is sin, and sin is damnation. You are in a perilous state, shepherd.

CORIN.

Not at all, Touchstone. Those that are good manners at the court are as ridiculous in the country as the behavior of the country is most mockable at the court. You told me you do not greet at the court without kissing your hands. That courtesy would be uncleanly if courtiers were shepherds.

TOUCHSTONE.

Give an example, briefly. Come, give an example.

CORIN.

Why, we are always handling our ewes, and their fleeces, you know, are greasy.

TOUCHSTONE.

Why, do not your courtier's hands sweat? And is not the grease of a mutton as wholesome as the sweat of a man? Shallow, shallow. A better example, I say. Come.

CORIN.

Besides, our hands are hard.

TOUCHSTONE.

Your lips will feel them all the sooner. Shallow again. Give me a better example.

CORIN.

And they are often covered with tar from treating our sheep; and would you have us kiss tar? The courtier's hands are perfumed with civet.

TOUCHSTONE.

Most shallow man! You are worm's meat compared to a good piece of flesh indeed! Learn from the wise and consider. Civet is of baser origin than tar, the very unclean discharge of a cat. Fix your example, shepherd.

CORIN.

You have too courtly a wit for me. I'll stop.

TOUCHSTONE.

Will you stop and be damned? God help you, shallow man! God cut you open, you are raw.

CORIN.

Sir, I am a true laborer. I earn what I eat, get what I wear, owe no man hate, envy no man's happiness, am glad of other men's good, content with my harm; and the greatest of my pride is to see my ewes graze and my lambs suck.

TOUCHSTONE.

That is another simple sin in you, to bring the ewes and the rams together and to make your living by the copulation of cattle; to be a pimp to a bell-wether and to betray a she-lamb of a twelvemonth to a crooked-headed, old, cuckoldly ram, out of all reasonable match. If you are not damned for this, the devil himself will have no shepherds. I cannot see how else you should escape.

Enter Rosalind as Ganymede.

CORIN.

Here comes young Master Ganymede, my new mistress's brother.

ROSALIND.

[Reads.]

From the east to western Inde

No jewel is like Rosalind.

Her worth being mounted on the wind, Through all the world bears Rosalind. All the pictures fairest lined

Are but black to Rosalind.

Let no face be kept in mind

But the fair of Rosalind.

TOUCHSTONE.

I'll rhyme for you like that for eight years together, excepting dinners and suppers and sleeping hours. It is the right butter-women's pace to market.

ROSALIND.

Out, fool!

TOUCHSTONE.

For a taste:

If a hart do lack a hind,

Let him seek out Rosalind.

If the cat will after kind,

So be sure will Rosalind.

Winter garments must be lined,

So must slender Rosalind.

They that reap must sheaf and bind,

Then to cart with Rosalind.

Sweetest nut hath sourest rind,

Such a nut is Rosalind.

He that sweetest rose will find

Must find love's prick, and Rosalind. This is the very false gallop of verses. Why do you infect yourself with them?

ROSALIND.

Peace, you dull fool, I found them on a tree.

TOUCHSTONE.

Truly, the tree yields bad fruit.

ROSALIND.

I'll graft it with you, and then I shall graft it with a medlar. Then it will be the earliest fruit in the country, for you'll be rotten before you are half ripe, and that's the right virtue of the medlar.

TOUCHSTONE.

You have spoken, but whether wisely or not, let the forest judge.

Enter Celia as Aliena, reading a paper.

ROSALIND.

Peace, here comes my sister, reading. Stand aside.

CELIA.

[Reads.]

Why should this a desert be?

For it is unpeopled? No!

Tongues I'll hang on every tree

That shall civil sayings show. Some, how brief the life of man

Runs his erring pilgrimage,

That the stretching of a span

Buckles in his sum of age;

Some, of violated vows

Twixt the souls of friend and friend. But upon the fairest boughs,

Or at every sentence' end,

Will I "Rosalinda" write,

Teaching all that read to know The quintessence of every sprite

Heaven would in little show.

Therefore heaven nature charged

That one body should be filled With all graces wide-enlarged.

Nature presently distilled

Helen's cheek, but not her heart, Cleopatra's majesty;

Atalanta's better part,

Sad Lucretia's modesty.

Thus Rosalind of many parts

By heavenly synod was devised, Of many faces, eyes, and hearts

To have the touches dearest prized. Heaven would that she these gifts should have, And I to live and die her slave._

ROSALIND.

O most gentle Jupiter, what a tedious sermon of love have you wearied your parishioners with, and never once cried, “Have patience, good people!”

CELIA.

How now! Back, friends. Shepherd, go off a little. Go with him, sirrah.

TOUCHSTONE.

Come, shepherd, let us make an honourable retreat, though not with bag and baggage, yet with scrip and scrippage.

[_Exeunt Corin and Touchstone._]

CELIA.

Did you hear these verses?

ROSALIND.

O yes, I heard them all, and more too, for some of them had more feet in them than the verses could bear.

CELIA.

That is no matter. The feet might bear the verses.

ROSALIND.

Ay, but the feet were lame and could not bear themselves without the verse, and therefore stood lamely in the verse.

CELIA.

But did you hear without wondering how your name should be hung and carved upon these trees?

ROSALIND.

I was seven of the nine days out of the wonder before you came; for look here what I found on a palm-tree. I was never so berhymed since Pythagoras’ time, when I was an Irish rat, which I can hardly remember.

CELIA.

Do you know who has done this?

ROSALIND.

Is it a man?

CELIA.

And a chain, that you once wore, about his neck. Do you change colour?

ROSALIND.

I pray you, who?

CELIA.

O Lord, Lord, it is a hard matter for friends to meet; but mountains may be removed with earthquakes and so encounter.

ROSALIND.

Nay, but who is it?

CELIA.

Is it possible?

ROSALIND.

Nay, I pray you now, with most earnest pleading, tell me who it is.

CELIA.

O wonderful, wonderful, most wonderful wonderful, and yet again wonderful, and after that, out of all exclaiming!

ROSALIND.

Good my complexion! Do you think, though I am dressed like a man, I have a doublet and hose in my disposition? One inch of delay more is a South Sea of discovery. I pray you tell me who it is quickly, and speak fast. I wish you could stammer, so that you might pour this hidden man out of your mouth, as wine comes out of a narrow-mouthed bottle—either too much at once or none at all. I pray you take the cork out of your mouth so that I may drink your news.

CELIA.

So you may put a man in your belly.

ROSALIND.

Is he of God’s making? What kind of man? Is his head worth a hat, or his chin worth a beard?

CELIA.

Nay, he has but a little beard.

ROSALIND.

Why, God will send more if the man will be thankful. Let me wait for the growth of his beard, if you do not delay me the knowledge of his chin.

CELIA.

It is young Orlando, who tripped up the wrestler’s heels and your heart both in an instant.

ROSALIND.

Nay, but the devil take mocking! Speak with a serious face and as a true maid.

CELIA.

In faith, cousin, it is he.

ROSALIND.

Orlando?

CELIA.

Orlando.

ROSALIND.

Alas the day, what shall I do with my doublet and hose? What did he do when you saw him? What did he say? How did he look? What was he wearing? What is he doing here? Did he ask for me? Where is he staying? How did he part from you? And when shall you see him again? Answer me in one word.

CELIA.

You must lend me Gargantua’s mouth first. It is a word too great for any mouth of this age’s size. To say yes and no to these particulars is more than to answer in a catechism.

ROSALIND.

But does he know that I am in this forest and in man’s clothing? Does he look as fresh as he did the day he wrestled?

CELIA.

It is as easy to count atoms as to answer the questions of a lover. But take a taste of my finding him, and enjoy it with good attention. I found him under a tree, like a dropped acorn.

ROSALIND.

It may well be called Jove’s tree when it drops forth such fruit.

CELIA.

Give me audience, good madam.

ROSALIND.

Proceed.

CELIA.

There lay he, stretched along like a wounded knight.

ROSALIND.

Though it be pity to see such a sight, it well becomes the ground.

CELIA.

Cry “holla!” to thy tongue, I prithee. It curvets unseasonably. He was furnished like a hunter.

ROSALIND.

O, ominous! He comes to kill my heart.

CELIA.

I would sing my song without a burden. Thou bring’st me out of tune.

ROSALIND.

Do you not know I am a woman? When I think, I must speak. Sweet, say on.

Enter Orlando and Jaques.

CELIA.

You bring me out. Soft, comes he not here?

ROSALIND.

’Tis he! Slink by, and note him.

[_Rosalind and Celia step aside._]

JAQUES.

I thank you for your company but, good faith, I had as lief have been myself alone.

ORLANDO.

And so had I, but yet, for fashion sake, I thank you too for your society.

JAQUES.

God be wi’ you, let’s meet as little as we can.

ORLANDO.

I do desire we may be better strangers.

JAQUES.

I pray you, mar no more trees with writing love songs in their barks.

ORLANDO.

I pray you, mar no more of my verses with reading them ill-favouredly.

JAQUES.

Rosalind is your love’s name?

ORLANDO.

Yes, just.

JAQUES.

I do not like her name.

ORLANDO.

There was no thought of pleasing you when she was christened.

JAQUES.

What stature is she of?

ORLANDO.

Just as high as my heart.

JAQUES.

You are full of pretty answers. Have you not been acquainted with goldsmiths’ wives, and conned them out of rings?

ORLANDO.

Not so; but I answer you right painted cloth, from whence you have studied your questions.

JAQUES.

You have a nimble wit. I think ’twas made of Atalanta’s heels. Will you sit down with me? And we two will rail against our mistress the world and all our misery.

ORLANDO.

I will chide no breather in the world but myself, against whom I know most faults.

JAQUES.

The worst fault you have is to be in love.

ORLANDO.

’Tis a fault I will not change for your best virtue. I am weary of you.

JAQUES.

By my troth, I was seeking for a fool when I found you.

ORLANDO.

He is drowned in the brook. Look but in, and you shall see him.

JAQUES.

There I shall see mine own figure.

ORLANDO.

Which I take to be either a fool or a cipher.

JAQUES.

I’ll tarry no longer with you. Farewell, good Signior Love.

ORLANDO.

I am glad of your departure. Adieu, good Monsieur Melancholy.

[_Exit Jaques.—Celia and Rosalind come forward._]

ROSALIND.

I will speak to him like a saucy lackey, and under that habit play the knave with him.

Do you hear, forester?

ORLANDO.

Very well. What would you?

ROSALIND.

I pray you, what is’t o’clock?

ORLANDO.

You should ask me what time o’ day. There’s no clock in the forest.

ROSALIND.

Then there is no true lover in the forest, else sighing every minute and groaning every hour would detect the lazy foot of time as well as a clock.

ORLANDO.

And why not the swift foot of time? Had not that been as proper?

ROSALIND.

By no means, sir. Time travels in divers paces with divers persons. I’ll tell you who time ambles withal, who time trots withal, who time gallops withal, and who he stands still withal.

ORLANDO.

I prithee, who doth he trot withal?

ROSALIND.

Marry, he trots hard with a young maid between the contract of her marriage and the day it is solemnized. If the interim be but a se’nnight, time’s pace is so hard that it seems the length of seven year.

ORLANDO.

Who ambles time withal?

ROSALIND.

With a priest who knows no Latin, and a rich man who does not have gout; for the one sleeps easily because he cannot study, and the other lives merrily because he feels no pain; the one lacking the burden of lean and wasteful learning, the other knowing no burden of heavy, tedious poverty. With these, time ambles along.

ORLANDO.

Who does he gallop with?

ROSALIND.

With a thief to the gallows; for though he walks as softly as foot can fall, he thinks himself too slow to get there.

ORLANDO.

Who does he stand still with?

ROSALIND.

With lawyers during vacation; for they sleep between court terms, and then they do not notice how time moves.

ORLANDO.

Where do you live, pretty youth?

ROSALIND.

With this shepherdess, my sister, here on the edge of the forest, like fringe upon a petticoat.

ORLANDO.

Are you native to this place?

ROSALIND.

Like the rabbit that you see lives where she was born.

ORLANDO.

Your accent is finer than you could have acquired in such a remote dwelling.

ROSALIND.

Many people have told me so. But indeed an old religious uncle of mine taught me to speak, who in his youth was a man of the city, one who knew courtship too well, for there he fell in love. I have heard him give many lectures against it, and I thank God I am not a woman, to be afflicted with so many foolish faults as he generally charged their whole sex with.

ORLANDO.

Can you remember any of the chief evils that he laid to the charge of women?

ROSALIND.

There were none chief. They were all like one another, as halfpence are; every single fault seemed monstrous until its fellow fault came to match it.

ORLANDO.

Please, tell me some of them.

ROSALIND.

No. I will not waste my medicine except on those who are sick. There is a man who haunts the forest and abuses our young plants by carving "Rosalind" on their bark; hangs odes upon hawthorns and elegies on brambles; all, indeed, making a goddess of the name of Rosalind. If I could meet that love-peddler, I would give him some good advice, for he seems to have the daily fever of love upon him.

ORLANDO.

I am he that is so love-shaken. Please tell me your remedy.

ROSALIND.

There is none of my uncle's signs upon you. He taught me how to recognize a man in love, in which cage of rushes I am sure you are not a prisoner.

ORLANDO.

What were his signs?

ROSALIND.

A lean cheek, which you do not have; a blue and sunken eye, which you do not have; a silent spirit, which you do not have; a neglected beard, which you do not have—but I pardon you for that, for simply your amount of beard is a younger brother's income. Then your stockings should be ungartered, your hat unbanded, your sleeve unbuttoned, your shoe untied, and everything about you showing a careless desolation. But you are no such man. You are rather perfectly precise in your attire, as if loving yourself rather than seeming the lover of anyone else.

ORLANDO.

Fair youth, I wish I could make you believe I love.

ROSALIND.

Make me believe it? You may as soon make her that you love believe it, which I am sure she is more ready to do than to confess she does. That is one of the points in which women always give the lie to their consciences. But, in truth, are you the one who hangs the verses on the trees, in which Rosalind is so admired?

ORLANDO.

I swear to you, youth, by the white hand of Rosalind, I am that he, that unfortunate he.

ROSALIND.

But are you as much in love as your rhymes say?

ORLANDO.

Neither rhyme nor reason can express how much.

ROSALIND.

Love is merely a madness, and, I tell you, deserves as well a dark house and a whip as madmen do; and the reason why they are not so punished and cured is that the lunacy is so ordinary that the whippers are in love too. Yet I profess curing it by counsel.

ORLANDO.

Did you ever cure any so?

ROSALIND.

Yes, one, and in this manner. He was to imagine me his love, his mistress, and I set him every day to woo me; at which time would I, being but a moonish youth, grieve, be effeminate, changeable, longing and liking, proud, fantastical, apish, shallow, inconstant, full of tears, full of smiles; for every passion something and for no passion truly anything, as boys and women are for the most part cattle of this colour; would now like him, now loathe him; then entertain him, then forswear him; now weep for him, then spit at him; that I drave my suitor from his mad humour of love to a living humour of madness, which was to forswear the full stream of the world and to live in a nook merely monastic. And thus I cured him, and this way will I take upon me to wash your liver as clean as a sound sheep's heart, that there shall not be one spot of love in 't.

ORLANDO.

I would not be cured, youth.

ROSALIND.

I would cure you, if you would but call me Rosalind and come every day to my cote and woo me.

ORLANDO.

Now, by the faith of my love, I will. Tell me where it is.

ROSALIND.

Go with me to it, and I'll show it you; and by the way you shall tell me where in the forest you live. Will you go?

ORLANDO.

With all my heart, good youth.

ROSALIND.

Nay, you must call me Rosalind. Come, sister, will you go?

[_Exeunt._]

SCENE III. Another part of the Forest

Enter Touchstone and Audrey; Jaques at a distance observing them.

TOUCHSTONE.

Come apace, good Audrey. I will fetch up your goats, Audrey. And how, Audrey? Am I the man yet? Doth my simple feature content you?

AUDREY.

Your features, Lord warrant us! What features?

TOUCHSTONE.

I am here with thee and thy goats, as the most capricious poet, honest Ovid, was among the Goths.

JAQUES.

[_Aside_.] O knowledge ill-inhabited, worse than Jove in a thatched house!

TOUCHSTONE.

When a man's verses cannot be understood, nor a man's good wit seconded with the forward child, understanding, it strikes a man more dead than a great reckoning in a little room. Truly, I would the gods had made thee poetical.

AUDREY.

I do not know what "poetical" is. Is it honest in deed and word? Is it a true thing?

TOUCHSTONE.

No, truly; for the truest poetry is the most feigning, and lovers are given to poetry, and what they swear in poetry may be said, as lovers, they do feign.

AUDREY.

Do you wish, then, that the gods had made me poetical?

TOUCHSTONE.

I do, truly, for thou swear'st to me thou art honest. Now if thou wert a poet, I might have some hope thou didst feign.

AUDREY.

Would you not have me honest?

TOUCHSTONE.

No, truly, unless thou wert hard-favoured; for honesty coupled to beauty is to have honey a sauce to sugar.

JAQUES.

[_Aside_.] A material fool!

AUDREY.

Well, I am not fair, and therefore I pray the gods make me honest.

TOUCHSTONE.

Truly, and to cast away honesty upon a foul slut were to put good meat into an unclean dish.

AUDREY.

I am not a slut, though I thank the gods I am foul.

TOUCHSTONE.

Well, praised be the gods for thy foulness; sluttishness may come hereafter. But be it as it may be, I will marry thee. And to that end I have been with Sir Oliver Martext, the vicar of the next village, who hath promised to meet me in this place of the forest and to couple us.

JAQUES.

[_Aside._] I would really like to see this meeting.

AUDREY.

Well, may the gods give us joy!

TOUCHSTONE.

Amen. A man might hesitate in this attempt if he were of a fearful heart. For here we have no temple but the wood, no assembly but horned beasts. But what of it? Courage! As horns are hateful, they are also necessary. It is said, "Many a man knows no end of his goods." Right. Many a man has good horns and knows no end of them. Well, that is the dowry of his wife; it is none of his own getting. Horns? Even so. Poor men alone? No, no, the noblest deer has them as huge as the common one. Is the single man therefore blessed? No. As a walled town is worthier than a village, so is the forehead of a married man more honorable than the bare brow of a bachelor. And by as much as defense is better than no skill, by so much is a horn more precious than to lack one.

Enter Sir Oliver Martext.

Here comes Sir Oliver. Sir Oliver Martext, you are well met. Will you dispatch us here under this tree, or shall we go with you to your chapel?

MARTEXT.

Is there no one here to give the woman?

TOUCHSTONE.

I will not take her as a gift from any man.

MARTEXT.

Truly, she must be given, or the marriage is not lawful.

JAQUES.

[_Coming forward._] Proceed, proceed. I will give her.

TOUCHSTONE.

Good evening, good Master What-you-call-it. How do you do, sir? You are very well met. God reward you for your last company. I am very glad to see you. Just a little matter in hand here, sir. Please, keep your hat on.

JAQUES.

Will you be married, fool?

TOUCHSTONE.

As the ox has his yoke, sir, the horse his curb, and the falcon her bells, so man has his desires. And as pigeons bill, so wedlock would be nibbling.

JAQUES.

And will you, being a man of your breeding, be married under a bush like a beggar? Get yourself to church, and have a good priest who can tell you what marriage is. This fellow will only join you together as they join wood paneling. Then one of you will prove a shrunk panel, and like green timber, warp, warp.

TOUCHSTONE.

[_Aside._] I am inclined to think I would be better married by him than by another. For he is not likely to marry me well, and not being well married, it will be a good excuse for me hereafter to leave my wife.

JAQUES.

Come with me, and let me counsel you.

TOUCHSTONE.

Come, sweet Audrey. We must be married, or we must live in sin. Farewell, good Master Oliver. Not—

_O sweet Oliver,

O brave Oliver,

Leave me not behind thee._

But—

_Wind away,—

Begone, I say,

I will not to wedding with thee._

[_Exeunt Touchstone, Audrey and Jaques._]

MARTEXT.

It does not matter. Not one fantastical fool among them all shall mock me out of my calling.

[_Exit._]

SCENE IV. Another part of the Forest. Before a Cottage

Enter Rosalind and Celia.

ROSALIND.

Do not talk to me, I will weep.

CELIA.

Do, I beg you, but still have the grace to consider that tears do not suit a man.

ROSALIND.

But do I not have cause to weep?

CELIA.

As good a cause as one could desire; therefore weep.

ROSALIND.

His very hair is of the deceiving color.

CELIA.

Somewhat browner than Judas's. Indeed, his kisses are Judas's own children.

ROSALIND.

In faith, his hair is of a good color.

CELIA.

An excellent color. Your chestnut was always the only color.

ROSALIND.

And his kissing is as full of holiness as the touch of holy bread.

CELIA.

He has bought a pair of cast-off lips from Diana. A nun of winter's sisterhood kisses no more religiously; the very ice of chastity is in them.

ROSALIND.

But why did he swear he would come this morning, and does not come?

CELIA.

No, certainly, there is no truth in him.

ROSALIND.

Do you think so?

CELIA.

Yes. I don’t think he’s a pickpocket or a horse thief, but as for his faithfulness in love, I think he’s as hollow as a covered goblet or a worm-eaten nut.

ROSALIND.

Not true in love?

CELIA.

Yes, when he is in love, but I don’t think he is.

ROSALIND.

You’ve heard him swear outright that he was.

CELIA.

“Was” is not “is.” Besides, a lover’s oath is no stronger than a bartender’s word. They both confirm false bills. He’s here in the forest, attending on the Duke, your father.

ROSALIND.

I met the Duke yesterday and had a long talk with him. He asked me what my parentage was. I told him, as good as his, so he laughed and let me go. But why talk of fathers when there is such a man as Orlando?

CELIA.

Oh, he’s a fine man! He writes fine verses, speaks fine words, swears fine oaths, and breaks them finely—quite crosswise, right through his lover’s heart, like a clumsy young jouster who spurs his horse only on one side and breaks his lance like a noble fool. But everything is fine that youth mounts and folly guides. Who comes here?

Enter Corin.

CORIN.

Mistress and master, you have often asked

About the shepherd who complained of love,

The one you saw sitting by me on the grass,

Praising the proud, disdainful shepherdess

Who was his beloved.

CELIA.

Well, what about him?

CORIN.

If you want to see a scene truly played out

Between the pale look of true love

And the red glow of scorn and proud disdain,

Go just a little way, and I will lead you,

If you want to watch it.

ROSALIND.

Oh, come, let us go.

The sight of lovers feeds those in love.

Bring us to this sight, and you shall say

I’ll prove an active actor in their play.

[They exit.]

SCENE V. Another part of the Forest

Enter Silvius and Phoebe.

SILVIUS.

Sweet Phoebe, do not scorn me; do not, Phoebe. Say that you do not love me, but do not say so

In bitterness. The common executioner,

Whose heart the accustomed sight of death makes hard, does not bring the axe down on a humbled neck

Without first begging pardon. Will you be sterner

Than he who lives and dies by bloody blows?

Enter Rosalind, Celia and Corin, at a distance.

PHOEBE.

I would not be your executioner.

I flee from you because I would not hurt you.

You tell me there is murder in my eye.

It’s pretty, surely, and very likely,

That eyes—which are the frailest and softest things, and which shut their cowardly gates on tiny specks—

Should be called tyrants, butchers, murderers! Now I frown at you with all my heart,

And if my eyes can wound, now let them kill you. Now pretend to faint; why, now fall down. Or if you cannot—oh, for shame, for shame—do not lie and say my eyes are murderers.

Now show me the wound my eye has made in you. Scratch yourself with just a pin, and some scar remains. Lean upon a reed,

And the mark and visible impression

Your palm keeps for a moment. But now my eyes,

Which I have thrown at you, do not hurt you. And I am sure there is no force in eyes

That can do harm.

SILVIUS.

Oh dear Phoebe,

If ever—and that ever may be near—

You meet in some young face the power of love, then you shall know the invisible wounds

That love’s sharp arrows make.

PHOEBE.

But until that time,

Do not come near me. And when that time comes, mock me, do not pity me,

Just as until that time I shall not pity you.

ROSALIND.

[Advancing.] And why, may I ask? Who might your mother be, that you insult, gloat, and all at once,

Over the wretched? What if you have no beauty—as, by my faith, I see no more in you

Than enough to go to bed without a candle—

Must you therefore be proud and pitiless?

Why, what does this mean? Why do you look at me like that?

I see nothing more in you than in the ordinary

goods nature produces for sale. By my little life,

I think she means to trap my eyes too!

No, truly, proud mistress, do not hope for that. It is not your dark eyebrows, your black silk hair, your shiny black eyes, nor your cream-colored cheek, that can tame my spirits into worshipping you.

You foolish shepherd, why do you follow her, like a damp south wind, puffing with wind and rain? You are a thousand times a better man

than she is a woman. It is fools like you

that fill the world with ugly children. It is not her mirror but you that flatters her, and because of you she sees herself as more handsome

than any of her features can show her.

But, mistress, know yourself; get down on your knees, and thank heaven, fasting, for a good man's love. For I must tell you frankly in your ear,

sell when you can; you are not for all markets. Beg the man's pardon, love him, take his offer;

ugliness is most ugly when it is ugly enough to mock. So take her, shepherd. Farewell.

PHOEBE.

Sweet youth, I wish you would scold for a whole year! I would rather hear you scold than this man woo.

ROSALIND.

He has fallen in love with your ugliness, and she will fall in love with my anger. If that is so, as fast as she answers you with frowning looks, I will season her with bitter words. Why do you look at me like that?

PHOEBE.

I bear you no ill will.

ROSALIND.

Please do not fall in love with me,

for I am more false than vows made when drunk.

Besides, I do not like you. If you want to know where I live, it is at the clump of olive trees nearby.

Will you go, sister? Shepherd, press her hard.

Come, sister. Shepherdess, look on him more kindly, and do not be proud. Though all the world could see, no one could be so deceived in what they see as he is.

Come, to our flock.

[Exeunt Rosalind, Celia and Corin.]

PHOEBE.

Dead shepherd, now I understand the truth of your saying:

"Who ever loved that loved not at first sight?"

SILVIUS.

Sweet Phoebe—

PHOEBE.

Ha, what do you say, Silvius?

SILVIUS.

Sweet Phoebe, pity me.

PHOEBE.

Why, I am sorry for you, gentle Silvius.

SILVIUS.

Wherever sorrow is, relief would be welcome.

If you do feel sorrow at my grief in love,

by giving love, your sorrow and my grief

would both be eliminated.

PHOEBE.

You have my love. Is that not neighborly?

SILVIUS.

I want you.

PHOEBE.

Why, that would be greediness.

Silvius, there was a time when I hated you;

and yet it is not that I love you now;

but since you can talk of love so well, your company, which was once annoying to me,

I will endure, and I will put you to use too.

But do not look for any further reward

than your own gladness that you are employed.

SILVIUS.

So holy and so perfect is my love,

and I am in such a poverty of favor,

that I shall think it a most plentiful harvest

to glean the broken stalks after the man

who reaps the main crop. Let fall now and then a scattered smile, and I will live on that.

PHOEBE.

Do you know the youth who spoke to me just now?

SILVIUS.

Not very well, but I have met him often,

and he has bought the cottage and the land that the old farmer once owned.

PHOEBE.

Do not think I love him, though I ask about him.

He is just a silly boy—yet he talks well.

But what do I care for words? Yet words do well

when the one who speaks them pleases those who hear. He is a handsome youth—not very handsome—

but surely he is proud, and yet his pride suits him. He will make a fine man. The best thing about him is his complexion; and faster than his tongue caused offense, his eye healed it.

He is not very tall, yet for his age he is tall; his legs are nothing special, and yet they are fine.

There was a pretty redness in his lip,

a little riper and more lively red

than the red mixed in his cheek. It was just the difference between a steady red and a blended damask.

There are some women, Silvius, who, if they had examined him piece by piece as I did, would have almost

fallen in love with him; but as for me,

I neither love him nor hate him; and yet

I have more reason to hate him than to love him. For what business did he have scolding me?

He said my eyes were black and my hair black, and now that I remember it, he mocked me.

I wonder why I did not answer him back.

But that does not matter: not answering is not the same as forgiving. I will write him a very mocking letter,

and you shall carry it. Will you, Silvius?

SILVIUS.

Phoebe, with all my heart.

PHOEBE.

I will write it right away,

for the words are in my head and in my heart.

I will be harsh with him and extremely brief.

Come with me, Silvius.

[They exit.]

###CHAPTER### Act Iv

SCENE I. The Forest of Arden

Enter Rosalind, Celia and Jaques.

JAQUES.

Please, pretty youth, let me get to know you better.

ROSALIND.

They say you are a melancholy fellow.

JAQUES.

I am; I love it better than laughing.

ROSALIND.

Those who go to extremes in either direction are insufferable people, and expose themselves to every common criticism worse than drunkards do.

JAQUES.

Why, it is good to be sad and say nothing.

ROSALIND.

Why then, it is good to be a post.

JAQUES.

I have neither the scholar's melancholy, which is rivalry; nor the musician's, which is whimsical; nor the courtier's, which is proud; nor the soldier's, which is ambitious; nor the lawyer's, which is calculating; nor the lady's, which is fastidious; nor the lover's, which is all of these. Mine is a melancholy of my own, compounded of many ingredients, extracted from many sources, and indeed from the varied contemplation of my travels, in which my frequent brooding wraps me in a most peculiar sadness.

ROSALIND.

A traveller! By my faith, you have great reason to be sad. I fear you have sold your own lands to see other men's. To have seen much and to have nothing is to have rich eyes and poor hands.

JAQUES.

Yes, I have gained my experience.

ROSALIND.

And your experience makes you sad. I would rather have a fool to make me merry than experience to make me sad—and to travel for it too.

Enter Orlando.

ORLANDO.

Good day and happiness, dear Rosalind!

JAQUES.

Well then, God be with you, if you are going to talk in blank verse.

ROSALIND.

Farewell, Monsieur Traveller. Be sure you lisp and wear strange clothes; belittle all the good things of your own country; be out of love with your birthplace, and almost scold God for making you with the face you have, or I will hardly believe you have ever floated in a gondola.

[_Exit Jaques._]

Why, how now, Orlando, where have you been all this while? You a lover! If you play such a trick on me again, never come into my sight again.

ORLANDO.

My fair Rosalind, I come within an hour of my promise.

ROSALIND.

Break an hour's promise in love? He that will divide a minute into a thousand parts, and break only one part of a thousandth of a minute in the affairs of love—it may be said of him that Cupid has tapped him on the shoulder, but I will guarantee his heart is untouched.

ORLANDO.

Pardon me, dear Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

No, if you are so late, come no more into my sight. I would as soon be wooed by a snail.

ORLANDO.

By a snail?

ROSALIND.

Yes, by a snail, for though he comes slowly, he carries his house on his head—a better dowry, I think, than you offer a woman. Besides, he brings his destiny with him.

ORLANDO.

What is that?

ROSALIND.

Why, horns, which men like you are obliged to receive from their wives. But he comes armed with his own fortune and prevents the slander against his wife.

ORLANDO.

Virtue does not make horns, and my Rosalind is virtuous.

ROSALIND.

And I am your Rosalind.

CELIA.

It pleases him to call you so, but he has a Rosalind of a better look than you.

ROSALIND.

Come, woo me, woo me, for now I am in a holiday mood, and likely enough to consent. What would you say to me now, if I were your very, very Rosalind?

ORLANDO.

I would kiss before I spoke.

ROSALIND.

No, you would do better to speak first, and when you were stuck for lack of things to say, you might take the opportunity to kiss. Very good orators, when they run out of words, will spit; and for lovers lacking—God help us—things to say, the cleanest solution is to kiss.

ORLANDO.

What if the kiss is denied?

ROSALIND.

Then she makes you beg, and there begins new material.

ORLANDO.

Who could run out of words, being before his beloved mistress?

ROSALIND.

Indeed, you would have to if I were your mistress, or I would think my virtue was less than my intelligence.

ORLANDO.

What, would I lose my suit?

ROSALIND.

Not your clothes, but your romantic pursuit. Am I not your Rosalind?

ORLANDO.

I am happy to say you are, because then I can talk about her.

ROSALIND.

Well, speaking as her, I say I will not have you.

ORLANDO.

Then, speaking as myself, I die.

ROSALIND.

No, honestly, die through a lawyer instead. This poor world is almost six thousand years old, and in all that time no man ever died in his own person, that is to say, from love. Troilus had his brains beaten out with a Greek club, yet he did what he could to die for love before that, and he is one of the great examples of love. Leander would have lived many a good year even if Hero had become a nun, if it had not been for a hot midsummer night. The good young man simply went out to bathe in the Hellespont, got a cramp, and drowned. The foolish historians of that time decided it was because of Hero of Sestos. But these are all lies. Men have died from time to time and worms have eaten them, but not for love.

ORLANDO.

I would not want my real Rosalind to think that way, because I swear her frown could kill me.

ROSALIND.

I swear by this hand, it would not kill a fly. But come, now I will be your Rosalind in a more willing mood. Ask me for anything you want, and I will grant it.

ORLANDO.

Then love me, Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

Yes, truly, I will—Fridays and Saturdays and every day.

ORLANDO.

And will you have me?

ROSALIND.

Yes, and twenty more like you.

ORLANDO.

What are you saying?

ROSALIND.

Are you not good?

ORLANDO.

I hope so.

ROSALIND.

Well then, can one desire too much of a good thing? Come, sister, you will be the priest and marry us. Give me your hand, Orlando. What do you say, sister?

ORLANDO.

Please, marry us.

CELIA.

I cannot say the words.

ROSALIND.

You must begin, “Will you, Orlando—”

CELIA.

Go on then. Will you, Orlando, have this Rosalind as your wife?

ORLANDO.

I will.

ROSALIND.

Yes, but when?

ORLANDO.

Why, right now, as quickly as she can marry us.

ROSALIND.

Then you must say, “I take you, Rosalind, as my wife.”

ORLANDO.

I take you, Rosalind, as my wife.

ROSALIND.

I might ask to see your license. But I do take you, Orlando, as my husband. There is a girl who rushes ahead of the priest, and certainly a woman’s thoughts run ahead of her actions.

ORLANDO.

So do all thoughts. They have wings.

ROSALIND.

Now tell me how long you would want her after you have won her.

ORLANDO.

Forever and a day.

ROSALIND.

Say “a day” without the “forever.” No, no, Orlando, men are like April when they court, and December when they marry. Girls are like May when they are maidens, but the weather changes when they become wives. I will be more jealous of you than a Barbary cock-pigeon is of his hen, louder than a parrot complaining about rain, more fickle than an ape, and more wildly changeable in my desires than a monkey. I will cry over nothing, like Diana in the fountain, and I will do it when you are in the mood to be cheerful. I will laugh like a hyena, and that when you feel like sleeping.

ORLANDO.

But will my Rosalind do that?

ROSALIND.

I swear by my life, she will do exactly as I do.

ORLANDO.

Oh, but she is wise.

ROSALIND.

Otherwise she would not have the cleverness to do this. The wiser a woman is, the more stubborn she is. Close the doors on a woman’s wit, and it will escape through the window. Shut that, and it will slip out through the keyhole. Stop that, and it will fly out with the smoke up the chimney.

ORLANDO.

A man with a wife that clever might say, “Wit, where are you going?”

ROSALIND.

No, you could save that warning until you caught your wife’s wit heading toward your neighbor’s bed.

ORLANDO.

And what clever excuse could cleverness possibly come up with for that?

ROSALIND.

Well, to say she came there looking for you. You'll never catch her without an answer unless you catch her without her tongue. Oh, any woman who can't turn her own mistake into her husband's fault should never nurse her own child, because she'll raise it to be a fool.

ORLANDO.

For these two hours, Rosalind, I will leave you.

ROSALIND.

Alas, dear love, I cannot do without you for two hours.

ORLANDO.

I must attend the Duke at dinner. By two o'clock I will be with you again.

ROSALIND.

Yes, go on, go on. I knew what you would turn out to be. My friends told me as much, and I thought no less. That flattering tongue of yours won me. It's just one more castaway, and so, come death! Two o'clock is your hour?

ORLANDO.

Yes, sweet Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

By my faith, and in all earnestness, and so God help me, and by all pretty oaths that are not dangerous, if you break one bit of your promise or come one minute late, I will think you the most pathetic promise-breaker, and the most hollow lover, and the most unworthy of the woman you call Rosalind that could be chosen out of the whole crowd of the unfaithful. So beware my judgment, and keep your promise.

ORLANDO.

With no less devotion than if you truly were my Rosalind. So, goodbye.

ROSALIND.

Well, Time is the old judge who examines all such offenders, and let time test you. Goodbye.

[Exit Orlando.]

CELIA.

You have simply abused our whole sex with your love-chatter! We should have your doublet and hose pulled over your head and show the world what the bird has done to her own nest.

ROSALIND.

Oh cousin, cousin, cousin, my pretty little cousin, if only you knew how many fathoms deep I am in love! But it cannot be measured; my affection has an unknown bottom, like the Bay of Portugal.

CELIA.

Or rather, bottomless, so that as fast as you pour affection in, it runs out.

ROSALIND.

No, let that same wicked bastard of Venus, who was conceived by thought, born of moodiness, and brought forth by madness—that blind rascally boy who abuses everyone's eyes because his own are missing—let him be judge of how deep I am in love. I'll tell you, Aliena, I cannot be out of the sight of Orlando. I'll go find some shade and sigh until he comes.

CELIA.

And I'll sleep.

[Exeunt.]

SCENE II. Another part of the Forest

Enter Jaques and Lords, dressed like foresters.

JAQUES.

Which one of you killed the deer?

FIRST LORD.

Sir, it was I.

JAQUES.

Let's present him to the Duke like a Roman conqueror, and it would be fitting to set the deer's horns upon his head as a branch of victory. Have you no song, forester, for this purpose?

SECOND LORD.

Yes, sir.

JAQUES.

Sing it. It doesn't matter whether it's in tune, so long as it makes enough noise.

SONG.

SECOND LORD.

[_Sings_.]

What shall he have that killed the deer?

His leather skin and horns to wear.

Then sing him home:

[_The rest shall bear this burden_.]

Take thou no scorn to wear the horn. It was a crest ere thou wast born. Thy father’s father wore it And thy father bore it.

The horn, the horn, the lusty horn Is not a thing to laugh to scorn.

[_Exeunt._]

SCENE III. Another part of the Forest

Enter Rosalind and Celia.

ROSALIND.

How say you now? Is it not past two o’clock? And here much Orlando.

CELIA.

I warrant you, with pure love and troubled brain he hath ta’en his bow and arrows and is gone forth to sleep.

Enter Silvius.

Look who comes here.

SILVIUS.

My errand is to you, fair youth.

My gentle Phoebe did bid me give you this.

[_Giving a letter._]

I know not the contents, but, as I guess

By the stern brow and waspish action

Which she did use as she was writing of it,

It bears an angry tenor. Pardon me,

I am but as a guiltless messenger.

ROSALIND.

Patience herself would startle at this letter And play the swaggerer. Bear this, bear all!

She says I am not fair, that I lack manners;

She calls me proud, and that she could not love me, Were man as rare as phoenix. ’Od’s my will,

Her love is not the hare that I do hunt.

Why writes she so to me? Well, shepherd, well, This is a letter of your own device.

SILVIUS.

No, I protest, I know not the contents.

Phoebe did write it.

ROSALIND.

Come, come, you are a fool,

And turned into the extremity of love.

I saw her hand. She has a leathern hand,

A freestone-coloured hand. I verily did think That her old gloves were on, but ’twas her hands. She has a huswife’s hand—but that’s no matter. I say she never did invent this letter;

This is a man’s invention, and his hand.

SILVIUS.

Sure, it is hers.

ROSALIND.

Why, ’tis a boisterous and a cruel style,

A style for challengers. Why, she defies me,

Like Turk to Christian. Women’s gentle brain

Could not drop forth such giant-rude invention, Such Ethiop words, blacker in their effect

Than in their countenance. Will you hear the letter?

SILVIUS.

So please you, for I never heard it yet,

Yet heard too much of Phoebe’s cruelty.

ROSALIND.

She Phoebes me. Mark how the tyrant writes.

[_Reads._]

_Art thou god to shepherd turned, That a maiden’s heart hath burned?_ Can a woman rail thus?

SILVIUS.

Call you this railing?

ROSALIND.

_Why, thy godhead laid apart,

Warr’st thou with a woman’s heart?_ Did you ever hear such railing?

_Whiles the eye of man did woo me, That could do no vengeance to me._ Meaning me a beast.

_If the scorn of your bright eyne Have power to raise such love in mine, Alack, in me what strange effect

Would they work in mild aspect?

Whiles you chid me, I did love,

How then might your prayers move? He that brings this love to thee

Little knows this love in me;

And by him seal up thy mind,

Whether that thy youth and kind

Will the faithful offer take

Of me, and all that I can make,

Or else by him my love deny,

And then I’ll study how to die._

SILVIUS.

Call you this chiding?

CELIA.

Alas, poor shepherd.

ROSALIND.

Do you pity him? No, he deserves no pity.—Wilt thou love such a woman? What, to make thee an instrument and play false strains upon thee? Not to be endured! Well, go your way to her, for I see love hath made thee a tame snake, and say this to her: that if she love me, I charge her to love thee; if she will not, I will never have her unless thou entreat for her. If you be a true lover, hence, and not a word, for here comes more company.

[_Exit Silvius._]

Enter Oliver.

OLIVER.

Good morning, fair ones. Please, if you know, where in the outskirts of this forest stands

A sheep pen fenced about with olive trees?

CELIA.

West of this place, down in the neighboring valley. The row of willows by the murmuring stream,

Left on your right hand, brings you to the place. But at this hour the house keeps itself.

There is no one within.

OLIVER.

If an eye may profit from a tongue,

Then I should know you by description,

Such garments, and such years. "The boy is fair, of female favor, and carries himself

Like a ripe sister; the woman is short,

And browner than her brother." Are you not

The owners of the house I asked for?

CELIA.

It is no boast, being asked, to say we are.

OLIVER.

Orlando sends his greetings to you both,

And to that youth he calls his Rosalind

He sends this bloody cloth. Are you he?

ROSALIND.

I am. What should we understand by this?

OLIVER.

Some of my shame, if you will know from me

What man I am, and how, and why, and where

This handkerchief was stained.

CELIA.

Please, tell it.

OLIVER.

When young Orlando last parted from you,

He left a promise to return again

Within an hour. And pacing through the forest, chewing the food of sweet and bitter fancy,

Look what happened. He threw his eye aside,

And mark what object presented itself.

Under an oak, whose boughs were mossed with age and whose high top was bald with dry antiquity,

A wretched ragged man, overgrown with hair,

Lay sleeping on his back. About his neck

A green and gilded snake had wreathed itself, who with her head, nimble in threats, approached the opening of his mouth. But suddenly,

Seeing Orlando, it unlinked itself

And with winding glides did slip away

Into a bush. Under that bush's shade

A lioness, with udders all drawn dry,

Lay crouching, head on ground, with catlike watch for when the sleeping man should stir. For it is the royal disposition of that beast

To prey on nothing that seems dead.

Seeing this, Orlando approached the man

And found it was his brother, his elder brother.

CELIA.

O, I have heard him speak of that same brother, and he described him as the most unnatural

That lived among men.

OLIVER.

And well he might do so,

For well I know he was unnatural.

ROSALIND.

But, to Orlando: did he leave him there,

Food for the sucked and hungry lioness?

OLIVER.

Twice did he turn his back and intended so;

But kindness, nobler ever than revenge,

And nature, stronger than his just occasion,

Made him give battle to the lioness,

Who quickly fell before him. In which struggle I awoke from miserable slumber.

CELIA.

Are you his brother?

ROSALIND.

Was it you he rescued?

CELIA.

Was it you that so often plotted to kill him?

OLIVER.

It was I; but it is not I. I do not shame

To tell you what I was, since my conversion

So sweetly tastes, being the thing I am.

ROSALIND.

But what about the bloody cloth?

OLIVER.

In a moment.

When from first to last between us two

Tears had most kindly bathed our recountings— as how I came into that desert place—

In brief, he led me to the gentle Duke,

Who gave me fresh clothing and hospitality,

Committing me unto my brother's love,

Who led me instantly unto his cave,

There stripped himself, and here upon his arm the lioness had torn some flesh away,

Which all this while had bled. And now he fainted, and cried in fainting upon Rosalind.

In brief, I revived him, bound up his wound,

And after some small space, being strong at heart, he sent me here, stranger as I am,

To tell this story, that you might excuse

His broken promise, and to give this cloth,

Dyed in his blood, unto the shepherd youth.

That he jokingly calls his Rosalind.

[Rosalind faints.]

CELIA.

Why, what's wrong, Ganymede, sweet Ganymede!

OLIVER.

Many people faint when they see blood.

CELIA.

There is more to it than that. Cousin—Ganymede!

OLIVER.

Look, he is coming around.

ROSALIND.

I wish I were at home.

CELIA.

We'll lead you there.

Please, will you take him by the arm?

OLIVER.

Cheer up, young man. You call yourself a man? You lack a man's courage.

ROSALIND.

I do, I admit it. Ah, sir, anyone would think this was well acted. Please tell your brother how well I acted. Heigh-ho.

OLIVER.

This was not acting. Your face gives too much proof that this was genuine feeling.

ROSALIND.

Acting, I assure you.

OLIVER.

Well then, take heart, and act like a man.

ROSALIND.

So I do. But, in truth, I should have been a woman by rights.

CELIA.

Come, you look paler and paler. Please let's head home. Good sir, come with us.

OLIVER.

That I will, for I must carry back an answer

About how you excuse my brother, Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

I shall think of something. But please commend my acting to him. Will you come?

[They exit.]

###CHAPTER### Act V

SCENE I. The Forest of Arden

Enter Touchstone and Audrey.

TOUCHSTONE.

We will find a time, Audrey; patience, gentle Audrey.

AUDREY.

Honestly, the priest was good enough, despite what the old gentleman said.

TOUCHSTONE.

A most wicked Sir Oliver, Audrey, a most vile Martext. But Audrey, there is a young man here in the forest who claims you.

AUDREY.

Yes, I know who it is. He has no claim on me in the world.

Enter William.

Here comes the man you mean.

TOUCHSTONE.

It is meat and drink to me to see a clown. By my faith, we who have good wits have much to answer for. We cannot help mocking; we cannot hold back.

WILLIAM.

Good evening, Audrey.

AUDREY.

God give you good evening, William.

WILLIAM.

And good evening to you, sir.

TOUCHSTONE.

Good evening, gentle friend. Cover your head, cover your head. No, please, be covered. How old are you, friend?

WILLIAM.

Twenty-five, sir.

TOUCHSTONE.

A ripe age. Is your name William?

WILLIAM.

William, sir.

TOUCHSTONE.

A fine name. Were you born here in the forest?

WILLIAM.

Yes, sir, I thank God.

TOUCHSTONE.

"Thank God." A good answer. Are you rich?

WILLIAM.

Faith, sir, so-so.

TOUCHSTONE.

"So-so" is good, very good, very excellent good. And yet it is not; it is but so-so. Are you wise?

WILLIAM.

Yes, sir, I have a decent wit.

TOUCHSTONE.

Why, you speak well. I now remember a saying: "The fool thinks he is wise, but the wise man knows himself to be a fool." The pagan philosopher, when he wanted to eat a grape, would open his lips as he put it into his mouth, meaning that grapes were made to eat and lips to open. Do you love this maid?

WILLIAM.

I do, sir.

TOUCHSTONE.

Give me your hand. Are you educated?

WILLIAM.

No, sir.

TOUCHSTONE.

Then learn this from me: to have is to have. For it is a figure in rhetoric that drink, being poured from a cup into a glass, by filling the one does empty the other. For all your writers agree that _ipse_ is "he." Now, you are not _ipse_, for I am he.

WILLIAM.

Which he, sir?

TOUCHSTONE.

He, sir, who must marry this woman. Therefore, you clown, abandon—which in plain language is "leave"—the society—which in rough terms is "company"—of this female—which in common speech is "woman"; which together is, abandon the society of this female, or, clown, you perish; or, to your better understanding, you die; or, in other words, I kill you, make you away, translate your life into death, your liberty into bondage. I will deal in poison with you, or in beating, or in steel. I will contend with you in factions; I will outrun you with cunning. I will kill you a hundred and fifty ways! Therefore tremble and depart.

AUDREY.

Do, good William.

WILLIAM.

God rest you merry, sir.

[_Exit._]

Enter Corin.

CORIN.

Our master and mistress are looking for you. Come away, away.

TOUCHSTONE.

Trip, Audrey, trip, Audrey! I am coming, I am coming.

[_Exeunt._]

SCENE II. Another part of the Forest

Enter Orlando and Oliver.

ORLANDO.

Is it possible that on so little acquaintance you should like her? That just seeing her, you should love her? And loving, woo her? And wooing, she should consent? And will you persist to enjoy her?

OLIVER.

Do not call the rashness of it into question, her poverty, the small acquaintance, my sudden wooing, or her sudden consenting. But say with me, I love Aliena; say with her that she loves me; agree with both that we may enjoy each other. It shall be to your good, for my father's house and all the revenue that was old Sir Rowland's I will settle upon you, and here I will live and die a shepherd.

Enter Rosalind.

ORLANDO.

You have my consent. Let your wedding be tomorrow. I will invite the Duke and all his loyal followers. Go and get Aliena ready, because here comes my Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

God save you, brother.

OLIVER.

And you, fair sister.

[_Exit._]

ROSALIND.

O my dear Orlando, how it grieves me to see you wearing your heart in a scarf!

ORLANDO.

It is my arm.

ROSALIND.

I thought your heart had been wounded by a lion's claws.

ORLANDO.

It is wounded, but by a lady's eyes.

ROSALIND.

Did your brother tell you how I pretended to faint when he showed me your handkerchief?

ORLANDO.

Yes, and greater wonders than that.

ROSALIND.

O, I know what you mean. No, it is true. Nothing was ever so sudden except the fight of two rams, and Caesar's boastful brag of "I came, saw, and conquered." For your brother and my sister no sooner met than they looked at each other; no sooner looked than they loved; no sooner loved than they sighed; no sooner sighed than they asked one another the reason; no sooner knew the reason than they sought the remedy. And in these steps they have built a staircase to marriage, which they will climb immediately, or else be unchaste before marriage. They are in the very heat of love, and they will be together. Clubs cannot separate them.

ORLANDO.

They shall be married tomorrow, and I will invite the Duke to the wedding. But O, how bitter it is to look into happiness through another man's eyes! Tomorrow I shall be all the more heavy-hearted, because I shall think my brother happy in having what he wishes for.

ROSALIND.

Why, then, tomorrow I cannot serve as Rosalind for you?

ORLANDO.

I can live no longer by thinking.

ROSALIND.

Then I will no longer weary you with idle talk. Know from me then—for now I speak with purpose—that I know you are a gentleman of good understanding. I do not say this so that you should think highly of my knowledge, just because I say I know you are. Nor do I seek greater esteem from you than may in some small measure make you believe me, for your own good, and not to honor me. Believe then, if you please, that I can do strange things. Since I was three years old, I have known a magician, most profound in his art and yet not evil. If you love Rosalind so deeply as your behavior shows, when your brother marries Aliena you shall marry her. I know what difficulties of fortune she faces, and it is not impossible for me, if it is not inconvenient for you, to set her before your eyes tomorrow, human as she is, and without any danger.

ORLANDO.

Do you speak in earnest?

ROSALIND.

By my life, I do, which I hold dearly, though I say I am a magician. Therefore put on your best clothes, invite your friends; for if you will be married tomorrow, you shall be, and to Rosalind, if you wish.

Enter Silvius and Phoebe.

Look, here comes a lover of mine and a lover of hers.

PHOEBE.

Young man, you have treated me very rudely

To show the letter that I wrote to you.

ROSALIND.

I do not care if I have; it is my intention

To seem scornful and rude to you.

You are followed there by a faithful shepherd. Look at him, love him; he worships you.

PHOEBE.

Good shepherd, tell this young man what it is to love.

SILVIUS.

It is to be all made of sighs and tears,

And so am I for Phoebe.

PHOEBE.

And I for Ganymede.

ORLANDO.

And I for Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

And I for no woman.

SILVIUS.

It is to be all made of faith and service,

And so am I for Phoebe.

PHOEBE.

And I for Ganymede.

ORLANDO.

And I for Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

And I for no woman.

SILVIUS.

It is to be all made of fantasy,

All made of passion, and all made of wishes,

All adoration, duty, and devotion,

All humbleness, all patience, and impatience, all purity, all trial, all devotion,

And so am I for Phoebe.

PHOEBE.

And so am I for Ganymede.

ORLANDO.

And so am I for Rosalind.

ROSALIND.

And so am I for no woman.

PHOEBE.

[_To Rosalind_.] If this is so, why do you blame me for loving you?

SILVIUS.

[_To Phoebe_.] If this is so, why do you blame me for loving you?

ORLANDO.

If this is so, why do you blame me for loving you?

ROSALIND.

Why do you also say, “Why do you blame me for loving you?”

ORLANDO.

To her that is not here, and cannot hear.

ROSALIND.

Please, no more of this. It is like the howling of Irish wolves against the moon.

[_To Silvius_.] I will help you if I can.

[_To Phoebe_.] I would love you if I could. Tomorrow meet me all together.

[_To Phoebe_.] I will marry you, if I ever marry a woman, and I will be married tomorrow.

[_To Orlando_.] I will satisfy you if I ever satisfied a man, and you shall be married tomorrow.

[_To Silvius_.] I will content you, if what pleases you contents you, and you shall be married tomorrow.

[_To Orlando_.] As you love Rosalind, meet.

[_To Silvius_.] As you love Phoebe, meet. And as I love no woman, I will meet. So farewell. I have left you instructions.

SILVIUS.

I will not fail, if I live.

PHOEBE.

Nor I.

ORLANDO.

Nor I.

[_Exeunt._]

SCENE III. Another part of the Forest

Enter Touchstone and Audrey.

TOUCHSTONE.

Tomorrow is the joyful day, Audrey. Tomorrow we will be married.

AUDREY.

I do desire it with all my heart, and I hope it is no dishonest desire to want to be a woman of the world.

Enter two Pages.

Here come two of the banished Duke’s pages.

FIRST PAGE.

Well met, honest gentleman.

TOUCHSTONE.

By my faith, well met. Come, sit, sit, and a song.

SECOND PAGE.

We are ready for you. Sit in the middle.

FIRST PAGE.

Shall we begin the song briskly, without clearing our throats or spitting or saying we are hoarse, which are the only introductions to a bad voice?

SECOND PAGE.

In faith, in faith, and both in tune like two gypsies on a horse.

SONG

PAGES.

[_Sing_.]

It was a lover and his lass,

With a hey, and a ho, and a hey nonino, that over the green cornfield did pass

In the spring-time, the only pretty ring time, when birds do sing, hey ding a ding, ding. Sweet lovers love the spring.

Between the acres of the rye,

With a hey, and a ho, and a hey nonino, these pretty country folks would lie,

In the spring-time, the only pretty ring time, when birds do sing, hey ding a ding, ding. Sweet lovers love the spring.

This carol they began that hour,

With a hey, and a ho, and a hey nonino, how that a life was but a flower,

In the spring-time, the only pretty ring time, when birds do sing, hey ding a ding, ding. Sweet lovers love the spring.

And therefore take the present time,

With a hey, and a ho, and a hey nonino, for love is crowned with the prime,

In the spring-time, the only pretty ring time, when birds do sing, hey ding a ding, ding. Sweet lovers love the spring.

TOUCHSTONE.

Truly, young gentlemen, though there was nothing special in the song, the singing was very out of tune.

FIRST PAGE.

You are mistaken, sir. We kept time; we did not lose our time.

TOUCHSTONE.

By my faith, yes. I count it time wasted to hear such a foolish song. God be with you, and God improve your voices. Come, Audrey.

[_They exit._]

SCENE IV. Another part of the Forest

Enter Duke Senior, Amiens, Jaques, Orlando, Oliver and Celia.

DUKE SENIOR.

Do you believe, Orlando, that the boy

Can do all this that he has promised?

ORLANDO.

I sometimes believe it and sometimes do not,

Like those who fear what they hope for, and know they fear.

Enter Rosalind, Silvius and Phoebe.

ROSALIND.

Patience once more while our agreement is confirmed. [_To the Duke._] You say that if I bring in your Rosalind, you will give her to Orlando here?

DUKE SENIOR.

That I would, even if I had kingdoms to give with her.

ROSALIND.

[_To Orlando._] And you say you will have her when I bring her?

ORLANDO.

That I would, even if I were king of all kingdoms.

ROSALIND.

[_To Phoebe._] You say you will marry me if I am willing?

PHOEBE.

That I will, even if I should die the hour after.

ROSALIND.

But if you refuse to marry me,

You will give yourself to this most faithful shepherd?

PHOEBE.

That is the bargain.

ROSALIND.

[_To Silvius._] You say that you will have Phoebe if she is willing?

SILVIUS.

Even if having her and death were both one thing.

ROSALIND.

I have promised to make all this matter even. Keep your word, O Duke, to give your daughter. You yours, Orlando, to receive his daughter.

Keep your word, Phoebe, that you will marry me, or else, refusing me, to wed this shepherd.

Keep your word, Silvius, that you will marry her if she refuses me. And from here I go

To make all these uncertainties even.

[_Rosalind and Celia exit._]

DUKE SENIOR.

I do remember in this shepherd boy

Some lively traces of my daughter's appearance.

ORLANDO.

My lord, the first time that I ever saw him

I thought he was a brother to your daughter.

But, my good lord, this boy is forest-born

And has been tutored in the rudiments

Of many desperate studies by his uncle,

Whom he reports to be a great magician,

Hidden in the circle of this forest.

Enter Touchstone and Audrey.

JAQUES.

There is surely another flood coming, and these couples are coming to the ark. Here comes a pair of very strange beasts, which in all tongues are called fools.

TOUCHSTONE.

Salutation and greeting to you all.

JAQUES.

Good my lord, bid him welcome. This is the motley-minded gentleman that I have so often met in the forest. He has been a courtier, he swears.

TOUCHSTONE.

If any man doubts that, let him put me to my trial. I have danced a measure; I have flattered a lady; I have been crafty with my friend, smooth with my enemy; I have ruined three tailors; I have had four quarrels, and nearly fought one.

JAQUES.

And how was that settled?

TOUCHSTONE.

Faith, we met, and found the quarrel was upon the seventh cause.

JAQUES.

How seventh cause?—Good my lord, do you like this fellow?

DUKE SENIOR.

I like him very well.

TOUCHSTONE.

God reward you, sir, I desire the same from you. I press in here, sir, amongst the rest of the country couples, to swear and to break oaths according as marriage binds and passion breaks. A poor virgin, sir, an ugly thing, sir, but my own; a poor whim of mine, sir, to take that which no man else will. Rich honesty dwells like a miser, sir, in a poor house, as your pearl in your foul oyster.

DUKE SENIOR.

By my faith, he is very quick and full of sayings.

TOUCHSTONE.

According to the fool's bolt, sir, and such sweet afflictions.

JAQUES.

But what about the seventh cause? How did you find the quarrel on the seventh cause?

TOUCHSTONE.

It was about a lie seven times removed—stand up straighter, Audrey—as follows, sir. I did not like the cut of a certain courtier’s beard. He sent me word that if I said his beard was not cut well, he was of the opinion that it was. This is called the “retort courteous.” If I sent him word again that it was not well cut, he would send me word that he cut it to please himself. This is called the “quip modest.” If again I said it was not well cut, he dismissed my judgment. This is called the “reply churlish.” If again I said it was not well cut, he would answer that I did not speak the truth. This is called the “reproof valiant.” If again I said it was not well cut, he would say I lied. This is called the “countercheck quarrelsome,” and so on to the “lie circumstantial” and the “lie direct.”

JAQUES.

And how often did you say his beard was not well cut?

TOUCHSTONE.

I dared go no further than the lie circumstantial, and he dared not give me the lie direct; so we measured swords and parted.

JAQUES.

Can you now list in order the degrees of the lie?

TOUCHSTONE.

Oh sir, we quarrel in print, by the book, just as you have books for good manners. I will name you the degrees: the first, the retort courteous; the second, the quip modest; the third, the reply churlish; the fourth, the reproof valiant; the fifth, the countercheck quarrelsome; the sixth, the lie with circumstance; the seventh, the lie direct. You can avoid all of these except the lie direct, and you can avoid that too with an “if.” I knew a case where seven justices could not settle a quarrel, but when the parties met themselves, one of them thought of an “if,” such as, “if you said so, then I said so;” and they shook hands and swore to be brothers. Your “if” is the only peacemaker; there is much virtue in “if.”

JAQUES.

Is this not a remarkable fellow, my lord? He is good at everything, and yet a fool.

DUKE SENIOR.

He uses his folly like a decoy horse, and under the cover of that he shoots his wit.

Enter Hymen, Rosalind in woman’s clothes, and Celia. Soft music.

HYMEN.

Then there is joy in heaven

When earthly things made right

Are reconciled together.

Good Duke, receive your daughter.

Hymen brought her from heaven,

Yes, brought her here,

So that you might join her hand with his, whose heart is within his chest.

ROSALIND.

[_To Duke Senior._] To you I give myself, for I am yours. [_To Orlando._] To you I give myself, for I am yours.

DUKE SENIOR.

If there is truth in sight, you are my daughter.

ORLANDO.

If there is truth in sight, you are my Rosalind.

PHOEBE.

If sight and shape are true,

Why then, my love, farewell.

ROSALIND.

[_To Duke Senior._] I will have no father, if you are not he. [_To Orlando._] I will have no husband, if you are not he. [_To Phoebe._] Nor will I ever wed a woman, if you are not she.

HYMEN.

Peace, now! I forbid confusion.

It is I who must make a conclusion

Of these most strange events.

Here are eight who must take hands

To join in Hymen’s bonds,

If truth holds true contents.

[_To Orlando and Rosalind._] You and you no cross shall part. [_To Celia and Oliver._] You and you are heart in heart. [_To Phoebe._] You must agree to his love

Or have a woman for your lord.

[_To Audrey and Touchstone._] You and you are as sure together as winter is to foul weather.

While we sing a wedding hymn,

Feed yourselves with questioning,

So that reason may diminish wonder

At how we met, and these things finish.

SONG.

Wedding is great Juno’s crown,

O blessed bond of board and bed.

It is Hymen who peoples every town,

High wedlock then be honoured.

Honour, high honour, and renown

To Hymen, god of every town.

DUKE SENIOR.

O my dear niece, welcome to me.

Even as a daughter, welcome in no less degree.

PHOEBE.

[_To Silvius_.] I will not go back on my word, now you are mine. Your faith joins my love to you.

Enter Jaques de Boys.

JAQUES DE BOYS.

Let me have your attention for a word or two.

I am the second son of old Sir Rowland,

And I bring this news to this fair assembly. Duke Frederick, hearing how every day

Men of great worth came to this forest,

Assembled a mighty force, which was on the march

Under his own command, purposely to take

His brother here and put him to the sword.

And he came to the edge of this wild wood,

Where, meeting with an old religious man,

After some conversation with him, he was converted

Both from his enterprise and from the world.

He bequeathed his crown to his banished brother, and restored all their lands to them again

Who were exiled with him. That this is true

I stake my life on.

DUKE SENIOR.

Welcome, young man.

You offer fair gifts to your brothers' weddings: to one, his withheld lands, and to the other

A land itself at large, a powerful dukedom.

First, in this forest let us finish those things

That here were well begun and well conceived;

And afterward, every one of this happy group

Who have endured hard days and nights with us shall share in the good of our restored fortune, according to the measure of their rank.

Meanwhile, forget this newly fallen dignity,

And fall into our rustic celebration.

Play, music! And you brides and bridegrooms all, with joy heaped up in measure, fall to the dance.

JAQUES.

Sir, by your leave. If I heard you rightly, the Duke has taken on a religious life

And thrown into neglect the pompous court.

JAQUES DE BOYS.

He has.

JAQUES.

To him I will go. Out of these converts

There is much matter to be heard and learned. [_To Duke Senior_.] You to your former honour I bequeath; your patience and your virtue well deserve it. [_To Orlando_.] You to a love that your true faith deserves. [_To Oliver_.] You to your land, and love, and great allies. [_To Silvius_.] You to a long and well-deserved bed. [_To Touchstone_.] And you to quarrelling, for your loving journey is only supplied for two months. So to your pleasures; I am for something other than dancing.

DUKE SENIOR.

Stay, Jaques, stay.

JAQUES.

To see no pastime, not I. What you would have

I will stay to learn at your abandoned cave.

[_Exit._]

DUKE SENIOR.

Proceed, proceed! We will begin these rites,

As we trust they will end, in true delights.

[_Dance. Exeunt all but Rosalind._]

###CHAPTER### Epilogue

ROSALIND.

It is not the usual custom for the lady to deliver the epilogue, but it is no more improper than having the lord deliver the prologue. If it is true that good wine needs no advertisement, it is also true that a good play needs no epilogue. Yet people do use good advertisements for good wine, and good plays are made even better with the help of good epilogues. What a difficult situation I am in, then, since I am neither a good epilogue nor able to charm you on behalf of a good play! I am not dressed like a beggar, so begging would not suit me. My approach is to appeal to you earnestly, and I will begin with the women. I ask you, O women, for the love you have for men, to like as much of this play as pleases you. And I ask you, O men, for the love you have for women—as I can tell by your smiling, none of you hates them—that between you and the women, the play may please you. If I were a woman, I would kiss as many of you as had beards that pleased me, faces that I liked, and breath that I did not dislike. And I am sure that as many of you as have good beards, or good faces, or sweet breath will, for my kind offer, bid me farewell when I curtsy.

[_Exit._]
