###TITLE### Frankenstein; or, the modern prometheus
###AUTHOR### Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley

###CHAPTER### Chapter 24

Letter 1

_To Mrs. Saville, England._

St. Petersburgh, Dec. 11th, 17—.

You will be happy to hear that no disaster has marked the beginning of an enterprise you viewed with such dark expectations. I arrived here yesterday, and my first task is to assure my dear sister of my well-being and growing confidence in the success of my undertaking.

I am already far north of London, and as I walk in the streets of Petersburgh, I feel a cold northern breeze play upon my cheeks, which braces my nerves and fills me with delight. Do you understand this feeling? This breeze, which has traveled from the regions toward which I am heading, gives me a foretaste of those icy climates. Inspired by this wind of promise, my daydreams become more passionate and vivid. I try in vain to convince myself that the pole is a place of frost and desolation; it always presents itself to my imagination as a region of beauty and delight. There, Margaret, the sun is always visible, its broad disk just skirting the horizon and spreading a perpetual splendor. There—for with your permission, my sister, I will put some trust in earlier navigators—there snow and frost are banished; and, sailing over a calm sea, we may be carried to a land surpassing in wonders and in beauty every region yet discovered on the habitable globe. Its products and features may be without example, just as the phenomena of the heavenly bodies undoubtedly are in those undiscovered solitudes. What may not be expected in a country of eternal light? I may there discover the wondrous power that attracts the needle and may regulate a thousand celestial observations that require only this voyage to make their seeming irregularities consistent forever. I shall satisfy my burning curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man. These are my enticements, and they are enough to conquer all fear of danger or death and to lead me to begin this laborious voyage with the joy a child feels when he sets out in a little boat, with his holiday companions, on an expedition of discovery up his native river. But even supposing all these guesses to be false, you cannot dispute the priceless benefit I shall confer on all mankind, to the last generation, by discovering a passage near the pole to those countries that currently require so many months to reach; or by determining the secret of the magnet, which, if possible at all, can only be achieved by an undertaking such as mine.

These reflections have dispelled the agitation with which I began my letter, and I feel my heart glow with an enthusiasm which elevates me to heaven, for nothing contributes so much to tranquillise the mind as a steady purpose—a point on which the soul may fix its intellectual eye. This expedition has been the favourite dream of my early years. I have read with ardour the accounts of the various voyages which have been made in the prospect of arriving at the North Pacific Ocean through the seas which surround the pole. You may remember that a history of all the voyages made for purposes of discovery composed the whole of our good Uncle Thomas’ library. My education was neglected, yet I was passionately fond of reading. These volumes were my study day and night, and my familiarity with them increased that regret which I had felt, as a child, on learning that my father’s dying injunction had forbidden my uncle to allow me to embark in a seafaring life.

These visions faded when I perused, for the first time, those poets whose effusions entranced my soul and lifted it to heaven. I also became a poet and for one year lived in a paradise of my own creation; I imagined that I also might obtain a niche in the temple where the names of Homer and Shakespeare are consecrated. You are well acquainted with my failure and how heavily I bore the disappointment. But just at that time I inherited the fortune of my cousin, and my thoughts were turned into the channel of their earlier bent.

Six years have passed since I resolved on my present undertaking. I can, even now, remember the hour from which I dedicated myself to this great enterprise. I commenced by inuring my body to hardship. I accompanied the whale-fishers on several expeditions to the North Sea; I voluntarily endured cold, famine, thirst, and want of sleep; I often worked harder than the common sailors during the day and devoted my nights to the study of mathematics, the theory of medicine, and those branches of physical science from which a naval adventurer might derive the greatest practical advantage. Twice I actually hired myself as an under-mate in a Greenland whaler, and acquitted myself to admiration. I must own I felt a little proud when my captain offered me the second dignity in the vessel and entreated me to remain with the greatest earnestness, so valuable did he consider my services.

And now, dear Margaret, do I not deserve to accomplish some great purpose? My life might have been passed in ease and luxury, but I preferred glory to every enticement that wealth placed in my path. Oh, that some encouraging voice would answer in the affirmative! My courage and my resolution is firm; but my hopes fluctuate, and my spirits are often depressed. I am about to proceed on a long and difficult voyage, the emergencies of which will demand all my fortitude: I am required not only to raise the spirits of others, but sometimes to sustain my own, when theirs are failing.

This is the best time of year for traveling in Russia. They fly quickly over the snow in their sledges; the motion is pleasant, and, in my opinion, far more agreeable than that of an English stagecoach. The cold is not extreme if you are wrapped in furs—a style of dress I have already adopted, for there is a great difference between walking on the deck and remaining seated motionless for hours, when no exercise prevents the blood from actually freezing in your veins. I have no desire to lose my life on the post-road between St. Petersburgh and Archangel.

I shall leave for the latter town in a fortnight or three weeks; and my intention is to hire a ship there, which can easily be done by paying the insurance for the owner, and to hire as many sailors as I think necessary among those who are accustomed to whale-fishing. I do not intend to sail until the month of June; and when shall I return? Ah, dear sister, how can I answer this question? If I succeed, many, many months, perhaps years, will pass before you and I may meet. If I fail, you will see me again soon, or never.

Farewell, my dear, excellent Margaret. May Heaven shower down blessings on you, and save me, so that I may again and again express my gratitude for all your love and kindness.

Your affectionate brother,

R. Walton

Letter 2

_To Mrs. Saville, England._

Archangel, 28th March, 17—.

How slowly the time passes here, surrounded as I am by frost and snow! Yet a second step is taken towards my enterprise. I have hired a vessel and am busy collecting my sailors; those whom I have already hired appear to be men I can depend on and are certainly possessed of fearless courage.

But I have one need which I have never yet been able to satisfy, and the absence of it now feels like a most severe hardship. I have no friend, Margaret: when I am glowing with the excitement of success, there will be no one to share my joy; if I am struck by disappointment, no one will try to support me in my low spirits. I shall commit my thoughts to paper, it is true; but that is a poor medium for communicating feeling. I long for the company of a man who could sympathize with me, whose eyes would answer mine. You may think me romantic, my dear sister, but I bitterly feel the lack of a friend. I have no one near me who is gentle yet courageous, possessed of a cultivated as well as a broad mind, whose tastes are like my own, to approve or improve my plans. How such a friend would repair the faults of your poor brother! I am too eager in carrying out my plans and too impatient with difficulties. But it is an even greater disadvantage to me that I am self-educated: for the first fourteen years of my life I ran wild on the common and read nothing but our Uncle Thomas’ books of voyages. At that age I became acquainted with the celebrated poets of our own country; but it was only when it was no longer in my power to gain the most important benefits from such an awareness that I realized the necessity of becoming acquainted with more languages than that of my native country. Now I am twenty-eight and am in reality more uneducated than many schoolboys of fifteen. It is true that I have thought more and that my daydreams are more extended and magnificent, but they lack (as the painters call it) _keeping;_ and I greatly need a friend who would have enough sense not to dismiss me as romantic, and enough affection for me to try to regulate my mind.

Well, these complaints are useless. I will certainly find no friend on the wide ocean, nor even here in Archangel, among merchants and seamen. Yet some feelings, unconnected to the dross of human nature, beat even in these rugged chests. My lieutenant, for instance, is a man of wonderful courage and enterprise. He is madly desirous of glory, or rather, to phrase it more accurately, of advancement in his profession. He is an Englishman, and in the midst of national and professional prejudices, unsoftened by education, he retains some of the noblest qualities of humanity. I first became acquainted with him on board a whale vessel. Finding that he was unemployed in this city, I easily engaged him to assist in my enterprise.

The master is a person of excellent character and is remarkable on the ship for his gentleness and the mildness of his discipline. This fact, added to his well-known integrity and fearless courage, made me very eager to hire him. A youth passed in solitude, my best years spent under your gentle and feminine care, has so refined the foundation of my character that I cannot overcome an intense distaste for the usual brutality practiced on board ship. I have never believed it to be necessary. When I heard of a sailor equally noted for his kindliness of heart and the respect and obedience paid to him by his crew, I felt myself especially fortunate in being able to secure his services. I first heard of him in rather a romantic manner, from a lady who owes the happiness of her life to him. This, briefly, is his story. Some years ago he loved a young Russian lady of modest fortune. Having amassed a considerable sum in prize-money, the girl’s father consented to the match. He saw his intended bride once before the planned ceremony, but she was bathed in tears. Throwing herself at his feet, she begged him to spare her, confessing at the same time that she loved another, but that he was poor, and that her father would never consent to the union. My generous friend reassured the pleading woman, and on being informed of the name of her lover, instantly abandoned his pursuit. He had already bought a farm with his money, on which he had designed to pass the remainder of his life. But he gave the whole to his rival, together with the remains of his prize-money to purchase stock, and then himself urged the young woman’s father to consent to her marriage with her lover. But the old man firmly refused, thinking himself bound in honor to my friend. When my friend found the father unyielding, he left his country, and did not return until he heard that his former beloved was married according to her wishes. “What a noble fellow!” you will exclaim. He is so, but then he is wholly uneducated. He is as silent as a Turk, and a kind of ignorant carelessness attends him, which, while it makes his conduct more astonishing, detracts from the interest and sympathy which otherwise he would command.

But do not think that I am wavering in my resolutions just because I complain a little, or because I can imagine a comfort for my struggles that I may never know. Those resolutions are as fixed as fate. My voyage is only delayed until the weather allows me to set sail. The winter has been dreadfully severe, but the spring promises well, and it is considered a remarkably early season. Perhaps I may sail sooner than I expected. I shall do nothing rashly. You know me well enough to trust in my prudence and carefulness whenever the safety of others is committed to my care.

I cannot describe to you my feelings at the near prospect of my undertaking. It is impossible to communicate to you the trembling sensation, half pleasurable and half fearful, with which I am preparing to depart. I am going to unexplored regions, to "the land of mist and snow," but I shall kill no albatross. Therefore do not be alarmed for my safety, or if I should come back to you as worn and woeful as the "Ancient Mariner." You will smile at my allusion, but I will disclose a secret. I have often attributed my attachment to, my passionate enthusiasm for, the dangerous mysteries of ocean to that production of the most imaginative of modern poets. There is something at work in my soul which I do not understand. I am practically industrious—painstaking, a workman who executes with perseverance and labour—but besides this there is a love for the marvellous, a belief in the marvellous, intertwined in all my projects, which hurries me out of the common pathways of men, even to the wild sea and unvisited regions I am about to explore.

But to return to dearer considerations. Shall I meet you again, after having crossed immense seas and returned by the most southern cape of Africa or America? I dare not expect such success, yet I cannot bear to look on the reverse of the picture. Continue for the present to write to me by every opportunity. I may receive your letters on some occasions when I need them most to support my spirits. I love you very tenderly. Remember me with affection, should you never hear from me again.

Your affectionate brother,

Robert Walton

Letter 3

To Mrs. Saville, England.

July 7th, 17—.

My dear Sister,

I write a few lines in haste to say that I am safe—and well advanced on my voyage. This letter will reach England by a merchant ship now on its homeward voyage from Archangel; more fortunate than I, who may not see my native land, perhaps, for many years. I am, however, in good spirits. My men are bold and apparently firm of purpose. Nor do the floating sheets of ice that continually pass us, indicating the dangers of the region towards which we are advancing, appear to dismay them. We have already reached a very high latitude. But it is the height of summer, and although not so warm as in England, the southern gales, which blow us speedily towards those shores which I so ardently desire to attain, breathe a degree of refreshing warmth which I had not expected.

No incidents have so far happened to us that would make a figure in a letter. One or two stiff gales and the springing of a leak are accidents which experienced navigators scarcely remember to record. I shall be well content if nothing worse happens to us during our voyage.

Adieu, my dear Margaret. Be assured that for my own sake, as well as yours, I will not rashly encounter danger. I will be cool, persevering, and prudent.

But success shall crown my efforts. Why should it not? So far I have gone, tracing a safe path over the trackless seas, with the very stars themselves as witnesses to my triumph. Why not continue over the untamed yet obedient water? What can stop the determined heart and resolved will of a man?

My swelling heart pours itself out like this without my meaning it to. But I must finish. Heaven bless my beloved sister!

R.W.

Letter 4

To Mrs. Saville, England.

August 5th, 17—.

Such a strange accident has happened to us that I cannot keep from recording it, although it is very likely that you will see me before these papers can reach you.

Last Monday (July 31st) we were nearly surrounded by ice, which closed in the ship on all sides, scarcely leaving her enough room to float. Our situation was somewhat dangerous, especially as we were wrapped in a very thick fog. We therefore stopped the ship, hoping that some change would take place in the atmosphere and weather.

About two o’clock the mist cleared away, and we saw, stretched out in every direction, vast and irregular plains of ice that seemed to have no end. Some of my comrades groaned, and my own mind began to grow restless with anxious thoughts, when a strange sight suddenly attracted our attention and drew our concern away from our own situation. We saw a low carriage, fixed on a sledge and drawn by dogs, pass on toward the north at a distance of half a mile. A being with the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic size, sat in the sledge and guided the dogs. We watched the rapid progress of the traveler with our telescopes until he was lost among the distant uneven shapes of the ice.

This sight filled us with complete wonder. We believed we were many hundred miles from any land, but this apparition seemed to suggest that the land was not, in reality, as far away as we had supposed. Shut in, however, by ice, it was impossible to follow his track, which we had observed with the greatest attention.

About two hours after this event we heard the distant sound of the sea under the ice, and before night the ice broke and freed our ship. We stopped until morning, however, fearing to hit in the dark those large loose masses that float about after the ice breaks up. I used this time to rest for a few hours.

In the morning, as soon as it was light, I went on deck and found all the sailors busy on one side of the vessel, apparently talking to someone in the sea. It was, in fact, a sledge like the one we had seen before, which had drifted toward us during the night on a large piece of ice. Only one dog remained alive, but there was a human being inside it whom the sailors were persuading to come aboard the ship. He was not, as the other traveler seemed to be, a savage inhabitant of some undiscovered island, but a European. When I appeared on deck the master said, “Here is our captain, and he will not allow you to perish on the open sea.”

On seeing me, the stranger spoke to me in English, although with a foreign accent. “Before I come on board your vessel,” he said, “will you be kind enough to tell me where you are bound?”

You can imagine my astonishment at hearing such a question from a man on the brink of destruction. I would have supposed that my vessel would have been a resource he would not have exchanged for the most precious wealth the earth can offer. I replied, however, that we were on a voyage of discovery towards the northern pole.

Upon hearing this he appeared satisfied and agreed to come on board. Good God! Margaret, if you had seen the man who thus surrendered for his safety, your surprise would have been boundless. His limbs were nearly frozen, and his body was dreadfully wasted by fatigue and suffering. I never saw a man in so wretched a condition. We attempted to carry him into the cabin, but as soon as he had left the fresh air he fainted. We accordingly brought him back to the deck and revived him by rubbing him with brandy and forcing him to swallow a small quantity. As soon as he showed signs of life we wrapped him up in blankets and placed him near the chimney of the kitchen stove. By slow degrees he recovered and ate a little soup, which restored him wonderfully.

Two days passed in this manner before he was able to speak, and I often feared that his sufferings had deprived him of his reason. When he had in some measure recovered, I moved him to my own cabin and attended to him as much as my duty would permit. I never saw a more interesting creature: his eyes generally have an expression of wildness, and even madness, but there are moments when, if anyone performs an act of kindness towards him or does him any trifling service, his whole face lights up with a beam of benevolence and sweetness that I never saw equalled. But he is generally melancholy and despairing, and sometimes he gnashes his teeth, as if impatient of the weight of woes that oppresses him.

When my guest was a little recovered I had great trouble keeping off the men, who wished to ask him a thousand questions. But I would not allow him to be tormented by their idle curiosity, in a state of body and mind whose restoration evidently depended upon complete rest. Once, however, the lieutenant asked why he had come so far upon the ice in so strange a vehicle.

His face instantly assumed an aspect of the deepest gloom, and he replied, “To seek one who fled from me.”

“And did the man whom you pursued travel in the same fashion?”

“Yes.”

“Then I fancy we have seen him, for the day before we picked you up we saw some dogs drawing a sledge, with a man in it, across the ice.”

This aroused the stranger’s attention, and he asked a multitude of questions concerning the route which the demon, as he called him, had pursued. Soon after, when he was alone with me, he said, “I have, doubtless, excited your curiosity, as well as that of these good people; but you are too considerate to make inquiries.”

“Certainly; it would indeed be very impertinent and inhuman of me to trouble you with any inquisitiveness of mine.”

“And yet you rescued me from a strange and perilous situation; you have benevolently restored me to life.”

Soon after this he asked if I thought that the breaking up of the ice had destroyed the other sledge. I replied that I could not answer with any degree of certainty, for the ice had not broken until near midnight, and the traveller might have arrived at a place of safety before that time; but of this I could not judge.

From this time a new spirit of life animated the decaying frame of the stranger. He showed the greatest eagerness to be on deck to watch for the sledge that had appeared before, but I persuaded him to remain in the cabin, for he is far too weak to bear the rawness of the atmosphere. I promised that someone would watch for him and give him instant notice if any new object should appear in sight.

Such is my journal of what relates to this strange occurrence up to the present day. The stranger has gradually improved in health but is very silent and appears uneasy when anyone except myself enters his cabin. Yet his manners are so conciliating and gentle that the sailors are all interested in him, although they have had very little communication with him. For my own part, I begin to love him as a brother, and his constant and deep grief fills me with sympathy and compassion. He must have been a noble creature in his better days, being even now in wreck so attractive and amiable.

I said in one of my letters, my dear Margaret, that I should find no friend on the wide ocean; yet I have found a man who, before his spirit had been broken by misery, I should have been happy to have possessed as the brother of my heart.

I shall continue my journal concerning the stranger at intervals, should I have any fresh incidents to record.

August 13th, 17—.

My affection for my guest increases every day. He excites at once my admiration and my pity to an astonishing degree. How can I see so noble a creature destroyed by misery without feeling the most poignant grief? He is so gentle, yet so wise; his mind is so cultivated, and when he speaks, although his words are chosen with the choicest art, yet they flow with rapidity and unparalleled eloquence.

He is now much recovered from his illness and is continually on the deck, apparently watching for the sledge that preceded his own. Yet, although unhappy, he is not so utterly occupied by his own misery that he does not interest himself deeply in the projects of others. He has frequently conversed with me on mine, which I have communicated to him without disguise. He entered attentively into all my arguments in favour of my eventual success and into every minute detail of the measures I had taken to secure it. I was easily led by the sympathy which he showed to use the language of my heart, to give utterance to the burning ardour of my soul and to say, with all the fervour that warmed me, how gladly I would sacrifice my fortune, my existence, my every hope, to the furtherance of my enterprise. One man’s life or death were but a small price to pay for the acquirement of the knowledge which I sought, for the dominion I should acquire and transmit over the elemental foes of our race. As I spoke, a dark gloom spread over my listener’s face. At first I perceived that he tried to suppress his emotion; he placed his hands before his eyes, and my voice quivered and failed me as I beheld tears trickle fast from between his fingers; a groan burst from his heaving breast. I paused; at length he spoke, in broken accents: “Unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk also of the intoxicating draught? Hear me; let me reveal my tale, and you will dash the cup from your lips!”

Such words, you may imagine, strongly excited my curiosity; but the fit of grief that had seized the stranger overcame his weakened powers, and many hours of rest and calm conversation were necessary to restore his composure.

Having conquered the violence of his feelings, he appeared to despise himself for being the slave of passion; and quelling the dark tyranny of despair, he led me again to talk about myself. He asked me about my earlier years. The tale was quickly told, but it awakened various trains of thought. I spoke of my desire to find a friend, of my thirst for a more intimate sympathy with another mind than had ever fallen to my lot, and expressed my conviction that a man could boast of little happiness who did not enjoy this blessing.

"I agree with you," replied the stranger; "we are unfinished creatures, only half made up, if one wiser, better, dearer than ourselves—such a friend ought to be—does not lend his aid to perfect our weak and faulty natures. I once had a friend, the most noble of human creatures, and I am entitled, therefore, to judge about friendship. You have hope, and the world before you, and no cause for despair. But I—I have lost everything and cannot begin life anew."

As he said this his face became expressive of a calm, settled grief that touched me to the heart. But he was silent and presently retired to his cabin.

Even broken in spirit as he is, no one can feel more deeply than he does the beauties of nature. The starry sky, the sea, and every sight afforded by these wonderful regions seem still to have the power of lifting his soul from earth. Such a man has a double existence: he may suffer misery and be overwhelmed by disappointments, yet when he has withdrawn into himself, he will be like a celestial spirit with a halo around him, within whose circle no grief or folly dares to enter.

Will you smile at the enthusiasm I express about this divine wanderer? You would not if you saw him. You have been educated and refined by books and retirement from the world, and you are therefore somewhat particular; but this only makes you more fit to appreciate the extraordinary merits of this wonderful man. Sometimes I have tried to discover what quality he possesses that raises him so immeasurably above any other person I ever knew. I believe it to be an intuitive discernment, a quick but never-failing power of judgment, a penetration into the causes of things, unequalled for clearness and precision; add to this a facility of expression and a voice whose varied intonations are soul-subduing music.

August 19th, 17—.

Yesterday the stranger said to me, “You may easily perceive, Captain Walton, that I have suffered great and unparalleled misfortunes. I had determined at one time that the memory of these evils should die with me, but you have won me to alter my determination. You seek for knowledge and wisdom, as I once did; and I ardently hope that the gratification of your wishes may not be a serpent to sting you, as mine has been. I do not know that the relation of my disasters will be useful to you; yet, when I reflect that you are pursuing the same course, exposing yourself to the same dangers which have rendered me what I am, I imagine that you may deduce an apt moral from my tale, one that may direct you if you succeed in your undertaking and console you in case of failure. Prepare to hear of occurrences which are usually deemed marvellous. Were we among the tamer scenes of nature I might fear to encounter your unbelief, perhaps your ridicule; but many things will appear possible in these wild and mysterious regions which would provoke the laughter of those unacquainted with the ever-varied powers of nature; nor can I doubt but that my tale conveys in its series internal evidence of the truth of the events of which it is composed.”

You may easily imagine that I was much gratified by the offered communication, yet I could not endure that he should renew his grief by a recital of his misfortunes. I felt the greatest eagerness to hear the promised narrative, partly from curiosity and partly from a strong desire to ameliorate his fate if it were in my power. I expressed these feelings in my answer.

“I thank you,” he replied, “for your sympathy, but it is useless; my fate is nearly fulfilled. I wait but for one event, and then I shall repose in peace. I understand your feeling,” continued he, perceiving that I wished to interrupt him; “but you are mistaken, my friend, if thus you will allow me to name you; nothing can alter my destiny; listen to my history, and you will perceive how irrevocably it is determined.”

He then told me that he would commence his narrative the next day when I should be at leisure. This promise drew from me the warmest thanks. I have resolved every night, when I am not imperatively occupied by my duties, to record, as nearly as possible in his own words, what he has related during the day. If I should be engaged, I will at least make notes. This manuscript will doubtless afford you the greatest pleasure; but to me, who know him, and who hear it from his own lips—with what interest and sympathy shall I read it in some future day! Even now, as I commence my task, his full-toned voice swells in my ears; his lustrous eyes dwell on me with all their melancholy sweetness; I see his thin hand raised in animation, while the lineaments of his face are irradiated by the soul within. Strange and harrowing must be his story, frightful the storm which embraced the gallant vessel on its course and wrecked it—thus!

###CHAPTER### Chapter 1

I am by birth a Genevese, and my family is one of the most distinguished of that republic. My ancestors had been for many years counsellors and syndics, and my father had filled several public situations with honour and reputation. He was respected by all who knew him for his integrity and tireless attention to public business. He spent his younger days constantly occupied by the affairs of his country. A variety of circumstances had prevented his marrying early, and it was not until the decline of life that he became a husband and the father of a family.

As the circumstances of his marriage illustrate his character, I cannot refrain from relating them. One of his most intimate friends was a merchant who, from a flourishing state, fell, through numerous mischances, into poverty. This man, whose name was Beaufort, was of a proud and unbending disposition and could not bear to live in poverty and obscurity in the same country where he had formerly been distinguished for his rank and magnificence. Having paid his debts, therefore, in the most honourable manner, he retreated with his daughter to the town of Lucerne, where he lived unknown and in wretchedness. My father loved Beaufort with the truest friendship and was deeply grieved by his retreat in these unfortunate circumstances. He bitterly deplored the false pride which led his friend to a conduct so little worthy of the affection that united them. He lost no time in endeavouring to seek him out, with the hope of persuading him to begin the world again through his credit and assistance.

Beaufort had taken effective measures to conceal himself, and it was ten months before my father discovered his home. Overjoyed at this discovery, he hastened to the house, which was situated in a mean street near the Reuss. But when he entered, misery and despair alone welcomed him. Beaufort had saved but a very small sum of money from the wreck of his fortunes, but it was sufficient to provide him with food for some months, and in the meantime he hoped to obtain some respectable employment in a merchant's house. The interval was, consequently, spent in inaction. His grief only became more deep and bitter when he had leisure for reflection, and at length it took so fast hold of his mind that at the end of three months he lay on a bed of sickness, incapable of any exertion.

His daughter attended him with the greatest tenderness, but she saw with despair that their little fund was rapidly decreasing and that there was no other prospect of support. But Caroline Beaufort possessed a mind of an uncommon kind, and her courage rose to support her in her adversity. She obtained plain work. She plaited straw and by various means managed to earn a pittance scarcely sufficient to support life.

Several months passed in this manner. Her father grew worse; her time was more entirely occupied in attending him; her means of subsistence decreased; and in the tenth month her father died in her arms, leaving her an orphan and a beggar. This last blow overcame her, and she knelt by Beaufort's coffin weeping bitterly, when my father entered the chamber. He came like a protecting spirit to the poor girl, who committed herself to his care. After the burial of his friend he conducted her to Geneva and placed her under the protection of a relation. Two years after this event Caroline became his wife.

There was a large age gap between my parents, but this seemed only to bring them closer in devoted love. My father had such a strong sense of justice that he needed to approve deeply before he could love strongly. Perhaps he had once been hurt by discovering someone unworthy of his love, and so he valued proven worth all the more. His attachment to my mother was full of gratitude and admiration, quite unlike the doting fondness of old age. It was inspired by respect for her virtues and a wish to repay her, in some measure, for the sorrows she had suffered. This gave an inexpressible grace to his treatment of her. Everything was arranged to suit her wishes and her comfort. He tried to shelter her, as a gardener shelters a rare exotic flower, from every rough wind. He surrounded her with everything that might bring pleasure to her gentle and kind heart. Her health, and even the calm of her usually steady spirit, had been shaken by what she had gone through. During the two years before their marriage, my father had gradually given up all his public duties. Immediately after their wedding, they went to Italy, seeking its pleasant climate and the change of scene that came with touring that land of wonders, as a way to restore her weakened body.

From Italy they traveled to Germany and France. I, their eldest child, was born in Naples and accompanied them on their travels as an infant. For several years I remained their only child. Much as they loved each other, they seemed to draw endless affection from a deep mine of love to give to me. My mother’s tender caresses and my father’s kind smile of pleasure as he looked at me are my first memories. I was their plaything and their idol, and something more—their child, the innocent and helpless creature given to them by Heaven. It was their duty to raise me well, and their choices would direct my future toward happiness or misery. With this deep awareness of what they owed to the life they had created, along with the active tenderness that filled them both, it is easy to imagine that while every hour of my infancy taught me patience, charity, and self-control, I was guided so gently that it all seemed like one continuous stream of joy to me.

For a long time I was their only child. My mother had wanted a daughter very much, but I remained their only offspring. When I was about five years old, while traveling beyond the borders of Italy, they spent a week on the shores of Lake Como. Their kind nature often led them into the cottages of the poor. For my mother, this was more than a duty; it was a need, a passion. Remembering what she had suffered and how she had been helped, she felt compelled to act as a guardian angel to the afflicted in her turn. During one of their walks, a poor cottage in the folds of a valley caught their attention because it looked so bleak. The number of half-clothed children gathered around it spoke of extreme poverty. One day, when my father had gone alone to Milan, my mother, accompanied by me, visited this home. She found a peasant and his wife, hardworking and weighed down by care and labor, sharing a meager meal with five hungry children. Among them, one child attracted my mother far more than the rest. She seemed of a different background. The four others were dark-eyed, sturdy little wanderers; this child was thin and very fair. Her hair was the brightest living gold, and despite her poor clothing, it seemed to set a crown of distinction on her head. Her brow was clear and broad, her blue eyes cloudless, and her lips and the shape of her face so expressive of sensitivity and sweetness that no one could look at her without seeing her as a different kind of being—someone heaven-sent, bearing a celestial mark in all her features.

The peasant woman, noticing that my mother gazed with wonder and admiration at this lovely girl, eagerly told her story. She was not her child, but the daughter of a Milanese nobleman. Her mother was German and had died giving birth to her. The infant had been placed with these good people to nurse. They were better off then. They had not been married long, and their eldest child had just been born. The father of their charge was one of those Italians raised on the memory of Italy's ancient glory—one among the _schiavi ognor frementi_, who worked to win his country's freedom. He became a victim of its weakness. Whether he had died or still lingered in the dungeons of Austria was unknown. His property was confiscated; his child became an orphan and a beggar. She stayed with her foster parents and blossomed in their rough home, fairer than a garden rose among dark-leaved brambles.

When my father returned from Milan, he found playing with me in the hall of our villa a child fairer than a painted cherub—a creature who seemed to radiate light from her face and whose form and movements were lighter than the chamois of the hills. The apparition was soon explained. With his permission, my mother persuaded her rustic guardians to give up their charge to her. They were fond of the sweet orphan. Her presence had seemed a blessing to them, but it would be unfair to her to keep her in poverty and want when Providence offered her such powerful protection. They consulted their village priest, and the result was that Elizabeth Lavenza became a member of my parents' household—my more than sister—the beautiful and adored companion of all my activities and pleasures.

Everyone loved Elizabeth. The passionate and almost reverential attachment with which all regarded her became, while I shared it, my pride and my delight. On the evening before she was brought to my home, my mother had said playfully, “I have a pretty present for my Victor—tomorrow he shall have it.” And when, the next day, she presented Elizabeth to me as her promised gift, I, with childish seriousness, interpreted her words literally and looked upon Elizabeth as mine—mine to protect, love, and cherish. All praises given to her I received as made to a possession of my own. We called each other familiarly by the name of cousin. No word, no expression could capture the kind of relation in which she stood to me—my more than sister, since until death she was to be mine only.

We were brought up together, and there was not quite a year's difference in our ages. I need not say that we were strangers to any kind of disunion or dispute. Harmony was the soul of our companionship, and the diversity and contrast between our characters drew us closer together. Elizabeth had a calmer and more focused disposition. But for all my passion, I was capable of more intense application and was more deeply filled with the thirst for knowledge. She busied herself with following the airy creations of the poets. And in the majestic and wondrous scenes around our Swiss home—the sublime shapes of the mountains, the changes of the seasons, storm and calm, the silence of winter, and the life and turbulence of our Alpine summers—she found ample room for admiration and delight. While my companion contemplated the magnificent appearances of things with a serious and satisfied spirit, I delighted in investigating their causes. The world was to me a secret which I desired to discover. Curiosity, earnest research to learn the hidden laws of nature, and a gladness close to rapture as they were revealed to me are among the earliest sensations I can remember.

When a second son was born, seven years younger than me, my parents gave up their wandering life entirely and settled in their native country. We had a house in Geneva and a country home on Belrive, the eastern shore of the lake, a little more than a league from the city. We lived mainly in the latter, and my parents' lives were passed in considerable seclusion. It was my nature to avoid crowds and to attach myself warmly to a few people. I was therefore indifferent to my schoolfellows in general, but I formed the closest friendship with one of them. Henry Clerval was the son of a merchant of Geneva. He was a boy of singular talent and imagination. He loved adventure, hardship, and even danger for its own sake. He was deeply read in books of chivalry and romance. He composed heroic songs and began to write many a tale of enchantment and knightly adventure. He tried to make us act plays and take part in masquerades, in which the characters were drawn from the heroes of Roncesvalles, of King Arthur's Round Table, and the chivalrous company who shed their blood to redeem the holy sepulchre from the hands of the infidels.

No human being could have passed a happier childhood than I did. My parents were filled with the very spirit of kindness and indulgence. We felt that they were not tyrants ruling our lives according to their whims, but the agents and creators of all the many delights we enjoyed. When I mixed with other families, I clearly saw how especially fortunate my situation was, and gratitude helped the growth of my love for my parents.

My temper was sometimes violent, and my emotions intense; but by some rule of my nature they were turned not toward childish pursuits but toward an eager desire to learn, and not to learn all things indiscriminately. I admit that neither the structure of languages, nor the laws of governments, nor the politics of various states held any attraction for me. It was the secrets of heaven and earth that I desired to learn; and whether it was the outward substance of things or the inner spirit of nature and the mysterious soul of man that occupied me, still my inquiries were directed to the metaphysical, or in its highest sense, the physical secrets of the world.

Meanwhile Clerval occupied himself, so to speak, with the moral relations of things. The busy stage of life, the virtues of heroes, and the actions of men were his theme; and his hope and his dream was to become one among those whose names are recorded in story as the gallant and adventurous benefactors of our species. The saintly soul of Elizabeth shone like a shrine-dedicated lamp in our peaceful home. Her sympathy was ours; her smile, her soft voice, the sweet glance of her heavenly eyes, were ever there to bless and encourage us. She was the living spirit of love to soften and attract; I might have become sullen in my study, rough through the passion of my nature, had she not been there to subdue me to a semblance of her own gentleness. And Clerval—could anything ill take hold of the noble spirit of Clerval? Yet he might not have been so perfectly humane, so thoughtful in his generosity, so full of kindness and tenderness amidst his passion for adventurous deeds, had she not shown him the real loveliness of doing good and made the doing of good the end and aim of his soaring ambition.

I feel exquisite pleasure in dwelling on the memories of childhood, before misfortune had tainted my mind and changed its bright visions of widespread usefulness into gloomy and narrow reflections upon myself. Besides, in drawing the picture of my early days, I also record those events which led, by imperceptible steps, to my later tale of misery, for when I would explain to myself the birth of that passion which afterward ruled my destiny, I find it arise, like a mountain river, from humble and almost forgotten sources; but, swelling as it went, it became the torrent which, in its course, has swept away all my hopes and joys.

Natural philosophy is the spirit that has governed my fate; I desire, therefore, in this narration, to state those facts which led to my preference for that science. When I was thirteen years of age we all went on a pleasure trip to the baths near Thonon; the harshness of the weather forced us to remain a day confined to the inn. In this house I happened to find a volume of the works of Cornelius Agrippa. I opened it with indifference; the theory which he attempts to demonstrate and the wonderful facts which he relates soon changed this feeling into enthusiasm. A new light seemed to dawn upon my mind, and bounding with joy, I told my discovery to my father. My father looked carelessly at the title page of my book and said, “Ah! Cornelius Agrippa! My dear Victor, do not waste your time upon this; it is sad trash.”

If, instead of this remark, my father had taken the pains to explain to me that the principles of Agrippa had been entirely exploded and that a modern system of science had been introduced which possessed much greater powers than the ancient, because the powers of the latter were chimerical, while those of the former were real and practical, under such circumstances I should certainly have thrown Agrippa aside and have contented my imagination, warmed as it was, by returning with greater ardour to my former studies. It is even possible that the train of my ideas would never have received the fatal impulse that led to my ruin. But the cursory glance my father had taken of my volume by no means assured me that he was acquainted with its contents, and I continued to read with the greatest avidity.

When I returned home my first care was to procure the whole works of this author, and afterwards of Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus. I read and studied the wild fancies of these writers with delight; they appeared to me treasures known to few besides myself. I have described myself as always having been imbued with a fervent longing to penetrate the secrets of nature. In spite of the intense labour and wonderful discoveries of modern philosophers, I always came from my studies discontented and unsatisfied. Sir Isaac Newton is said to have avowed that he felt like a child picking up shells beside the great and unexplored ocean of truth. Those of his successors in each branch of natural philosophy with whom I was acquainted appeared even to my boy’s apprehensions as tyros engaged in the same pursuit.

The untaught peasant beheld the elements around him and was acquainted with their practical uses. The most learned philosopher knew little more. He had partially unveiled the face of Nature, but her immortal lineaments were still a wonder and a mystery. He might dissect, anatomise, and give names; but, not to speak of a final cause, causes in their secondary and tertiary grades were utterly unknown to him. I had gazed upon the fortifications and impediments that seemed to keep human beings from entering the citadel of nature, and rashly and ignorantly I had repined.

But here were books, and here were men who had penetrated deeper and knew more. I took their word for all that they averred, and I became their disciple. It may appear strange that such should arise in the eighteenth century; but while I followed the routine of education in the schools of Geneva, I was, to a great degree, self-taught with regard to my favourite studies. My father was not scientific, and I was left to struggle with a child’s blindness, added to a student’s thirst for knowledge. Under the guidance of my new preceptors I entered with the greatest diligence into the search of the philosopher’s stone and the elixir of life; but the latter soon obtained my undivided attention. Wealth was an inferior object, but what glory would attend the discovery if I could banish disease from the human frame and render man invulnerable to any but a violent death!

Nor were these my only visions. My favorite authors freely promised the raising of ghosts or devils, and I eagerly sought to fulfill that promise. If my spells were always unsuccessful, I blamed my own inexperience and mistakes rather than any lack of skill or truthfulness in my teachers. And so for a time I was occupied by discredited systems, mixing a thousand contradictory theories like a beginner, and floundering desperately in a swamp of scattered knowledge, guided by an eager imagination and childish reasoning. Then an accident again changed the direction of my ideas.

When I was about fifteen years old, we had gone to our house near Belrive when we witnessed a most violent and terrible thunderstorm. It advanced from behind the Jura mountains, and the thunder burst at once with frightful loudness from various parts of the sky. While the storm lasted, I watched its progress with curiosity and delight. As I stood at the door, I suddenly saw a stream of fire shoot from an old and beautiful oak tree that stood about twenty yards from our house. As soon as the dazzling light vanished, the oak had disappeared, and nothing remained but a blasted stump. When we visited it the next morning, we found the tree shattered in a strange way. It was not splintered by the shock, but entirely reduced to thin ribbons of wood. I had never seen anything so utterly destroyed.

Before this I was not unfamiliar with the more obvious laws of electricity. On this occasion a man of great research in natural philosophy was with us. Excited by this catastrophe, he began to explain a theory he had formed on the subject of electricity and galvanism, which was at once new and astonishing to me. All that he said greatly overshadowed Cornelius Agrippa, Albertus Magnus, and Paracelsus, the lords of my imagination. But by some misfortune, the overthrow of these men made me unwilling to continue my usual studies. It seemed to me as if nothing would or could ever be known. All that had so long engaged my attention suddenly grew worthless. By one of those whims of the mind to which we are perhaps most subject in early youth, I at once gave up my former occupations. I set down natural history and all its offspring as a deformed and failed creation, and I felt the greatest disdain for a would-be science that could never even step within the threshold of real knowledge. In this mood I turned to mathematics and the branches of study belonging to that science, as being built upon secure foundations and so worthy of my consideration.

Thus strangely are our souls constructed, and by such slight ties are we bound to prosperity or ruin. When I look back, it seems to me as if this almost miraculous change of inclination and will was the immediate suggestion of the guardian angel of my life—the last effort made by the spirit of preservation to turn away the storm that was even then hanging in the stars and ready to surround me. Her victory was announced by an unusual calmness and gladness of soul that followed the giving up of my old and lately tormenting studies. It was in this way that I was to be taught to connect evil with pursuing them, and happiness with ignoring them.

It was a strong effort of the spirit of good, but it was useless. Destiny was too powerful, and its unchangeable laws had ordered my complete and terrible destruction.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 3

When I turned seventeen, my parents decided I should become a student at the university of Ingolstadt. Until then I had attended the schools of Geneva, but my father thought it necessary for the completion of my education that I should become acquainted with customs other than those of my native country. My departure was therefore set for an early date, but before that day could arrive, the first misfortune of my life occurred—an omen, as it were, of my future misery.

Elizabeth had caught scarlet fever; her illness was severe, and she was in the greatest danger. During her illness many arguments had been made to persuade my mother to refrain from attending upon her. She had at first yielded to our pleas, but when she heard that the life of her favourite was threatened, she could no longer control her anxiety. She attended her sickbed; her watchful attentions triumphed over the malignancy of the disease—Elizabeth was saved, but the consequences of this imprudence were fatal to her preserver. On the third day my mother fell ill; her fever was accompanied by the most alarming symptoms, and the looks of her medical attendants predicted the worst outcome. On her deathbed the fortitude and kindness of this best of women did not desert her. She joined the hands of Elizabeth and myself. “My children,” she said, “my firmest hopes of future happiness were placed on the prospect of your union. This expectation will now be the consolation of your father. Elizabeth, my love, you must supply my place to my younger children. Alas! I regret that I am taken from you; and, happy and beloved as I have been, is it not hard to quit you all? But these are not thoughts befitting me; I will endeavour to resign myself cheerfully to death and will indulge a hope of meeting you in another world.”

She died calmly, and her countenance expressed affection even in death. I need not describe the feelings of those whose dearest ties are torn by that most irreparable evil, the void that presents itself to the soul, and the despair that is exhibited on the countenance. It is so long before the mind can persuade itself that she whom we saw every day and whose very existence appeared a part of our own can have departed forever—that the brightness of a beloved eye can have been extinguished and the sound of a voice so familiar and dear to the ear can be hushed, never more to be heard. These are the reflections of the first days; but when the passage of time proves the reality of the evil, then the actual bitterness of grief begins. Yet from whom has not that rude hand torn away some dear connection? And why should I describe a sorrow which all have felt, and must feel? The time at length arrives when grief is rather an indulgence than a necessity; and the smile that plays upon the lips, although it may be deemed a sacrilege, is not banished. My mother was dead, but we still had duties which we ought to perform; we must continue our course with the rest and learn to think ourselves fortunate while one remains whom the spoiler has not seized.

My departure for Ingolstadt, which had been delayed by these events, was now decided upon again. I got my father to allow me a few more weeks. It seemed wrong to me to leave the deathlike quiet of the mourning house so soon and rush into the busy world. I was new to sorrow, but it still frightened me. I did not want to leave the sight of those who remained to me, and above all, I wanted to see my sweet Elizabeth somewhat comforted.

She hid her grief and tried to act as a comforter to us all. She looked steadily at life and took on its duties with courage and energy. She devoted herself to those she had been taught to call her uncle and cousins. Never was she so enchanting as at this time, when she brought back the sunshine of her smiles and spent them upon us. She forgot even her own regret in her efforts to make us forget.

The day of my departure finally arrived. Clerval spent the last evening with us. He had tried to persuade his father to allow him to come with me and become my fellow student, but without success. His father was a narrow-minded merchant and saw idleness and ruin in his son's hopes and ambition. Henry deeply felt the misfortune of being kept from a broad education. He said little, but when he spoke I saw in his brightening eye and lively expression a restrained but firm resolve not to be chained to the miserable details of commerce.

We sat late. We could not tear ourselves away from each other or bring ourselves to say the word “Farewell!” It was said at last, and we went to bed pretending to seek rest, each imagining that the other was fooled. But when at dawn I went down to the carriage that was to take me away, they were all there—my father again to bless me, Clerval to press my hand once more, my Elizabeth to repeat her requests that I would write often and to give the last gentle attentions to her playmate and friend.

I threw myself into the carriage that was to take me away and gave myself over to the most melancholy thoughts. I, who had always been surrounded by kind companions, continually trying to give each other pleasure—I was now alone. At the university where I was going, I would have to make my own friends and be my own protector. My life until now had been remarkably secluded and domestic, and this had given me an unconquerable dislike of new faces. I loved my brothers, Elizabeth, and Clerval; these were “old familiar faces,” but I believed myself completely unsuited for the company of strangers. Such were my thoughts as I began my journey; but as I went on, my spirits and hopes rose. I eagerly desired to gain knowledge. I had often, when at home, thought it hard to remain shut up in one place during my youth and had longed to enter the world and take my place among other human beings. Now my wishes were granted, and it would indeed have been foolish to regret it.

I had plenty of time for these and many other thoughts during my journey to Ingolstadt, which was long and tiring. At last the high white steeple of the town met my eyes. I got out and was led to my solitary apartment to spend the evening as I pleased.

The next morning I delivered my letters of introduction and paid a visit to some of the principal professors. Chance—or rather the evil influence, the Angel of Destruction, which held complete power over me from the moment I turned my reluctant steps from my father's door—led me first to M. Krempe, professor of natural philosophy. He was a rough man, but deeply knowledgeable in the secrets of his science. He asked me several questions about my progress in the different branches of science related to natural philosophy. I replied carelessly, and partly with contempt, mentioning the names of my alchemists as the main authors I had studied. The professor stared. "Have you," he said, "really spent your time studying such nonsense?"

I replied that I had. "Every minute," continued M. Krempe with passion, "every instant that you have wasted on those books is utterly and entirely lost. You have burdened your memory with discredited systems and useless names. Good God! In what desert land have you lived, where no one was kind enough to inform you that these fancies which you have so greedily absorbed are a thousand years old and as stale as they are ancient? I little expected, in this enlightened and scientific age, to find a follower of Albertus Magnus and Paracelsus. My dear sir, you must begin your studies entirely anew."

So saying, he stepped aside and wrote down a list of several books on natural philosophy which he wanted me to obtain, and dismissed me after mentioning that at the beginning of the following week he intended to start a course of lectures on natural philosophy in its general relations, and that M. Waldman, a fellow professor, would lecture on chemistry on the alternate days that he skipped.

I returned home not disappointed, for I have said that I had long considered those authors useless whom the professor condemned; but I returned not at all more inclined to return to these studies in any form. M. Krempe was a little squat man with a gruff voice and a repulsive face; the teacher, therefore, did not win me over to his pursuits. In perhaps too philosophical and connected a manner, I have given an account of the conclusions I had come to concerning them in my early years. As a child I had not been content with the results promised by the modern professors of natural science. With a confusion of ideas only to be explained by my extreme youth and my lack of a guide on such matters, I had retraced the steps of knowledge along the paths of time and exchanged the discoveries of recent researchers for the dreams of forgotten alchemists. Besides, I had a contempt for the uses of modern natural philosophy. It was very different when the masters of the science sought immortality and power; such views, although futile, were grand; but now the scene had changed. The ambition of the researcher seemed to limit itself to the destruction of those visions on which my interest in science was chiefly founded. I was required to exchange dreams of boundless grandeur for realities of little worth.

Such were my thoughts during the first two or three days of my stay at Ingolstadt, which I mainly spent getting to know the area and the main residents of my new home. But as the next week began, I thought about what M. Krempe had told me about the lectures. And although I could not bring myself to go and hear that little conceited fellow speak from a podium, I remembered what he had said about M. Waldman, whom I had never seen, as he had been out of town until now.

Partly from curiosity and partly from boredom, I went into the lecture room, which M. Waldman entered shortly after. This professor was very unlike his colleague. He appeared to be about fifty years old, but with a face that showed the greatest kindness; a few grey hairs covered his temples, but those at the back of his head were nearly black. He was short but stood remarkably upright, and his voice was the sweetest I had ever heard. He began his lecture by reviewing the history of chemistry and the various improvements made by different scholars, speaking with great feeling the names of the most distinguished discoverers. He then gave a brief overview of the current state of the science and explained many of its basic terms. After making a few introductory experiments, he finished with a speech of high praise for modern chemistry, the words of which I shall never forget:

“The ancient teachers of this science,” he said, “promised impossibilities and achieved nothing. The modern masters promise very little; they know that metals cannot be transformed and that the elixir of life is a fantasy. But these philosophers, whose hands seem made only to work with dirt, and whose eyes to study the microscope or crucible, have indeed performed miracles. They enter the hidden places of nature and show how she works in her secret corners. They rise into the heavens; they have discovered how the blood circulates, and the nature of the air we breathe. They have gained new and almost unlimited powers; they can command the thunder of the skies, imitate the earthquake, and even mock the invisible world with its own shadows.”

Such were the professor’s words—or rather, let me say, such were the words of fate—spoken to destroy me. As he went on, I felt as if my soul were wrestling with a real enemy; one by one the various keys were touched that formed the mechanism of my being; chord after chord was sounded, and soon my mind was filled with one thought, one idea, one purpose. So much has been done, exclaimed the soul of Frankenstein—more, far more, will I achieve; following the path already marked, I will pioneer a new way, explore unknown powers, and reveal to the world the deepest mysteries of creation.

I did not close my eyes that night. My inner being was in a state of rebellion and turmoil; I felt that order would come from it, but I had no power to create it. Gradually, after dawn, sleep came. I awoke, and my thoughts from the night before were like a dream. There only remained a resolution to return to my old studies and to devote myself to a science for which I believed I had a natural talent. On the same day I paid M. Waldman a visit. His manner in private was even more gentle and attractive than in public, for there was a certain dignity in his bearing during his lecture which in his own house was replaced by the greatest friendliness and kindness. I gave him nearly the same account of my former pursuits as I had given to his fellow professor. He listened with attention to the little story about my studies and smiled at the names of Cornelius Agrippa and Paracelsus, but without the contempt that M. Krempe had shown. He said that “These were men to whose tireless zeal modern philosophers owed most of the foundations of their knowledge. They had left to us, as an easier task, to give new names and arrange in connected classifications the facts which they in large part had helped to bring to light. The labours of men of genius, however wrongly directed, almost never fail in ultimately turning to the solid advantage of mankind.” I listened to his statement, which was delivered without any arrogance or affectation, and then added that his lecture had removed my prejudices against modern chemists; I expressed myself in measured terms, with the modesty and respect due from a youth to his instructor, without letting slip (inexperience in life would have made me ashamed) any of the enthusiasm which drove my intended labours. I asked for his advice about the books I ought to obtain.

“I am happy,” said M. Waldman, “to have gained a disciple; and if your effort equals your ability, I have no doubt of your success. Chemistry is that branch of natural philosophy in which the greatest improvements have been and may be made; it is for that reason that I have made it my particular study; but at the same time, I have not neglected the other branches of science. A man would make but a very poor chemist if he attended to that department of human knowledge alone. If your wish is to become truly a man of science and not merely a minor experimenter, I would advise you to apply yourself to every branch of natural philosophy, including mathematics.”

He then took me into his laboratory and explained to me the uses of his various machines, instructing me as to what I ought to obtain and promising me the use of his own when I should have advanced far enough in the science not to disturb their mechanism. He also gave me the list of books which I had requested, and I took my leave.

Thus ended a day memorable to me; it decided my future destiny.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 4

From this day natural philosophy, and particularly chemistry in its broadest sense, became almost my only occupation. I read eagerly those works, so full of genius and insight, which modern researchers have written on these subjects. I attended lectures and cultivated the acquaintance of the university's men of science, and I found even in M. Krempe a great deal of sound sense and real information, combined, it is true, with a repulsive face and manners, but not on that account any less valuable. In M. Waldman I found a true friend. His gentleness was never tinged by dogmatism, and his instructions were given with an air of frankness and good nature that banished every idea of pedantry. In a thousand ways he smoothed the path of knowledge for me and made the most abstruse inquiries clear and easy to my understanding. My application was at first fluctuating and uncertain; it gained strength as I proceeded and soon became so ardent and eager that the stars often disappeared in the light of morning while I was still engaged in my laboratory.

As I applied myself so closely, it may be easily imagined that my progress was rapid. My passion was indeed the astonishment of the students, and my proficiency that of the masters. Professor Krempe often asked me, with a sly smile, how Cornelius Agrippa was going, while M. Waldman expressed the most heartfelt delight in my progress. Two years passed in this manner, during which I paid no visit to Geneva, but was engaged, heart and soul, in the pursuit of some discoveries which I hoped to make. None but those who have experienced them can conceive of the enticements of science. In other studies you go as far as others have gone before you, and there is nothing more to know; but in a scientific pursuit there is continual food for discovery and wonder. A mind of moderate capacity which closely pursues one study must inevitably arrive at great proficiency in that study; and I, who continually sought the attainment of one object of pursuit and was solely wrapped up in this, improved so rapidly that at the end of two years I made some discoveries in the improvement of some chemical instruments, which procured me great esteem and admiration at the university. When I had arrived at this point and had become as well acquainted with the theory and practice of natural philosophy as depended on the lessons of any of the professors at Ingolstadt, my residence there being no longer conducive to my improvements, I thought of returning to my friends and my native town, when an incident happened that prolonged my stay.

One of the things that especially caught my attention was the structure of the human body, and indeed of any living animal. Where, I often asked myself, did the principle of life come from? It was a bold question, and one that has always been considered a mystery. Yet how many things are we on the verge of understanding, if only cowardice or carelessness did not hold back our curiosity. I turned these thoughts over in my mind and decided from then on to devote myself more particularly to those branches of natural philosophy that relate to physiology. Unless I had been driven by an almost supernatural enthusiasm, my work on this subject would have been tedious and almost unbearable. To examine the causes of life, we must first turn to death. I became familiar with the science of anatomy, but this was not enough; I also had to observe the natural decay and corruption of the human body. In my upbringing, my father had taken the greatest care that my mind should not be filled with supernatural fears. I do not ever remember trembling at a tale of superstition or fearing the appearance of a ghost. Darkness had no effect on my imagination, and a churchyard was to me merely the resting place of bodies without life, which, from being the home of beauty and strength, had become food for the worm. Now I was led to examine the cause and progress of this decay, and I was forced to spend days and nights in vaults and burial houses. My attention was fixed on every object most unbearable to the delicacy of human feeling. I saw how the fine form of man was degraded and wasted away; I saw the corruption of death take over from the blooming cheek of life; I saw how the worm inherited the wonders of the eye and brain. I paused, examining and analyzing all the small details of cause and effect, as shown in the change from life to death, and death to life, until from the middle of this darkness a sudden light broke in upon me—a light so brilliant and wonderful, yet so simple, that while I became dizzy with the vastness of the prospect it revealed, I was surprised that among so many men of genius who had directed their inquiries toward the same science, I alone should be chosen to discover so astonishing a secret.

Remember, I am not recording the vision of a madman. The sun does not shine more certainly in the heavens than what I now claim is true. Some miracle might have produced it, yet the stages of the discovery were distinct and believable. After days and nights of incredible labor and fatigue, I succeeded in discovering the cause of generation and life; more than that, I became myself capable of giving animation to lifeless matter.

The astonishment I had first felt at this discovery soon gave way to delight and rapture. After so much time spent in painful labor, to arrive at once at the summit of my desires was the most gratifying end to my toils. But this discovery was so great and overwhelming that all the steps by which I had been gradually led to it were erased, and I saw only the result. What had been the study and desire of the wisest men since the creation of the world was now within my grasp. Not that, like a magic scene, it all opened upon me at once. The information I had obtained was of a nature to direct my efforts once I should point them toward the object of my search, rather than to show that object already accomplished. I was like the Arabian who had been buried with the dead and found a passage to life, aided only by one glimmering and seemingly useless light.

I see by your eagerness and the wonder and hope which your eyes express, my friend, that you expect to be told the secret with which I am acquainted. That cannot be. Listen patiently until the end of my story, and you will easily understand why I am reserved on that subject. I will not lead you on, unguarded and ardent as I then was, to your destruction and certain misery. Learn from me, if not by my warnings, at least by my example, how dangerous the pursuit of knowledge is, and how much happier that man is who believes his native town to be the world, than he who aspires to become greater than his nature will allow.

When I found so astonishing a power placed within my hands, I hesitated a long time about the manner in which I should use it. Although I possessed the capacity of bestowing animation, yet to prepare a frame for the reception of it, with all its intricacies of fibres, muscles, and veins, still remained a work of inconceivable difficulty and labor. I doubted at first whether I should attempt the creation of a being like myself, or one of simpler organization. But my imagination was too much excited by my first success to permit me to doubt my ability to give life to an animal as complex and wonderful as man. The materials at present within my command hardly appeared adequate to so difficult an undertaking, but I did not doubt that I would ultimately succeed. I prepared myself for a multitude of setbacks. My operations might be constantly baffled, and at last my work might be imperfect. Yet when I considered the improvement which every day takes place in science and mechanics, I was encouraged to hope my present attempts would at least lay the foundations of future success. Nor could I consider the magnitude and complexity of my plan as any argument of its impracticability. It was with these feelings that I began the creation of a human being. As the minuteness of the parts formed a great hindrance to my speed, I resolved, contrary to my first intention, to make the being of a gigantic stature, that is to say, about eight feet in height, and proportionally large. After having formed this determination and having spent some months in successfully collecting and arranging my materials, I began.

No one can imagine the rush of feelings that carried me forward, like a hurricane, in the first excitement of success. Life and death seemed to me like imaginary boundaries that I would be the first to break through, pouring a flood of light into our dark world. A new species would bless me as its creator and source; many happy and wonderful beings would owe their existence to me. No father could claim the gratitude of his child as completely as I would deserve theirs. Thinking along these lines, I believed that if I could give life to lifeless matter, I might in time—though I now found it impossible—renew life where death had apparently given the body over to decay.

These thoughts kept my spirits up while I pursued my work with tireless passion. My cheeks had grown pale from study, and my body had become thin from staying indoors. Sometimes, on the very edge of certainty, I failed; yet I still clung to the hope that the next day or the next hour might fulfill. One secret that I alone possessed was the hope to which I had devoted myself; and the moon watched over my midnight labors while, with unrelaxed and breathless eagerness, I chased nature into her hiding places. Who could imagine the horrors of my secret work as I dug among the unholy dampness of graves or tortured living animals to bring lifeless clay to life? My limbs now tremble, and my eyes grow blurry at the memory; but back then a relentless and almost frantic impulse drove me forward. I seemed to have lost all soul or feeling except for this one pursuit. It was indeed only a passing trance, one that only made me feel with renewed sharpness as soon as the unnatural drive stopped working and I returned to my old habits. I collected bones from burial vaults and disturbed, with disrespectful fingers, the tremendous secrets of the human body. In a lonely room—or rather a cell—at the top of the house, separated from all the other rooms by a hallway and staircase, I kept my workshop of filthy creation. My eyeballs were starting from their sockets as I attended to the details of my work. The dissecting room and the slaughterhouse supplied many of my materials; and often my human nature turned with disgust from my task, while, still driven on by an eagerness that constantly increased, I brought my work near to completion.

The summer months passed while I was engaged, heart and soul, in one pursuit. It was a most beautiful season; never did the fields produce a more plentiful harvest or the vines yield a richer crop of grapes, but my eyes were blind to the charms of nature. And the same feelings that made me ignore the scenes around me also caused me to forget those friends who were so many miles away and whom I had not seen for so long. I knew my silence worried them, and I well remembered my father's words: “I know that while you are pleased with yourself you will think of us with affection, and we shall hear from you regularly. You must forgive me if I regard any interruption in your correspondence as proof that your other duties are equally neglected.”

I knew well, therefore, what my father’s feelings would be, but I could not tear my thoughts from my work. The work was loathsome in itself, but it had taken an irresistible hold of my imagination. I wished, as it were, to put off everything related to my feelings of affection until the great object that swallowed up every habit of my nature should be completed.

I then thought that my father would be unjust if he blamed my neglect on vice or faultiness on my part, but I am now convinced that he was justified in thinking that I should not be altogether free from blame. A human being in perfection ought always to preserve a calm and peaceful mind and never allow passion or a passing desire to disturb his tranquillity. I do not think that the pursuit of knowledge is an exception to this rule. If the study to which you apply yourself tends to weaken your affections and destroy your taste for those simple pleasures in which no impurity can possibly mix, then that study is certainly unlawful, that is to say, not befitting the human mind. If this rule were always observed; if no man allowed any pursuit whatsoever to interfere with the tranquillity of his domestic affections, Greece would not have been enslaved, Caesar would have spared his country, America would have been discovered more gradually, and the empires of Mexico and Peru would not have been destroyed.

But I forget that I am moralizing in the most interesting part of my tale, and your looks remind me to proceed.

My father made no reproach in his letters and only took notice of my silence by asking about my occupations more particularly than before. Winter, spring, and summer passed away during my labours, but I did not watch the blossom or the expanding leaves—sights which before always gave me supreme delight—so deeply was I engrossed in my occupation. The leaves of that year had withered before my work drew near to a close, and now every day showed me more plainly how well I had succeeded. But my enthusiasm was checked by my anxiety, and I appeared rather like one doomed by slavery to toil in the mines, or any other unwholesome trade, than an artist occupied by his favourite employment. Every night I was oppressed by a slow fever, and I became nervous to a most painful degree. The fall of a leaf startled me, and I shunned my fellow creatures as if I had been guilty of a crime. Sometimes I grew alarmed at the wreck I saw that I had become. The energy of my purpose alone sustained me: my labours would soon end, and I believed that exercise and amusement would then drive away the beginning of disease. I promised myself both of these when my creation should be complete.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 5

It was on a dreary November night that I saw the completion of all my hard work. With an anxiety that almost amounted to agony, I gathered the instruments of life around me so that I might bring a spark of being into the lifeless thing that lay at my feet. It was already one in the morning; the rain pattered dismally against the windows, and my candle was nearly burnt out, when, by the glimmer of the half-extinguished light, I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open. It breathed hard, and a convulsive motion shook its limbs.

How can I describe my emotions at this disaster, or how can I describe the wretch whom I had tried to form with such infinite pains and care? His limbs were in proportion, and I had selected his features as beautiful. Beautiful! Great God! His yellow skin scarcely covered the work of muscles and arteries beneath. His hair was of a lustrous black and flowing, and his teeth were of a pearly whiteness. But these luxuries only formed a more horrid contrast with his watery eyes, which seemed almost of the same colour as the dun-white sockets in which they were set, his shrivelled complexion, and his straight black lips.

The different accidents of life are not so changeable as the feelings of human nature. I had worked hard for nearly two years for the sole purpose of bringing life into an inanimate body. For this I had deprived myself of rest and health. I had desired it with a passion that far exceeded moderation. But now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart. Unable to endure the sight of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room and spent a long time pacing through my bedchamber, unable to calm my mind enough to sleep. At length exhaustion replaced the turmoil I had endured before, and I threw myself on the bed in my clothes, trying to find a few moments of forgetfulness. But it was in vain. I slept, indeed, but I was disturbed by the wildest dreams. I thought I saw Elizabeth, in the bloom of health, walking in the streets of Ingolstadt. Delighted and surprised, I embraced her. But as I pressed the first kiss on her lips, they became livid with the hue of death. Her features appeared to change, and I thought that I held the corpse of my dead mother in my arms. A shroud enveloped her form, and I saw the grave-worms crawling in the folds of the flannel. I started from my sleep with horror. A cold dew covered my forehead, my teeth chattered, and every limb became convulsed. Then, by the dim and yellow light of the moon as it forced its way through the window shutters, I saw the wretch—the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed, and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me. His jaws opened, and he muttered some inarticulate sounds while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He might have spoken, but I did not hear. One hand was stretched out, seemingly to hold me back, but I escaped and rushed downstairs. I took refuge in the courtyard belonging to the house where I lived, and there I remained during the rest of the night, walking up and down in the greatest agitation, listening attentively, catching and fearing each sound as if it were to announce the approach of the demoniacal corpse to which I had so miserably given life.

Oh! No one could bear the horror of that face. A mummy brought back to life could not be as hideous as that creature. I had looked at him while he was unfinished; he was ugly then, but when those muscles and joints became capable of movement, he became something that even Dante could not have imagined.

I spent the night miserably. Sometimes my pulse beat so quickly and violently that I felt every artery throbbing; at other times, I nearly collapsed from exhaustion and extreme weakness. Mixed with this horror, I felt the bitterness of disappointment. Dreams that had been my nourishment and pleasant rest for so long now became a hell to me. The change was so sudden, the ruin so complete!

Morning, gloomy and wet, finally dawned and revealed to my sleepless and aching eyes the church of Ingolstadt, its white steeple and clock, which showed six o'clock. The porter opened the gates of the courtyard, which had been my refuge that night, and I went out into the streets. I walked with quick steps, as if I were trying to avoid the creature I feared would appear at every turn. I did not dare return to my apartment, but felt driven to hurry on, even though I was soaked by the rain pouring from a black and cheerless sky.

I continued walking like this for some time, trying through physical exercise to ease the burden that weighed on my mind. I moved through the streets without any clear idea of where I was or what I was doing. My heart pounded with the sickness of fear, and I hurried on with unsteady steps, not daring to look around me:

Like one who, on a lonely road,

Does walk in fear and dread,

And, having once turned round, walks on,

And turns no more his head;

Because he knows a frightful fiend

Does close behind him tread.

[Coleridge's "Ancient Mariner."]

Continuing like this, I eventually came to the inn where the various stagecoaches and carriages usually stopped. Here I paused, I do not know why; but I stood for some minutes with my eyes fixed on a coach that was coming toward me from the other end of the street. As it drew nearer, I saw that it was the Swiss diligence; it stopped right where I was standing, and when the door opened, I saw Henry Clerval, who, on seeing me, immediately jumped out. "My dear Frankenstein," he exclaimed, "how glad I am to see you! How lucky that you should be here at the very moment I arrive!"

Nothing could equal my delight on seeing Clerval; his presence brought back to my thoughts my father, Elizabeth, and all those scenes of home so dear to my recollection. I grasped his hand, and in a moment forgot my horror and misfortune; I felt suddenly, and for the first time during many months, calm and serene joy. I welcomed my friend, therefore, in the most cordial manner, and we walked towards my college. Clerval continued talking for some time about our mutual friends and his own good fortune in being permitted to come to Ingolstadt. “You may easily believe,” said he, “how great was the difficulty to persuade my father that all necessary knowledge was not comprised in the noble art of book-keeping; and, indeed, I believe I left him incredulous to the last, for his constant answer to my unwearied entreaties was the same as that of the Dutch schoolmaster in The Vicar of Wakefield: ‘I have ten thousand florins a year without Greek, I eat heartily without Greek.’ But his affection for me at length overcame his dislike of learning, and he has permitted me to undertake a voyage of discovery to the land of knowledge.”

“It gives me the greatest delight to see you; but tell me how you left my father, brothers, and Elizabeth.”

“Very well, and very happy, only a little uneasy that they hear from you so seldom. By the by, I mean to lecture you a little upon their account myself. But, my dear Frankenstein,” continued he, stopping short and gazing full in my face, “I did not before remark how very ill you appear; so thin and pale; you look as if you had been watching for several nights.”

“You have guessed right; I have lately been so deeply engaged in one occupation that I have not allowed myself sufficient rest, as you see; but I hope, I sincerely hope, that all these employments are now at an end and that I am at length free.”

I trembled excessively; I could not endure to think of, and far less to allude to, the occurrences of the preceding night. I walked with a quick pace, and we soon arrived at my college. I then reflected, and the thought made me shiver, that the creature whom I had left in my apartment might still be there, alive and walking about. I dreaded to behold this monster, but I feared still more that Henry should see him. Entreating him, therefore, to remain a few minutes at the bottom of the stairs, I darted up towards my own room. My hand was already on the lock of the door before I recollected myself. I then paused, and a cold shivering came over me. I threw the door forcibly open, as children are accustomed to do when they expect a spectre to stand in waiting for them on the other side; but nothing appeared. I stepped fearfully in: the apartment was empty, and my bedroom was also freed from its hideous guest. I could hardly believe that so great a good fortune could have befallen me, but when I became assured that my enemy had indeed fled, I clapped my hands for joy and ran down to Clerval.

We went up to my room, and the servant soon brought breakfast, but I could not control myself. It was not only joy that filled me; I felt my skin tingling with extreme sensitivity, and my pulse was beating fast. I could not stay in one place for even a moment; I jumped over the chairs, clapped my hands, and laughed out loud. At first Clerval thought my unusual mood was simply happiness at his arrival, but when he looked at me more closely, he saw a wildness in my eyes that he could not explain, and my loud, uncontrolled, hollow laughter frightened and shocked him.

“My dear Victor,” he cried, “what, for God’s sake, is the matter? Do not laugh like that. How ill you are! What is the cause of all this?”

“Do not ask me,” I cried, putting my hands over my eyes, because I thought I saw the dreaded ghost slip into the room; “he can tell you. Oh, save me! Save me!” I imagined that the monster grabbed me; I struggled violently and fell down in a fit.

Poor Clerval! What must he have felt? A meeting that he had looked forward to with such joy had turned so strangely bitter. But I did not witness his grief, for I was unconscious and did not regain my senses for a long, long time.

This was the beginning of a nervous fever that kept me bedridden for several months. During all that time Henry was my only nurse. I later learned that, knowing my father’s advanced age and inability to make such a long journey, and how miserable my sickness would make Elizabeth, he spared them this grief by hiding the full extent of my illness. He knew that I could not have a kinder or more attentive nurse than himself; and, confident in his hope for my recovery, he did not doubt that, instead of doing harm, he was performing the kindest action he could toward them.

But I was in reality very ill, and surely nothing but the unlimited and constant care of my friend could have brought me back to life. The shape of the monster to whom I had given existence was forever before my eyes, and I raved endlessly about him. No doubt my words surprised Henry; at first he believed them to be the wanderings of my disturbed imagination, but the persistence with which I continually returned to the same subject convinced him that my illness truly had its origin in some extraordinary and terrible event.

Very slowly, and with frequent relapses that alarmed and saddened my friend, I recovered. I remember the first time I became able to observe the outside world with any kind of pleasure, I noticed that the fallen leaves had disappeared and that young buds were sprouting from the trees that shaded my window. It was a glorious spring, and the season contributed greatly to my recovery. I also felt feelings of joy and affection revive in my heart; my gloom disappeared, and in a short time I became as cheerful as I had been before I was struck by that fatal passion.

“Dearest Clerval,” I exclaimed, “how kind, how very good you are to me. This whole winter, instead of being spent in study, as you had promised yourself, has been used up in my sick room. How shall I ever repay you? I feel the greatest regret for the disappointment I have caused, but you will forgive me.”

“You will repay me completely if you do not upset yourself, but get well as fast as you can; and since you seem to be in such good spirits, I may speak to you about one subject, may I not?”

I trembled. One subject! What could it be? Could he be referring to someone I did not even dare to think about?

“Calm yourself,” said Clerval, who noticed my change of color. “I will not mention it if it upsets you. But your father and cousin would be very happy to receive a letter from you in your own handwriting. They hardly know how ill you have been and are worried by your long silence.”

“Is that all, my dear Henry? How could you suppose that my first thought would not fly to those dear, dear friends whom I love and who so deserve my love?”

“If this is your present mood, my friend, you will perhaps be glad to see a letter that has been lying here for you for some days. It is from your cousin, I believe.”

###CHAPTER### Chapter 6

Clerval then put the following letter into my hands. It was from my own Elizabeth:

“My dearest Cousin,

“You have been ill, very ill, and even the constant letters of dear kind Henry are not enough to reassure me about you. You are forbidden to write—to hold a pen; yet one word from you, dear Victor, is necessary to calm our fears. For a long time I have thought that each post would bring this line, and my persuasions have restrained my uncle from undertaking a journey to Ingolstadt. I have prevented him from facing the inconveniences and perhaps dangers of so long a journey, yet how often have I regretted not being able to perform it myself! I imagine that the task of attending on your sickbed has fallen to some hired old nurse, who could never guess your wishes nor care for them with the affection of your poor cousin. Yet that is over now: Clerval writes that you are indeed getting better. I eagerly hope that you will confirm this news soon in your own handwriting.

“Get well—and return to us. You will find a happy, cheerful home and friends who love you dearly. Your father’s health is strong, and he asks only to see you, only to be assured that you are well; and not a care will ever cloud his kind face. How pleased you would be to see the improvement of our Ernest! He is now sixteen and full of activity and spirit. He wants to be a true Swiss and to enter foreign service, but we cannot part with him, at least until his elder brother returns to us. My uncle is not pleased with the idea of a military career in a distant country, but Ernest never had your powers of concentration. He looks upon study as a hateful chain; his time is spent in the open air, climbing the hills or rowing on the lake. I fear that he will become an idler unless we give in and permit him to enter the profession which he has chosen.

Little has changed since you left us, except that our dear children have grown. The blue lake and snow-clad mountains never change, and I think our peaceful home and contented hearts follow the same unchanging laws. My small daily tasks keep me busy and amused, and I am rewarded for any effort by seeing only happy, kind faces around me. Only one change has taken place in our little household since you left. Do you remember how Justine Moritz came into our family? Probably you do not, so I will tell her story in a few words. Madame Moritz, her mother, was a widow with four children, and Justine was the third. This girl had always been her father’s favorite, but through some strange perversity, her mother could not stand her, and after M. Moritz died, she treated her very badly. My aunt noticed this, and when Justine was twelve years old, she persuaded her mother to let her live with us. The republican institutions of our country have produced simpler and happier manners than those found in the great monarchies around us. Therefore, there is less distinction between the different classes of people here, and the lower classes, being neither so poor nor so looked down upon, have more refined and moral manners. A servant in Geneva does not mean the same thing as a servant in France or England. Justine, received into our family in this way, learned the duties of a servant, a position which, in our fortunate country, does not include the idea of ignorance or the sacrifice of a person’s human dignity.

Justine, as you may remember, was a great favorite of yours, and I recall you once said that if you were in a bad mood, one look from Justine could chase it away, for the same reason Ariosto gives about the beauty of Angelica—she looked so open-hearted and happy. My aunt grew very fond of her and was led to give her a better education than she had first intended. This kindness was fully repaid; Justine was the most grateful little creature in the world. I do not mean that she made any declarations—I never heard one pass her lips—but you could see by her eyes that she almost adored her protectress. Although she was cheerful by nature and in many ways carefree, she paid the closest attention to every gesture of my aunt. She thought her the model of all excellence and tried to imitate her way of speaking and her manners, so that even now she often reminds me of her.

When my dearest aunt died, everyone was too busy with their own grief to notice poor Justine, who had cared for her during her illness with the most anxious devotion. Poor Justine was very ill, but other trials were waiting for her.

One by one, her brothers and sister died; and her mother, with the exception of her neglected daughter, was left childless. The conscience of the woman was troubled; she began to think that the deaths of her favourites was a judgement from heaven to chastise her partiality. She was a Roman Catholic; and I believe her confessor confirmed the idea which she had conceived. Accordingly, a few months after your departure for Ingolstadt, Justine was called home by her repentant mother. Poor girl! She wept when she quitted our house; she was much altered since the death of my aunt; grief had given softness and a winning mildness to her manners, which had before been remarkable for vivacity. Nor was her residence at her mother’s house of a nature to restore her gaiety. The poor woman was very vacillating in her repentance. She sometimes begged Justine to forgive her unkindness, but much oftener accused her of having caused the deaths of her brothers and sister. Perpetual fretting at length threw Madame Moritz into a decline, which at first increased her irritability, but she is now at peace for ever. She died on the first approach of cold weather, at the beginning of this last winter. Justine has just returned to us; and I assure you I love her tenderly. She is very clever and gentle, and extremely pretty; as I mentioned before, her mien and her expression continually remind me of my dear aunt.

I must say also a few words to you, my dear cousin, of little darling William. I wish you could see him; he is very tall of his age, with sweet laughing blue eyes, dark eyelashes, and curling hair. When he smiles, two little dimples appear on each cheek, which are rosy with health. He has already had one or two little wives, but Louisa Biron is his favourite, a pretty little girl of five years of age.

Now, dear Victor, I dare say you wish to be indulged in a little gossip concerning the good people of Geneva. The pretty Miss Mansfield has already received the congratulatory visits on her approaching marriage with a young Englishman, John Melbourne, Esq. Her ugly sister, Manon, married M. Duvillard, the rich banker, last autumn. Your favourite schoolfellow, Louis Manoir, has suffered several misfortunes since the departure of Clerval from Geneva. But he has already recovered his spirits, and is reported to be on the point of marrying a lively pretty Frenchwoman, Madame Tavernier. She is a widow, and much older than Manoir; but she is very much admired, and a favourite with everybody.

I have written myself into better spirits, dear cousin; but my anxiety returns upon me as I conclude. Write, dearest Victor,—one line—one word will be a blessing to us. Ten thousand thanks to Henry for his kindness, his affection, and his many letters; we are sincerely grateful. Adieu! my cousin; take care of yourself; and, I entreat you, write!

Elizabeth Lavenza.

Geneva, March 18th, 17—.

Dear, dear Elizabeth! I exclaimed, when I had read her letter: I will write instantly and relieve them from the anxiety they must feel. I wrote, and this exertion greatly fatigued me; but my convalescence had commenced, and proceeded regularly. In another fortnight I was able to leave my chamber.

One of my first duties after recovering was to introduce Clerval to the several professors of the university. In doing this, I went through a kind of rough treatment that did not suit the wounds my mind had suffered. Ever since that fatal night, the end of my labours and the beginning of my misfortunes, I had felt a violent hatred even for the name of natural philosophy. When I was otherwise quite restored to health, the sight of a chemical instrument would renew all the agony of my nervous symptoms. Henry saw this and had removed all my apparatus from my view. He had also changed my apartment, for he noticed that I had developed a dislike for the room that had previously been my laboratory. But these efforts of Clerval were useless when I visited the professors. M. Waldman inflicted torture when he praised, with kindness and warmth, the astonishing progress I had made in the sciences. He soon noticed that I disliked the subject, but not guessing the real cause, he attributed my feelings to modesty and changed the subject from my improvement to the science itself, with a desire, as I clearly saw, of drawing me out. What could I do? He meant to please, and he tormented me. I felt as if he had placed carefully, one by one, in my view those instruments which were later to be used in putting me to a slow and cruel death. I writhed under his words, yet dared not show the pain I felt. Clerval, whose eyes and feelings were always quick to sense the emotions of others, declined the subject, using his total ignorance as an excuse, and the conversation took a more general turn. I thanked my friend from my heart, but I did not speak. I saw plainly that he was surprised, but he never tried to draw my secret from me. And although I loved him with a mixture of affection and reverence that knew no bounds, I could never persuade myself to confide in him that event which was so often present in my memory, but which I feared the telling of would only impress more deeply.

M. Krempe was not equally gentle. In my condition at that time, of almost unbearable sensitivity, his harsh, blunt praise gave me even more pain than the kind approval of M. Waldman. “Damn the fellow!” he cried. “Why, M. Clerval, I assure you he has outstripped us all. Yes, stare if you please, but it is nevertheless true. A youngster who, only a few years ago, believed in Cornelius Agrippa as firmly as in the gospel, has now set himself at the head of the university. And if he is not soon pulled down, we shall all be put to shame. Yes, yes,” he continued, noticing my face showing suffering, “M. Frankenstein is modest, an excellent quality in a young man. Young men should be unsure of themselves, you know, M. Clerval. I was myself when young, but that wears out in a very short time.”

M. Krempe had now begun a speech of praise about himself, which happily turned the conversation away from a subject that was so annoying to me.

Clerval had never shared my interest in natural science, and his literary interests were completely different from mine. He came to the university with the goal of mastering the oriental languages, hoping this would open a path for the life he had planned for himself. Determined not to lead an undistinguished life, he looked toward the East as a place where his adventurous spirit could thrive. Persian, Arabic, and Sanskrit captured his attention, and I was easily persuaded to join him in these studies. Idleness had always been painful to me, and now that I wanted to escape from my own thoughts and hated my former studies, I felt great relief in studying alongside my friend. I found not only instruction but also comfort in the works of the orientalists. Unlike him, I did not try to gain a critical knowledge of their languages, since I intended to use them only for temporary amusement. I read merely to understand their meaning, and they richly rewarded my efforts. Their melancholy is soothing, and their joy uplifting, to a degree I never experienced in studying the authors of any other country. When you read their writings, life seems to consist of a warm sun and a garden of roses—of the smiles and frowns of a beautiful adversary, and the fire that consumes your own heart. How different from the manly and heroic poetry of Greece and Rome!

Summer passed away in these activities, and my return to Geneva was planned for late autumn. But I was delayed by several accidents, and then winter and snow arrived. The roads were considered impassable, and my journey was postponed until the following spring. I felt this delay very bitterly, for I longed to see my native town and my beloved friends. My return had only been delayed so long because I was unwilling to leave Clerval in a strange place before he had become acquainted with any of its inhabitants. The winter, however, was spent cheerfully, and although spring was unusually late, when it came its beauty made up for its slowness.

The month of May had already begun, and I was expecting the letter daily that would set the date of my departure, when Henry proposed a walking tour in the area around Ingolstadt, so that I might say a personal farewell to the country I had lived in for so long. I gladly agreed to this suggestion. I enjoyed exercise, and Clerval had always been my favorite companion on walks of this kind that I had taken among the scenes of my native country.

We spent two weeks wandering around like this. My health and spirits had been restored for some time, and they grew even stronger thanks to the fresh air I breathed, the natural events of our journey, and my friend’s conversation. Study had previously cut me off from contact with other people and made me unsociable, but Clerval brought out the better feelings of my heart. He taught me again to love the appearance of nature and the cheerful faces of children. What an excellent friend! How sincerely you loved me and tried to raise my mind until it was equal to your own. A selfish pursuit had restricted and narrowed me, until your gentleness and affection warmed and opened my senses. I became the same happy creature who, a few years ago, loved and was loved by everyone, and had no sorrow or care. When I was happy, lifeless nature had the power to give me the most delightful sensations. A calm sky and green fields filled me with joy. The present season was truly divine; the spring flowers bloomed in the hedges, while the summer flowers were already budding. I was not troubled by the thoughts that had weighed on me during the previous year, despite my attempts to push them away, like an unbearable burden.

Henry was delighted by my cheerfulness and sincerely shared my feelings. He made every effort to entertain me, while also expressing the emotions that filled his soul. The resources of his mind on this occasion were truly astonishing. His conversation was full of imagination, and very often, in imitation of Persian and Arabic writers, he invented tales of wonderful fancy and passion. At other times he repeated my favorite poems, or drew me into arguments, which he supported with great skill.

We returned to our college on a Sunday afternoon. The peasants were dancing, and everyone we met appeared cheerful and happy. My own spirits were high, and I bounded along with feelings of unrestrained joy and excitement.

My journey was very melancholy. At first I wished to hurry on, for I longed to console and sympathize with my loved and sorrowing friends. But when I drew near my native town, I slowed my pace. I could hardly bear the multitude of feelings that crowded into my mind. I passed through scenes familiar to my youth, but which I had not seen for nearly six years. How changed everything might be during that time! One sudden and devastating change had taken place, but a thousand small circumstances might have gradually worked other changes, which, although they happened more quietly, might be no less decisive. Fear overcame me; I dared not go forward, dreading a thousand nameless evils that made me tremble, although I could not define them.

I remained two days at Lausanne in this painful state of mind. I gazed at the lake: the waters were calm, all around was still, and the snowy mountains, “the palaces of nature,” were unchanged. Gradually the peaceful and heavenly scene restored me, and I continued my journey toward Geneva.

The road ran by the side of the lake, which became narrower as I approached my native town. I could see more clearly the dark slopes of the Jura mountains and the bright summit of Mont Blanc. I wept like a child. “Dear mountains! My own beautiful lake! How do you welcome your wanderer? Your summits are clear; the sky and lake are blue and calm. Is this a sign of peace, or is it mocking my unhappiness?”

I fear, my friend, that I will bore you by dwelling on these early details. But those were days of relative happiness, and I remember them with pleasure. My country, my beloved country! Who but a native can understand the delight I felt in seeing your streams, your mountains, and, more than anything, your lovely lake again!

Yet, as I drew nearer home, grief and fear overcame me once more. Night closed in around me, and when I could hardly see the dark mountains, I felt even more gloomy. The scene before me seemed a vast and shadowy landscape of evil, and I sensed vaguely that I was destined to become the most wretched of human beings. Alas! I predicted truly, and was wrong in only one detail: in all the misery I imagined and dreaded, I did not conceive even a hundredth part of the anguish I was destined to endure.

It was completely dark when I arrived on the outskirts of Geneva. The gates of the town were already shut, so I had to spend the night at Secheron, a village half a league from the city. The sky was clear, and since I could not rest, I decided to visit the spot where my poor William had been murdered. As I could not pass through the town, I had to cross the lake in a boat to reach Plainpalais. During this short trip I saw lightning playing over the summit of Mont Blanc in the most beautiful shapes. The storm seemed to be approaching quickly, and when I landed, I climbed a low hill so I could watch its progress. It advanced; the sky clouded over, and I soon felt the rain coming down slowly in large drops, but its force quickly increased.

I left my seat and walked on, although the darkness and storm grew worse every minute and thunder burst with a terrifying crash over my head. It echoed from Salêve, the Juras, and the Alps of Savoy. Vivid flashes of lightning dazzled my eyes, lighting up the lake and making it look like a vast sheet of fire. Then for an instant everything seemed pitch black, until my eyes recovered from the flash. The storm, as often happens in Switzerland, appeared at once in different parts of the sky. The most violent part hung exactly north of the town, over the stretch of lake between the promontory of Belrive and the village of Copêt. Another storm lit up the Jura with faint flashes, and another darkened and sometimes revealed the Môle, a peaked mountain to the east of the lake.

While I watched the storm, so beautiful yet terrifying, I wandered on with a quick step. This noble war in the sky lifted my spirits. I clasped my hands and cried out, “William, dear angel! This is your funeral, this is your dirge!” As I said these words, I saw in the gloom a figure that stole from behind a clump of trees near me. I stood fixed, gazing intently. I could not be mistaken. A flash of lightning lit up the figure and showed its shape plainly to me. Its gigantic size, and the deformity of its appearance, more hideous than anything human, instantly told me that it was the wretch, the filthy demon, to whom I had given life. What was he doing there? Could he be (I shuddered at the thought) the murderer of my brother? No sooner did that idea cross my mind than I became convinced it was true. My teeth chattered, and I had to lean against a tree for support. The figure passed me quickly, and I lost it in the darkness. Nothing in human shape could have destroyed the innocent child. He was the murderer! I could not doubt it. The mere presence of the idea was irresistible proof of the fact. I thought of chasing the devil, but it would have been useless, for another flash showed him to me hanging among the rocks of the nearly vertical slope of Mont Salêve, a hill that borders Plainpalais on the south. He soon reached the summit and disappeared.

I remained motionless. The thunder stopped, but the rain still continued, and the scene was wrapped in impenetrable darkness. I turned over in my mind the events that I had until now tried to forget: the whole course of my progress toward the creation, the appearance of the work of my own hands at my bedside, its departure. Two years had now nearly passed since the night on which he first received life. And was this his first crime? Alas! I had turned loose into the world a depraved wretch whose delight was in carnage and misery. Had he not murdered my brother?

No one can imagine the anguish I suffered during the rest of the night, which I spent, cold and wet, in the open air. But I did not feel the discomfort of the weather. My imagination was busy with scenes of evil and despair. I considered the being whom I had cast among mankind, and given the will and power to carry out purposes of horror, such as the deed he had now done, almost as my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave and forced to destroy all that was dear to me.

Day dawned, and I headed toward town. The gates were open, and I hurried to my father’s house. My first thought was to reveal what I knew about the murderer and have him pursued immediately. But I hesitated when I thought about the story I would have to tell. A being I myself had created and brought to life had met me at midnight among the cliffs of an inaccessible mountain. I also remembered the nervous fever that had seized me just at the time I dated my creation, which would make an otherwise utterly improbable tale sound like delirium. I knew very well that if anyone else had told me such a story, I would have considered it the ravings of insanity. Besides, the strange nature of the creature would evade all pursuit, even if I were believed enough to persuade my relatives to begin one. And what use would pursuit be anyway? Who could arrest a creature capable of scaling the overhanging sides of Mont Salêve? These thoughts decided me, and I resolved to remain silent.

It was about five in the morning when I entered my father’s house. I told the servants not to disturb the family, and went into the library to wait for their usual hour of rising.

Six years had passed, gone by like a dream except for one permanent mark, and I stood in the same place where I had last embraced my father before leaving for Ingolstadt. Beloved and honored parent! He still remained to me. I gazed at the picture of my mother, which hung over the mantelpiece. It was a historical subject, painted at my father’s request, and showed Caroline Beaufort in an agony of despair, kneeling beside the coffin of her dead father. Her clothing was rustic, and her cheek pale; but there was an air of dignity and beauty that hardly permitted the feeling of pity. Below this picture was a miniature of William, and my tears flowed when I looked at it. While I was thus occupied, Ernest entered. He had heard me arrive and hurried to welcome me. “Welcome, my dearest Victor,” he said. “Ah! I wish you had come three months ago, and then you would have found us all joyful and happy. You come to us now to share a misery that nothing can ease; yet your presence will, I hope, revive our father, who seems sinking under his misfortune; and your persuasions will lead poor Elizabeth to stop her useless and tormenting self-accusations. Tears, unrestrained, fell from my brother’s eyes; a sense of mortal agony crept over my body. Before, I had only imagined the wretchedness of my devastated home; the reality came upon me as a new, and no less terrible, disaster. I tried to calm Ernest; I asked more closely about my father, and here I named my cousin.

“She most of all,” said Ernest, “needs consolation. She accused herself of having caused my brother’s death, and that made her very miserable. But since the murderer has been discovered—”

“The murderer discovered! Good God! How can that be? Who could attempt to pursue him? It is impossible; one might as well try to catch the winds, or hold back a mountain stream with a straw. I saw him too; he was free last night!”

“I do not know what you mean,” replied my brother, in accents of wonder, “but to us the discovery we have made completes our misery. “No one did at first; but several circumstances came out, that have almost forced conviction upon us; and her own behaviour has been so confused, as to add to the evidence of facts a weight that, I fear, leaves no hope for doubt. But she will be tried today, and you will then hear all.”

He then related that, the morning on which the murder of poor William had been discovered, Justine had been taken ill, and confined to her bed for several days. During this interval, one of the servants, happening to examine the apparel she had worn on the night of the murder, had discovered in her pocket the picture of my mother, which had been judged to be the temptation of the murderer. The servant instantly showed it to one of the others, who, without saying a word to any of the family, went to a magistrate; and, upon their deposition, Justine was apprehended. On being charged with the fact, the poor girl confirmed the suspicion in a great measure by her extreme confusion of manner.

This was a strange tale, but it did not shake my faith; and I replied earnestly, “You are all mistaken; I know the murderer. Justine, poor, good Justine, is innocent.”

At that instant my father entered. I saw unhappiness deeply impressed on his countenance, but he endeavoured to welcome me cheerfully; and, after we had exchanged our mournful greeting, would have introduced some other topic than that of our disaster, had not Ernest exclaimed, “Good God, papa! Victor says that he knows who was the murderer of poor William.”

“We do also, unfortunately,” replied my father, “for indeed I had rather have been for ever ignorant than have discovered so much depravity and ungratitude in one I valued so highly.”

“If she is, God forbid that she should suffer as guilty. She is to be tried today, and I hope, I sincerely hope, that she will be acquitted.”

This speech calmed me. I was firmly convinced in my own mind that Justine, and indeed every human being, was guiltless of this murder. I had no fear, therefore, that any circumstantial evidence could be brought forward strong enough to convict her. My tale was not one to announce publicly; its astounding horror would be looked upon as madness by the vulgar. Did any one indeed exist, except I, the creator, who would believe, unless his senses convinced him, in the existence of the living monument of presumption and rash ignorance which I had let loose upon the world?

We were soon joined by Elizabeth. Time had changed her since I last saw her; it had given her a loveliness beyond the beauty of her childhood. She had the same openness, the same liveliness, but now it was paired with an expression fuller of feeling and intelligence. She welcomed me with the greatest affection. “Your arrival, my dear cousin,” she said, “fills me with hope. Perhaps you will find some way to clear my poor innocent Justine. Alas! Who is safe, if she is convicted of a crime? I rely on her innocence as certainly as I do on my own. Our misfortune is doubly hard for us; we have not only lost that lovely darling boy, but this poor girl, whom I sincerely love, is to be taken away by an even worse fate. If she is condemned, I will never know joy again. But she will not be, I am sure she will not be; and then I will be happy again, even after the sad death of my little William.”

“She is innocent, my Elizabeth,” I said, “and that will be proved. Fear nothing, and let your spirits be lifted by the assurance of her acquittal.”

“How kind and generous you are! Everyone else believes in her guilt, and that made me miserable, because I knew it was impossible. And to see everyone else so fatally prejudiced made me hopeless and despairing.” She wept.

“Dearest niece,” said my father, “dry your tears. If she is, as you believe, innocent, rely on the justice of our laws, and on my efforts to prevent the slightest shadow of bias.”

###CHAPTER### Chapter 8

We passed a few sad hours until eleven o’clock, when the trial was to begin. My father and the rest of the family had to attend as witnesses, so I went with them to the court. During this whole wretched mockery of justice I suffered living torture. It was to be decided whether the result of my curiosity and lawless schemes would cause the death of two of my fellow beings. One was a smiling baby full of innocence and joy. The other had been murdered far more dreadfully, with every added infamy that could make the murder memorable in horror. Justine was also a girl of merit and had qualities that promised to make her life happy. Now all was to be wiped out in a shameful grave, and I was the cause! A thousand times rather would I have confessed myself guilty of the crime charged to Justine, but I was absent when it was committed, and such a declaration would have been considered the ravings of a madman and would not have freed her who suffered through me.

Justine appeared calm. She was dressed in mourning, and her face, always engaging, was made exquisitely beautiful by the solemnity of her feelings. Yet she seemed confident in her innocence and did not tremble, although she was stared at and cursed by thousands. All the kindness that her beauty might otherwise have stirred was wiped out in the minds of the spectators by their imagined horror of the crime she was supposed to have committed. She was tranquil, yet her tranquillity was clearly forced. Since her earlier confusion had been used as proof of her guilt, she worked up her mind to an appearance of courage. When she entered the court she looked around it and quickly discovered where we were seated. A tear seemed to dim her eye when she saw us, but she quickly recovered herself, and a look of sorrowful affection seemed to prove her complete innocence.

The trial began, and after the advocate against her had stated the charge, several witnesses were called. Several strange facts combined against her, which might have shaken anyone who did not have such proof of her innocence as I had. She had been out the whole of the night on which the murder had been committed, and towards morning a market-woman had seen her not far from the spot where the body of the murdered child was afterwards found. The woman asked her what she was doing there, but she looked very strangely and only gave a confused and unintelligible answer. She returned to the house about eight o’clock, and when someone asked where she had passed the night, she replied that she had been looking for the child and asked earnestly if anything had been heard about him. When shown the body, she fell into violent hysterics and kept to her bed for several days. The picture was then produced which the servant had found in her pocket. When Elizabeth, in a faltering voice, proved that it was the same one which, an hour before the child had been missed, she had placed round his neck, a murmur of horror and indignation filled the court.

Justine was called on for her defence. As the trial had proceeded, her face had changed. Surprise, horror, and misery were strongly expressed. Sometimes she struggled with her tears, but when she was asked to plead, she gathered her strength and spoke in an audible although unsteady voice.

“God knows,” she said, “how entirely I am innocent. But I do not pretend that my protests alone should clear me. I rest my innocence on a plain and simple explanation of the facts that have been brought against me, and I hope the character I have always had will lead my judges to a favorable interpretation where any circumstance appears doubtful or suspicious.”

She then explained that, with Elizabeth’s permission, she had spent the evening of the night of the murder at the house of an aunt at Chêne, a village about a league from Geneva. On her return, at about nine o’clock, she met a man who asked her if she had seen anything of the child who was lost. She was alarmed by this and spent several hours looking for him. Then the gates of Geneva were shut, and she was forced to remain for several hours of the night in a barn belonging to a cottage, unwilling to call up the inhabitants, to whom she was well known. She spent most of the night there watching. Toward morning she believed she slept for a few minutes. Some steps disturbed her, and she awoke. It was dawn, and she left her shelter so that she might again try to find my brother. If she had gone near the spot where his body lay, it was without her knowledge. That she had been confused when questioned by the market woman was not surprising, since she had passed a sleepless night and the fate of poor William was still uncertain. Concerning the picture she could give no account.

“I know,” continued the unhappy victim, “how heavily and fatally this one circumstance weighs against me, but I have no power of explaining it. And when I have expressed my utter ignorance, I am only left to guess at the ways by which it might have been placed in my pocket. But here also I am stopped. I believe that I have no enemy on earth, and surely no one would have been so wicked as to destroy me without cause. Did the murderer place it there? I know of no opportunity he had for doing so. Or, if he had, why should he have stolen the jewel only to part with it again so soon?

“I commit my cause to the justice of my judges, yet I see no room for hope. I beg permission to have a few witnesses examined concerning my character. And if their testimony does not outweigh my supposed guilt, I must be condemned, although I would pledge my salvation on my innocence.”

Several witnesses were called who had known her for many years, and they spoke well of her. But fear and hatred of the crime of which they supposed her guilty made them timid and unwilling to come forward. Elizabeth saw that even this last resource—her excellent character and irreproachable conduct—was about to fail the accused. Then, although violently agitated, she asked permission to address the court.

“I am,” she said, “the cousin of the unhappy child who was murdered—or rather his sister, for I was raised by and have lived with his parents ever since, and even long before his birth. It may therefore seem improper for me to come forward on this occasion. But when I see a fellow creature about to perish through the cowardice of her pretended friends, I wish to be allowed to speak, so that I may say what I know of her character. I am well acquainted with the accused. I have lived in the same house with her, at one time for five years and at another for nearly two years. During all that period she appeared to me the most kind and good-natured of human beings. She nursed Madame Frankenstein, my aunt, in her last illness with the greatest affection and care. Afterwards she attended her own mother during a long and difficult illness in a manner that excited the admiration of all who knew her. After that she again lived in my uncle’s house, where she was loved by all the family. She was warmly attached to the child who is now dead and acted towards him like a most affectionate mother. For my own part, I do not hesitate to say that, despite all the evidence produced against her, I believe and rely on her perfect innocence. She had no motive for such an action. As for the trinket on which the main proof rests, if she had truly wanted it, I would have willingly given it to her—so much do I esteem and value her.”

A murmur of approval followed Elizabeth’s simple and powerful appeal. But it was prompted by her generous intervention, and not in favor of poor Justine, on whom public outrage was turned with renewed violence, accusing her of the blackest ingratitude. Justine herself wept as Elizabeth spoke, but she did not answer. My own distress and anguish were extreme during the whole trial. I believed in her innocence; I knew it. Could the demon who had murdered my brother—I did not for a minute doubt it—also, in his hellish sport, have betrayed the innocent to death and disgrace? I could not bear the horror of my situation. When I saw that the popular voice and the faces of the judges had already condemned my unhappy victim, I rushed out of the court in agony. The tortures of the accused did not equal mine. She was sustained by innocence, but the fangs of remorse tore at my chest and would not release their hold.

I passed a night of pure wretchedness. In the morning I went to the court; my lips and throat were dry. I dared not ask the fatal question, but I was known, and the officer guessed the reason for my visit. The ballots had been cast; they were all black, and Justine was condemned.

I cannot pretend to describe what I felt then. I had experienced sensations of horror before, and I have tried to give them adequate expression, but words cannot convey an idea of the heart-sickening despair that I endured at that moment. The person I spoke to added that Justine had already confessed her guilt. “That evidence,” he remarked, “was hardly required in so obvious a case, but I am glad of it. Indeed, none of our judges like to condemn a criminal upon circumstantial evidence, however decisive it may be.”

This was strange and unexpected news. What could it mean? Had my eyes deceived me? Was I really as mad as the whole world would believe me to be if I revealed the object of my suspicions? I hurried home, and Elizabeth eagerly demanded the result.

“My cousin,” I replied, “it has been decided just as you might have expected. All judges would rather let ten innocent people suffer than let one guilty person escape. But she has confessed.”

This was a terrible blow to poor Elizabeth, who had firmly believed in Justine’s innocence. “Alas!” she said. “How can I ever believe in human goodness again? Justine, whom I loved and respected as my sister, how could she put on those smiles of innocence only to betray? Her gentle eyes seemed incapable of any harshness or deceit, and yet she has committed a murder.”

Soon after, we heard that the poor victim had asked to see my cousin. My father did not want her to go, but said he would leave it to her own judgment and feelings to decide. “Yes,” said Elizabeth, “I will go, even though she is guilty. And you, Victor, shall come with me; I cannot go alone.” The thought of this visit was torture to me, yet I could not refuse.

We entered the gloomy prison cell and saw Justine sitting on some straw at the far end. Her hands were chained, and her head rested on her knees. She stood up when she saw us enter, and when we were left alone with her, she threw herself at Elizabeth’s feet, weeping bitterly. My cousin wept too.

“Oh, Justine!” she said. “Why did you rob me of my last comfort? I trusted in your innocence, and though I was already very miserable, I was not as miserable as I am now.”

“And do you also believe that I am so very, very wicked? Do you also join with my enemies to crush me, to condemn me as a murderer?” Her voice was choked with sobs.

“Get up, my poor girl,” said Elizabeth. “Why do you kneel, if you are innocent? I am not one of your enemies. I believed you were guiltless, despite all the evidence, until I heard that you had declared your own guilt. That report, you say, is false. And be assured, dear Justine, that nothing can shake my confidence in you for a moment, except your own confession.”

“I did confess, but I confessed a lie. I confessed so that I might receive absolution. But now that falsehood lies heavier on my heart than all my other sins. May the God of heaven forgive me! Ever since I was condemned, my confessor has pressured me constantly. He threatened and intimidated me, until I almost began to think that I was the monster he said I was. He threatened excommunication and hellfire in my final moments if I remained stubborn. Dear lady, I had no one to support me. Everyone looked on me as a wretch doomed to disgrace and damnation. What could I do? In an evil hour, I agreed to a lie. And only now am I truly miserable.”

She paused, weeping, and then continued, “I thought with horror, my sweet lady, that you should believe your Justine—whom your blessed aunt had so highly honored, and whom you loved—was a creature capable of a crime that none but the devil himself could have committed. Dear William! Dearest blessed child! I shall soon see you again in heaven, where we shall all be happy. And that consoles me, as I go to suffer disgrace and death.”

“Oh, Justine! Forgive me for having distrusted you for even one moment. Why did you confess? But do not mourn, dear girl. Do not be afraid. I will proclaim, I will prove your innocence. I will soften the stony hearts of your enemies with my tears and prayers. You shall not die! You, my playfellow, my companion, my sister, perish on the scaffold! No! No! I could never survive such a horrible misfortune.”

Justine shook her head mournfully. “I do not fear to die,” she said; “that pain is past. God gives me strength and courage to endure the worst. I leave a sad and bitter world; and if you remember me and think of me as one unjustly condemned, I am resigned to the fate awaiting me. Learn from me, dear lady, to submit patiently to the will of heaven!”

During this conversation I had withdrawn to a corner of the prison room, where I could hide the horrible anguish that possessed me. Despair! Who dared talk of that? The poor victim, who on the next day was to cross the awful boundary between life and death, did not feel such deep and bitter agony as I did. I gnashed my teeth and ground them together, letting out a groan that came from my inmost soul. Justine started. When she saw who it was, she came over to me and said, “Dear sir, you are very kind to visit me; you, I hope, do not believe that I am guilty?”

I could not answer. “No, Justine,” said Elizabeth; “he is more convinced of your innocence than I was, for even when he heard that you had confessed, he did not believe it.”

“I truly thank him. In these last moments I feel the sincerest gratitude towards those who think of me with kindness. How sweet is the affection of others to such a wretch as I am! It removes more than half my misfortune, and I feel as if I could die in peace now that my innocence is acknowledged by you, dear lady, and your cousin.”

Thus the poor sufferer tried to comfort others and herself. She indeed gained the resignation she desired. But I, the true murderer, felt the never-dying worm alive in my chest, which allowed no hope or consolation. Elizabeth also wept and was unhappy, but hers was the misery of innocence, which, like a cloud that passes over the fair moon, hides its brightness for a while but cannot tarnish it. Anguish and despair had penetrated into the core of my heart; I bore a hell within me which nothing could extinguish. We stayed several hours with Justine, and it was with great difficulty that Elizabeth could tear herself away. “I wish,” she cried, “that I could die with you; I cannot live in this world of misery.”

Justine put on an air of cheerfulness, while she struggled to hold back her bitter tears. She embraced Elizabeth and said in a voice of half-suppressed emotion, “Farewell, sweet lady, dearest Elizabeth, my beloved and only friend; may heaven, in its bounty, bless and preserve you; may this be the last misfortune that you will ever suffer! Live, and be happy, and make others so.”

And the next day Justine died. Elizabeth’s heart-rending eloquence failed to move the judges from their settled conviction in the guilt of the saintly sufferer. My passionate and indignant appeals were lost upon them. And when I received their cold answers and heard the harsh, unfeeling reasoning of these men, my intended confession died away on my lips. Thus I might proclaim myself a madman, but I could not revoke the sentence passed upon my wretched victim. She perished on the scaffold as a murderess!

From the tortures of my own heart, I turned to contemplate the deep and silent grief of my Elizabeth. This also was my doing! And my father’s sorrow, and the desolation of that once so happy home—all was the work of my thrice-accursed hands! You weep, unhappy ones, but these are not your last tears! Again you shall raise the funeral wail, and the sound of your lamentations shall be heard again and again! Frankenstein, your son, your kinsman, your early, much-loved friend—he who would spend every vital drop of blood for your sakes, who has no thought or sense of joy except as it is mirrored in your dear faces, who would fill the air with blessings and spend his life in serving you—he bids you weep, to shed countless tears; happy beyond his hopes, if only inexorable fate is satisfied, and if the destruction pauses before the peace of the grave has succeeded your sad torments!

Thus spoke my prophetic soul, as, torn by remorse, horror, and despair, I watched those I loved pour out vain sorrow upon the graves of William and Justine, the first hapless victims of my unhallowed arts.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 9

Nothing is more painful to the human mind than the dead calm of inaction and certainty that follows after the feelings have been worked up by a quick succession of events. That calm deprives the soul both of hope and fear. Justine died; she rested; and I was alive. The blood flowed freely in my veins, but a weight of despair and remorse pressed on my heart which nothing could remove. Sleep fled from my eyes. I wandered like an evil spirit, for I had committed deeds of mischief beyond description horrible, and more, much more (I persuaded myself) was yet behind. Yet my heart overflowed with kindness and the love of virtue. I had begun life with benevolent intentions and thirsted for the moment when I should put them in practice and make myself useful to my fellow beings. Now all was blasted. Instead of that serenity of conscience which allowed me to look back upon the past with self-satisfaction, and from thence to gather promise of new hopes, I was seized by remorse and the sense of guilt, which hurried me away to a hell of intense tortures such as no language can describe.

This state of mind preyed upon my health, which had perhaps never entirely recovered from the first shock it had sustained. I shunned the face of man. All sound of joy or complacency was torture to me. Solitude was my only consolation—deep, dark, deathlike solitude.

My father observed with pain the alteration perceptible in my disposition and habits. He endeavoured by arguments drawn from the feelings of his serene conscience and guiltless life to inspire me with fortitude and awaken in me the courage to dispel the dark cloud which brooded over me. “Do you think, Victor,” said he, “that I do not suffer also? No one could love a child more than I loved your brother”—tears came into his eyes as he spoke—“but is it not a duty to the survivors that we should refrain from augmenting their unhappiness by an appearance of immoderate grief? It is also a duty owed to yourself, for excessive sorrow prevents improvement or enjoyment, or even the discharge of daily usefulness, without which no man is fit for society.”

This advice, although good, was totally inapplicable to my case. I should have been the first to hide my grief and console my friends if remorse had not mingled its bitterness, and terror its alarm, with my other sensations. Now I could only answer my father with a look of despair and endeavour to hide myself from his view.

Around this time we moved back to our house at Belrive. This change was especially welcome to me. The gates closed regularly at ten o’clock, and after that hour it was impossible to stay on the lake, which had made living within the walls of Geneva very tiresome for me. Now I was free. Often, after the rest of the family had gone to bed, I took the boat and spent many hours on the water. Sometimes I set the sails and let the wind carry me along; sometimes, after rowing into the middle of the lake, I let the boat drift on its own and gave in to my own miserable thoughts. When everything around me was peaceful, and I was the only restless thing wandering in a scene so beautiful and heavenly—except for some bat, or the frogs, whose harsh and broken croaking was heard only when I neared the shore—I was often tempted to plunge into the silent lake, so that the waters might close over me and my troubles forever. But I held back when I thought of the brave and suffering Elizabeth, whom I loved tenderly, and whose life was tied to mine. I thought also of my father and my surviving brother; should I, by my shameful desertion, leave them exposed and unprotected against the malice of the fiend I had let loose among them?

At these moments I wept bitterly and wished that peace would return to my mind, if only so that I could offer them comfort and happiness. But that could not happen. Remorse destroyed every hope. I had been the cause of unchangeable evils, and I lived in daily fear that the monster I had created would commit some new wickedness. I had a vague feeling that all was not over, and that he would still commit some terrible crime whose enormity would almost erase the memory of the past. There was always reason for fear as long as anything I loved remained. My hatred of this fiend cannot be imagined. When I thought of him I ground my teeth, my eyes burned, and I fiercely wished to destroy that life which I had so thoughtlessly given. When I thought about his crimes and malice, my hatred and desire for revenge burst all limits of restraint. I would have made a journey to the highest peak of the Andes if, once there, I could have thrown him down to the base. I wished to see him again so that I could pour out the fullest measure of my hatred on his head and avenge the deaths of William and Justine.

Our house was a house of mourning. My father’s health was deeply shaken by the horror of recent events. Elizabeth was sad and despairing; she no longer took pleasure in her usual activities; all pleasure seemed to her like an insult to the dead; she thought that endless sorrow and tears were the only proper tribute she should pay to innocence so destroyed. She was no longer that happy creature who in earlier youth had wandered with me on the banks of the lake and talked with joy of our future hopes. The first of those sorrows that are sent to detach us from the earth had visited her, and its dimming influence had put out her dearest smiles.

“When I think, my dear cousin,” she said, “about the miserable death of Justine Moritz, I no longer see the world and its ways as I once did. Before, I viewed the stories of vice and injustice that I read in books or heard from others as tales of ancient times or imaginary evils. And if she really had committed the crime for which she suffered, she would certainly have been the most depraved of human beings. For the sake of a few jewels, to have murdered the son of her benefactor and friend—a child she had nursed from birth and seemed to love as if he were her own! I could not consent to the death of any human being, but I would certainly have thought such a creature unfit to remain in society. But she was innocent. I know it, I feel she was innocent. You share the same opinion, and that confirms my belief. Alas, Victor, when falsehood can look so much like the truth, who can be certain of happiness? I feel as if I were walking on the edge of a cliff, toward which thousands are crowding and trying to push me into the abyss. William and Justine were murdered, and the murderer escapes. He walks about the world free, and perhaps even respected. But even if I were condemned to die on the scaffold for the same crimes, I would not change places with such a wretch.”

I listened to this speech with the most extreme agony. I was the true murderer—not in deed, but in effect. Elizabeth read my anguish in my face and, kindly taking my hand, said, “My dearest friend, you must calm yourself. Dear Victor, banish these dark passions. Remember the friends around you, who place all their hopes in you. Have we lost the power to make you happy? Ah! While we love, while we are true to each other, here in this land of peace and beauty, your native country, we may enjoy every quiet blessing—what can disturb our peace?”

And could such words from her, whom I treasured above every other gift of fortune, not be enough to chase away the fiend that lurked in my heart? Even as she spoke, I drew closer to her, as if in terror that at that very moment the destroyer might be near to rob me of her.

So neither the tenderness of friendship, nor the beauty of earth, nor of heaven, could rescue my soul from misery. The very tones of love were powerless. I was wrapped in a cloud that no healing influence could pierce. The wounded deer dragging its failing limbs to some untouched thicket, there to gaze at the arrow that had pierced it and to die, was but a picture of me.

Sometimes I could cope with the sullen despair that overwhelmed me, but sometimes the whirlwind passions of my soul drove me to seek relief from my intolerable feelings through physical exercise and change of place. It was during one of these episodes that I suddenly left my home and headed towards the nearby Alpine valleys. I sought in the magnificence and eternity of such scenes to forget myself and my fleeting, human sorrows. My wanderings were directed towards the valley of Chamounix. I had visited it frequently during my boyhood. Six years had passed since then: I was a wreck, but nothing had changed in those wild and enduring scenes.

I performed the first part of my journey on horseback. I afterwards hired a mule, as it was more sure-footed and less likely to be injured on these rugged roads. The weather was fine; it was about the middle of August, nearly two months after the death of Justine, that miserable time from which I dated all my woe. The weight upon my spirit was noticeably lightened as I plunged deeper into the ravine of Arve. The immense mountains and precipices that hung over me on every side, the sound of the river raging among the rocks, and the dashing of the waterfalls around spoke of a power as mighty as God himself—and I ceased to fear or bow before any being less almighty than the one that had created and ruled the elements, here displayed in their most terrifying form. Still, as I climbed higher, the valley took on a more magnificent and astonishing character. Ruined castles hanging on the cliffs of pine-covered mountains, the rushing Arve, and cottages peeping out here and there from among the trees formed a scene of singular beauty. But it was made greater and more sublime by the mighty Alps, whose white and shining pyramids and domes towered above all, as if belonging to another earth, the homes of another race of beings.

I passed the bridge of Pélissier, where the ravine formed by the river opened before me, and I began to climb the mountain that hangs over it. Soon after, I entered the valley of Chamounix. This valley is more wonderful and sublime, but not so beautiful and picturesque as that of Servox, through which I had just passed. The high and snowy mountains were its immediate boundaries, but I saw no more ruined castles and fertile fields. Immense glaciers approached the road; I heard the rumbling thunder of the falling avalanche and marked the smoke of its passage. Mont Blanc, the supreme and magnificent Mont Blanc, rose up from the surrounding aiguilles, and its tremendous dome overlooked the valley.

A tingling, long-lost sense of pleasure often came over me during this journey. Some turn in the road, some new object suddenly seen and recognized, reminded me of days gone by and were associated with the lighthearted gaiety of boyhood. The very winds whispered in soothing tones, and maternal Nature told me to weep no more. Then again the kindly influence stopped working—I found myself chained once more to grief and indulging in all the misery of reflection. Then I spurred on my animal, striving to forget the world, my fears, and more than all, myself—or, in a more desperate fashion, I got down and threw myself on the grass, weighed down by horror and despair.

At length I arrived at the village of Chamounix. My body and mind, which had endured extreme fatigue, now gave way to exhaustion. For a short time I remained at the window, watching the pale lightning play above Mont Blanc and listening to the rushing of the Arve as it continued its noisy course below. Those soothing sounds acted like a lullaby on my too-sharp senses. When I placed my head upon my pillow, sleep crept over me. I felt it as it came and blessed the giver of oblivion.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 10

I spent the following day roaming through the valley. I stood beside the sources of the Arveiron, which rise in a glacier that is slowly advancing down from the summit of the hills to barricade the valley. The abrupt sides of vast mountains were before me; the icy wall of the glacier overhung me; a few shattered pines were scattered around. The solemn silence of this glorious presence-chamber of imperial Nature was broken only by the brawling waves or the fall of some vast fragment, the thunder sound of the avalanche or the cracking of the accumulated ice, reverberated along the mountains. Through the silent working of immutable laws, the ice was ever and anon rent and torn, as if it had been but a plaything in their hands. These sublime and magnificent scenes afforded me the greatest consolation that I was capable of receiving. They elevated me from all littleness of feeling, and although they did not remove my grief, they subdued and tranquillised it. In some degree, also, they diverted my mind from the thoughts over which it had brooded for the last month. I retired to rest at night; my slumbers, as it were, were waited on and ministered to by the assemblance of grand shapes which I had contemplated during the day. They congregated round me; the unstained snowy mountain-top, the glittering pinnacle, the pine woods, and ragged bare ravine, the eagle, soaring amidst the clouds—they all gathered round me and bade me be at peace.

Where had they fled when the next morning I awoke? All of soul-inspiring power fled with sleep, and dark melancholy clouded every thought. The rain was pouring in torrents, and thick mists hid the summits of the mountains, so that I could not even see the faces of those mighty friends. Still I would penetrate their misty veil and seek them in their cloudy retreats. What were rain and storm to me? My mule was brought to the door, and I resolved to ascend to the summit of Montanvert. I remembered the effect that the view of the tremendous and ever-moving glacier had produced upon my mind when I first saw it. It had then filled me with a sublime ecstasy that gave wings to the soul and allowed it to soar from the obscure world to light and joy. The sight of the awful and majestic in nature had indeed always the effect of solemnising my mind and causing me to forget the passing cares of life. I determined to go without a guide, for I was well acquainted with the path, and the presence of another would destroy the solitary grandeur of the scene.

The climb is steep, but the path winds back and forth constantly, which helps you overcome the mountain's sheer face. The landscape is terribly bleak. In a thousand places you can see traces of winter avalanches, where trees lie broken and scattered on the ground—some completely destroyed, others bent and leaning against the mountain's rocky outcrops or resting sideways on other trees. As you climb higher, the path is crossed by snow-filled ravines, down which stones continually roll from above. One of these is especially dangerous, since the slightest sound—even speaking in a loud voice—creates a vibration in the air strong enough to bring destruction down on the speaker's head. The pines are not tall or lush, but they are dark and give the scene a severe atmosphere. I looked down at the valley below. Vast mists were rising from the rivers that ran through it and curling in thick clouds around the opposite mountains, whose peaks were hidden in the uniform clouds, while rain poured from the dark sky and deepened the melancholy impression I received from everything around me. Alas! Why does man boast of having finer feelings than animals? It only makes them more dependent beings. If our impulses were limited to hunger, thirst, and desire, we might be nearly free. Presently a breeze cleared away the cloud, and I descended onto the glacier. The surface is very uneven, rising like the waves of a rough sea, dipping low, and broken by deep crevasses. The field of ice is almost a league wide, but I spent nearly two hours crossing it. The opposite mountain is a bare, sheer rock face. From where I now stood, Montanvert was directly opposite, a league away; and above it rose Mont Blanc, in awe-inspiring majesty. The sea—or rather the vast river of ice—wound among the mountains that surrounded it, their airy summits hanging over its hollows. Their icy, glittering peaks shone in the sunlight above the clouds. As he said this he led the way across the ice, and I followed. My heart was full, and I did not answer him. I had until now supposed him to be the murderer of my brother, and I eagerly sought a confirmation or denial of this opinion. For the first time, also, I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were, and that I ought to make him happy before I complained of his wickedness. These motives urged me to agree to his request. We crossed the ice, therefore, and climbed the opposite rock. The air was cold, and the rain again began to fall. We entered the hut, the fiend with an air of triumph, I with a heavy heart and low spirits. It is very hard for me to remember the earliest time of my life; everything from that period seems confused and unclear. A strange rush of sensations came over me, and I saw, felt, heard, and smelled all at once. It was a long time before I learned to tell the operations of my different senses apart. Sometimes I tried to imitate the pleasant songs of the birds but could not. I reflected on this, and by touching the various branches, I discovered the cause and busied myself collecting a great quantity of wood, so that I might dry it and have a plentiful supply of fire. When night came on and brought sleep with it, I was in the greatest fear that my fire should go out. I covered it carefully with dry wood and leaves and placed wet branches upon it; and then, spreading my cloak, I lay on the ground and sank into sleep.

It was morning when I awoke, and my first care was to visit the fire. I uncovered it, and a gentle breeze quickly fanned it into a flame. I observed this as well and made a fan of branches, which roused the embers when they were nearly extinguished. When night came again I found, with pleasure, that the fire gave light as well as heat and that the discovery of this element was useful to me in my food, for I found that some of the scraps the travellers had left had been roasted, and tasted much more savoury than the berries I gathered from the trees. I tried, therefore, to prepare my food in the same manner, placing it on the live embers. I found that the berries were spoiled by this process, and the nuts and roots much improved.

Food, however, became scarce, and I often spent the whole day searching in vain for a few acorns to ease the pangs of hunger. When I found this, I resolved to leave the place that I had lived in until then, to seek one where the few wants I experienced would be more easily satisfied. In this move I greatly lamented the loss of the fire which I had obtained through accident and did not know how to reproduce. I gave several hours to the serious consideration of this difficulty, but I was obliged to give up all attempts to supply it, and wrapping myself up in my cloak, I struck across the wood towards the setting sun. I passed three days in these wanderings and at length discovered the open country. A great fall of snow had taken place the night before, and the fields were of one uniform white; the appearance was bleak, and I found my feet chilled by the cold damp substance that covered the ground.

It was about seven in the morning, and I longed to find food and shelter. At length I noticed a small hut on a rising piece of ground, which had probably been built for the convenience of some shepherd. This was a new sight to me, and I examined the structure with great curiosity. Finding the door open, I entered. An old man sat inside, near a fire, over which he was preparing his breakfast. He turned when he heard a noise, and when he saw me he shrieked loudly. Then he ran out of the hut and across the fields with a speed that his frail body hardly seemed capable of. His appearance, so different from any I had ever seen before, and his flight somewhat surprised me. But I was delighted by the appearance of the hut. Here the snow and rain could not get in, and the ground was dry. To me it seemed as exquisite and divine a retreat as Pandæmonium appeared to the demons of hell after their sufferings in the lake of fire. I greedily devoured the remains of the shepherd’s breakfast, which consisted of bread, cheese, milk, and wine. I did not like the wine, however. Then, overcome by fatigue, I lay down among some straw and fell asleep.

It was noon when I awoke. Drawn by the warmth of the sun, which shone brightly on the white ground, I decided to continue my travels. I put the remains of the peasant’s breakfast in a wallet I found, and then I walked across the fields for several hours, until at sunset I arrived at a village. How miraculous this seemed! The huts, the neater cottages, and the stately houses engaged my admiration by turns. The vegetables in the gardens, and the milk and cheese that I saw placed at the windows of some of the cottages, tempted my appetite. I entered one of the best of these, but I had hardly placed my foot inside the door before the children shrieked and one of the women fainted. The whole village was roused. Some fled, and some attacked me, until, grievously bruised by stones and many other kinds of thrown weapons, I escaped to the open country. Fearfully, I took refuge in a low hovel, quite bare, which made a wretched appearance after the palaces I had seen in the village. This hovel, however, was joined to a cottage of a neat and pleasant appearance, but after my recent, dearly bought experience, I dared not enter it. My place of refuge was built of wood, but it was so low that I could barely sit upright in it. No wood, however, was placed on the earth that formed the floor, but it was dry. And although the wind entered through countless cracks, I found it an agreeable shelter from the snow and rain.

Here, then, I retreated and lay down, happy to have found a shelter, however miserable, from the harshness of the season and, even more, from the cruelty of man. As soon as morning dawned, I crept from my kennel so that I could look at the nearby cottage and see if I could remain in the shelter I had found. It was situated against the back of the cottage and was surrounded on its exposed sides by a pigsty and a clear pool of water. One part was open, and that was where I had crept in. But now I covered every gap through which I might be seen with stones and wood, though I arranged them so that I could move them when I needed to pass out. All the light I enjoyed came through the pigsty, and that was enough for me.

Having thus arranged my dwelling and carpeted it with clean straw, I retired, for I saw the figure of a man at a distance, and I remembered too well my treatment the night before to trust myself in his power. I had first, however, provided for my sustenance for that day by a loaf of coarse bread, which I stole, and a cup with which I could drink more conveniently than from my hand of the pure water which flowed by my retreat. The floor was a little raised, so that it was kept perfectly dry, and by its nearness to the chimney of the cottage it was tolerably warm.

Being thus provided, I resolved to reside in this hovel until something should occur which might alter my determination. It was indeed a paradise compared to the bleak forest, my former residence, the rain-dropping branches, and dank earth. I ate my breakfast with pleasure and was about to remove a plank to get myself a little water when I heard a step, and looking through a small chink, I beheld a young creature, with a pail on her head, passing before my hovel. The girl was young and of gentle manner, unlike what I have since found cottagers and farmhouse servants to be. Yet she was plainly dressed, a coarse blue petticoat and a linen jacket being her only clothing; her fair hair was plaited but not adorned: she looked patient yet sad. I lost sight of her, and in about a quarter of an hour she returned bearing the pail, which was now partly filled with milk. As she walked along, seemingly weighed down by the burden, a young man met her, whose face expressed a deeper sadness. Uttering a few sounds with an air of melancholy, he took the pail from her head and carried it to the cottage himself. She followed, and they disappeared. Presently I saw the young man again, with some tools in his hand, cross the field behind the cottage; and the girl was also busy, sometimes in the house and sometimes in the yard.

On examining my dwelling, I found that one of the windows of the cottage had formerly occupied a part of it, but the panes had been filled up with wood. In one of these was a small and almost invisible chink through which the eye could just penetrate. Through this crevice a small room was visible, whitewashed and clean but very bare of furniture. In one corner, near a small fire, sat an old man, leaning his head on his hands in a dejected attitude. The young girl was occupied in tidying the cottage; but presently she took something out of a drawer, which employed her hands, and she sat down beside the old man, who, taking up an instrument, began to play and to produce sounds sweeter than the voice of the thrush or the nightingale. It was a lovely sight, even to me, poor wretch who had never beheld anything beautiful before. The silver hair and kind face of the aged cottager won my reverence, while the gentle manners of the girl drew my love. He played a sweet mournful air which I saw drew tears from the eyes of his amiable companion, of which the old man took no notice, until she sobbed audibly; he then spoke a few words, and the fair creature, leaving her work, knelt at his feet. He raised her and smiled with such kindness and affection that I felt sensations of a peculiar and overpowering nature; they were a mixture of pain and pleasure, such as I had never before experienced, either from hunger or cold, warmth or food; and I withdrew from the window, unable to bear these emotions.

Soon after this the young man returned, carrying a load of wood on his shoulders. The girl met him at the door and helped him set it down. She took some of the wood into the cottage and placed it on the fire. Then she and the young man went apart into a corner of the cottage, and he showed her a large loaf and a piece of cheese. She seemed pleased and went into the garden for some roots and plants, which she placed in water and then put on the fire. She afterwards continued her work, while the young man went into the garden and seemed busy digging and pulling up roots. After he had worked for about an hour, the young woman joined him and they entered the cottage together.

The old man had, in the meantime, been thoughtful, but when his companions appeared he put on a more cheerful manner, and they sat down to eat. The meal was quickly finished. The young woman again busied herself with tidying the cottage. The old man walked in front of the cottage in the sun for a few minutes, leaning on the arm of the young man. Nothing could be more beautiful than the contrast between these two excellent people. One was old, with silver hair and a face glowing with kindness and love. The younger was slight and graceful in his figure, and his features were shaped with the finest symmetry, yet his eyes and manner expressed the deepest sadness and despair. The old man returned to the cottage, and the young man, with tools different from those he had used in the morning, walked off across the fields.

Night quickly fell, but to my great surprise, I found that the cottagers had a way of making light last by using candles. I was delighted to find that the setting of the sun did not put an end to the pleasure I felt in watching my human neighbours. In the evening the young girl and her companion were busy with various tasks that I did not understand. The old man again took up the instrument that had produced the wonderful sounds that had enchanted me in the morning. As soon as he had finished, the young man began, not to play, but to make sounds that were monotonous, and neither like the harmony of the old man’s instrument nor the songs of the birds. I later found that he was reading aloud, but at that time I knew nothing of the science of words or letters.

After the family had been occupied like this for a short time, they put out their lights and went, as I guessed, to rest.

I lay on my straw, but I could not sleep. I thought about what had happened that day. What struck me most was the gentle manners of these people, and I longed to join them, but I did not dare. I remembered too well how badly the barbarous villagers had treated me the night before. I resolved that, whatever course of action I might later think was right, for now I would stay quietly in my hovel, watching and trying to discover the motives behind their actions.

The cottagers got up the next morning before sunrise. The young woman tidied the cottage and prepared the food, and the young man left after the first meal.

This day passed in the same routine as the one before it. The young man was constantly busy outdoors, and the girl with various hard tasks inside. The old man, whom I soon realized was blind, spent his free time playing his instrument or in quiet thought. Nothing could exceed the love and respect that the younger cottagers showed toward their elderly companion. They performed every small act of affection and duty for him with gentleness, and he rewarded them with his kind smiles.

They were not entirely happy. The young man and his companion often went apart and seemed to weep. I saw no reason for their unhappiness, but I was deeply affected by it. If such lovely creatures were miserable, it was less strange that I, an imperfect and solitary being, should be wretched. Yet why were these gentle beings unhappy? They had a delightful house (for such it was in my eyes) and every comfort; they had a fire to warm them when cold and delicious food when hungry; they wore excellent clothes; and, even more, they enjoyed each other's company and conversation, exchanging looks of affection and kindness every day. What did their tears mean? Did they really express pain? At first I could not answer these questions, but constant attention and time explained many things to me that had at first seemed mysterious.

A considerable period passed before I discovered one of the causes of this kind family's uneasiness: it was poverty, and they suffered that hardship to a very painful degree. Their food consisted entirely of vegetables from their garden and the milk of one cow, which gave very little during the winter, when its owners could barely find food to keep it alive. I believe they often suffered the pangs of hunger very sharply, especially the two younger cottagers, for several times they placed food before the old man while keeping none for themselves.

This act of kindness moved me deeply. I had been in the habit, during the night, of stealing part of their store for my own use, but when I found that by doing this I caused pain to the cottagers, I stopped and satisfied myself with berries, nuts, and roots that I gathered from a nearby wood.

I also discovered another way in which I could help with their work. I found that the young man spent a large part of each day collecting wood for the family fire, and during the night I often took his tools, whose use I quickly figured out, and brought home enough firewood to last for several days.

I remember the first time I did this. When the young woman opened the door in the morning, she looked greatly astonished to see a large pile of wood outside. She called out in a loud voice, and the young man joined her. He also seemed surprised. I was pleased to see that he did not go to the forest that day, but spent it repairing the cottage and tending the garden.

Gradually I made a discovery of even greater importance. I found that these people had a way of sharing their experiences and feelings with one another through spoken sounds. I noticed that the words they spoke sometimes brought pleasure or pain, smiles or sadness, to the faces and minds of those listening. This was truly a godlike skill, and I desperately wanted to learn it. But every attempt I made failed. Their speech was fast, and the words they used had no obvious connection to visible objects, so I could not find any clue to help me understand what they referred to. However, through great effort, and after staying in my shelter for several cycles of the moon, I discovered the names they gave to some of the most common objects of conversation. I learned and used the words fire, milk, bread, and wood. I also learned the names of the cottagers themselves. The young man and his companion each had several names, but the old man had only one, which was father. The girl was called sister or Agatha, and the young man Felix, brother, or son. I cannot describe the joy I felt when I learned the meanings attached to each of these sounds and was able to say them. I recognized several other words without yet being able to understand or use them, such as good, dearest, unhappy.

I spent the winter this way. The gentle manners and beauty of the cottagers made me grow very fond of them. When they were unhappy, I felt sad. When they were joyful, I shared in their happiness. I saw few other human beings besides them, and if anyone else happened to enter the cottage, their rough manners and clumsy movements only made me appreciate my friends' superior qualities even more. I could see that the old man often tried to encourage his children, as he sometimes called them, to shake off their sadness. He would speak in a cheerful tone, with a kindness that gave pleasure even to me. Agatha listened respectfully, her eyes sometimes filling with tears, which she tried to wipe away without being noticed. But I usually found that her face and voice were more cheerful after listening to her father's encouragement. It was not the same with Felix. He was always the saddest of the group, and even to my inexperienced senses, he seemed to have suffered more deeply than his companions. But if his face was more sorrowful, his voice was more cheerful than his sister's, especially when he spoke to the old man.

“I could mention countless examples, small as they were, that showed the kind nature of these good cottagers. Even in the middle of poverty and need, Felix happily brought his sister the first little white flower that peeked out from under the snowy ground. Early in the morning, before she had gotten up, he cleared away the snow that blocked her path to the milk-house, drew water from the well, and brought wood from the outhouse. To his constant surprise, he always found his wood supply had been refilled by an invisible hand. During the day, I believe, he sometimes worked for a neighboring farmer, because he often went out and did not return until dinner, yet brought no wood back with him. At other times he worked in the garden, but since there was little to do in the frosty season, he read to the old man and Agatha.

“This reading had confused me greatly at first, but gradually I realized that he made many of the same sounds when he read as when he spoke. I guessed, therefore, that he found on the paper symbols for speech that he understood, and I desperately longed to understand these too. But how was that possible when I did not even understand the sounds the symbols stood for? I did improve noticeably in this skill, but not enough to follow any kind of conversation, even though I applied my whole mind to the effort. I clearly saw that, although I eagerly wanted to reveal myself to the cottagers, I should not try until I had first mastered their language. That knowledge might make them overlook the deformity of my appearance, for I had also become aware of this through the contrast constantly before my eyes.

“I had admired the perfect forms of my cottagers—their grace, beauty, and delicate complexions. But how terrified I was when I saw myself in a clear pool! At first I jumped back, unable to believe that it was really me reflected in the mirror. When I became fully convinced that I was in fact the monster that I am, I was filled with the bitterest feelings of despair and shame. Alas! I did not yet fully know the fatal effects of this miserable deformity.

“As the sun grew warmer and the daylight lasted longer, the snow disappeared, and I saw the bare trees and the black earth. From this time Felix was busier, and the heartbreaking signs of approaching famine went away. Their food, as I later learned, was plain, but it was healthy, and they had enough of it. Several new kinds of plants came up in the garden, which they prepared and ate. These signs of comfort increased daily as the season advanced.

“The old man, leaning on his son, walked each day at noon when it did not rain—as I learned it was called when the sky poured down its waters. This happened often, but a strong wind quickly dried the earth, and the season became far more pleasant than it had been.

My way of life in my hovel was always the same. During the morning I watched the movements of the cottagers, and when they went off to their various tasks, I slept. The rest of the day I spent observing my friends. When they had gone to bed, if there was any moon or the night was starry, I went into the woods and gathered my own food and fuel for the cottage. When I came back, as often as it was needed, I cleared the snow from their path and did those tasks that I had seen Felix do. I later found that these labors, done by an invisible hand, greatly amazed them; and once or twice I heard them, on these occasions, say the words “good spirit, wonderful”; but I did not then understand what these terms meant.

My thoughts now became more active, and I longed to discover the motives and feelings of these lovely creatures. I was curious to know why Felix seemed so miserable and Agatha so sad. I thought—foolish wretch!—that it might be in my power to restore happiness to these deserving people. When I slept or was away, the forms of the venerable blind father, the gentle Agatha, and the excellent Felix drifted before me. I looked upon them as superior beings who would decide my future destiny. In my imagination I formed a thousand pictures of presenting myself to them, and of how they would receive me. I imagined that they would be disgusted, until, through my gentle manner and calming words, I should first win their favor and afterwards their love.

These thoughts cheered me and led me to apply with fresh energy to learning the art of language. My organs were indeed harsh, but flexible; and although my voice was very unlike the soft music of their tones, yet I pronounced such words as I understood with tolerable ease. It was like the ass and the lapdog; yet surely the gentle ass whose intentions were affectionate, although his manners were rough, deserved better treatment than blows and curses.

The pleasant showers and gentle warmth of spring greatly changed the appearance of the earth. Men who before this change seemed to have been hidden in caves now spread out and were busy with various kinds of cultivation. The birds sang in more cheerful notes, and the leaves began to bud forth on the trees. Happy, happy earth! Fit home for gods, which so short a time before was bleak, damp, and unhealthy. My spirits were lifted by the enchanting appearance of nature; the past was wiped from my memory, the present was calm, and the future was gilded by bright rays of hope and expectations of joy.

I now hasten to the more moving part of my story. I shall relate events that impressed me with feelings which, from what I had been, have made me what I am.

Spring advanced rapidly; the weather became fine and the skies cloudless. It surprised me that what before was desert and gloomy should now bloom with the most beautiful flowers and verdure. My senses were gratified and refreshed by a thousand scents of delight and a thousand sights of beauty.

Felix replied in a cheerful accent, and the old man was recommencing his music when someone tapped at the door.

Her hair of a shining raven black, and curiously braided; her eyes were dark, but gentle, although animated; her features of a regular proportion, and her complexion wondrously fair, each cheek tinged with a lovely pink.

She appeared affected by different feelings; wiping a few tears from her lovely eyes, she held out her hand to Felix, who kissed it rapturously and called her, as well as I could distinguish, his sweet Arabian. She did not appear to understand him, but smiled. He assisted her to dismount, and dismissing her guide, conducted her into the cottage. Some conversation took place between him and his father, and the young stranger knelt at the old man's feet and would have kissed his hand, but he raised her and embraced her affectionately.

They made many signs that I did not understand, but I saw that her presence brought happiness into the cottage, lifting their sadness the way the sun clears away the morning mist. The stranger learned about twenty words in the first lesson; most of them were words I already understood, but I benefited from the others.

“My days were spent paying close attention so that I might master the language more quickly; and I can boast that I improved more rapidly than the Arabian, who understood very little and spoke in broken accents, while I understood and could imitate almost every word that was spoken.

“While I improved in speech, I also learned the science of letters as it was taught to the stranger, and this opened before me a wide field for wonder and delight.

“The book from which Felix instructed Safie was Volney’s _Ruins of Empires_. I would not have understood the meaning of this book if Felix, in reading it, had not given very detailed explanations. He appeared at one time a mere scion of the evil principle and at another as all that can be conceived of noble and godlike. “These words led me to turn towards myself. I learned that the possessions most esteemed by your fellow creatures were high and unsullied descent united with riches. What a strange thing knowledge is! Once it takes hold of the mind, it clings to it like lichen to a rock. Sometimes I wished to shake off all thought and feeling, but I learned there was only one way to overcome the sensation of pain, and that was death—a state I feared yet did not understand. But where were my friends and relations? No father had watched over my infant days, no mother had blessed me with smiles and caresses; or if they had, all my past life was now a blot, a blind emptiness in which I could distinguish nothing. Some time passed before I learned the history of my friends. It was one that could not fail to impress itself deeply on my mind, unfolding as it did a number of circumstances, each interesting and wonderful to one so utterly inexperienced as I was.

The name of the old man was De Lacey. He was descended from a good family in France, where he had lived for many years in comfort, respected by his superiors and beloved by his equals. His son had been brought up to serve his country, and Agatha had ranked with ladies of the highest distinction. A few months before my arrival they had lived in a large and luxurious city called Paris, surrounded by friends and possessing every enjoyment that virtue, refinement of intellect, or taste, accompanied by a moderate fortune, could provide.

The father of Safie had been the cause of their ruin. He was a Turkish merchant and had lived in Paris for many years, when, for some reason which I could not learn, he became offensive to the government. He was seized and thrown into prison on the very day that Safie arrived from Constantinople to join him. He was tried and condemned to death. The injustice of his sentence was very obvious; all Paris was outraged; and it was believed that his religion and wealth, rather than the crime alleged against him, had been the cause of his condemnation.

Felix had happened to be present at the trial; his horror and indignation were uncontrollable when he heard the decision of the court. He made, at that moment, a solemn vow to rescue him and then looked around for the means. After many unsuccessful attempts to gain admittance to the prison, he found a strongly barred window in an unguarded part of the building, which lit the dungeon of the unfortunate Muhammadan, who, loaded with chains, waited in despair for the execution of the barbarous sentence. Felix visited the grate at night and made known to the prisoner his intentions to help him. The Turk, amazed and delighted, tried to stir the zeal of his rescuer with promises of reward and wealth. Felix rejected his offers with contempt, yet when he saw the lovely Safie, who was allowed to visit her father and who by her gestures expressed her lively gratitude, the young man could not help admitting to himself that the captive possessed a treasure which would fully reward his toil and risk.

The Turk quickly perceived the impression that his daughter had made on the heart of Felix and tried to secure him more entirely in his interests by promising her hand in marriage as soon as he should be conveyed to a place of safety. Felix was too sensitive to accept this offer, yet he looked forward to the possibility of the event as to the fulfillment of his happiness.

During the following days, while the preparations were going forward for the escape of the merchant, the zeal of Felix was warmed by several letters that he received from this lovely girl, who found means to express her thoughts in the language of her lover with the help of an old man, a servant of her father who understood French. She thanked him in the most passionate terms for his intended services towards her parent, and at the same time she gently mourned her own fate.

“I have copies of these letters, for during my time in the hovel I managed to get writing materials, and the letters were often in the hands of Felix or Agatha. Before I leave, I will give them to you; they will prove the truth of my story. But for now, since the sun is already far down, I will only have time to repeat their substance to you.

“Safie related that her mother was a Christian Arab, seized and made a slave by the Turks. Recommended by her beauty, she had won the heart of Safie's father, who married her. The young girl spoke in high and enthusiastic terms of her mother, who, born in freedom, rejected the bondage to which she was now reduced. She taught her daughter the principles of her religion and taught her to aspire to higher powers of intellect and an independence of spirit forbidden to the female followers of Muhammad. This lady died, but her lessons were indelibly impressed on the mind of Safie, who sickened at the prospect of returning to Asia and being shut up within the walls of a harem, allowed only to occupy herself with childish amusements, ill-suited to the temper of her soul, now accustomed to grand ideas and a noble desire for virtue. The prospect of marrying a Christian and remaining in a country where women were allowed to take a rank in society was enchanting to her.

“The day for the execution of the Turk was fixed, but on the night before it he left his prison and before morning was many leagues from Paris. Felix had obtained passports in the name of his father, sister, and himself. He had previously communicated his plan to his father, who aided the deceit by leaving his house under the pretence of a journey and hiding himself, with his daughter, in an obscure part of Paris.

“Felix led the fugitives through France to Lyons and across Mont Cenis to Leghorn, where the merchant had decided to wait for a favourable opportunity of passing into some part of the Turkish dominions.

“Safie resolved to remain with her father until the moment of his departure. Before that time the Turk renewed his promise that she should be united to his deliverer; and Felix remained with them in expectation of that event. In the meantime he enjoyed the society of the Arabian, who showed towards him the simplest and tenderest affection. They conversed with one another through the means of an interpreter, and sometimes with the interpretation of looks; and Safie sang to him the divine airs of her native country.

“The Turk allowed this intimacy to take place and encouraged the hopes of the youthful lovers, while in his heart he had formed far other plans. He hated the idea that his daughter should be united to a Christian, but he feared the resentment of Felix if he should appear lukewarm, for he knew that he was still in the power of his deliverer if he should choose to betray him to the Italian state where they lived. He turned over a thousand plans by which he might prolong the deceit until it might be no longer necessary, and secretly take his daughter with him when he departed. His plans were helped by the news which arrived from Paris.

The government of France was furious that their prisoner had escaped, and they worked hard to find and punish the person who helped him. Felix’s part in the plot was soon discovered, and De Lacey and Agatha were thrown into prison. When the news reached Felix, it woke him from his dream of happiness. His blind, elderly father and his gentle sister were locked in a filthy dungeon while he enjoyed the open air and the company of the woman he loved. This thought tortured him. He quickly arranged with the Turk that if the Turk found a good chance to escape before Felix could return to Italy, Safie would stay as a boarder at a convent in Leghorn. Then, leaving the lovely Arabian woman, he hurried to Paris and gave himself up to the law, hoping this would free De Lacey and Agatha.

He did not succeed. They stayed in prison for five months before the trial took place. The result took away their fortune and condemned them to permanent exile from their native country.

They found a miserable refuge in the cottage in Germany, where I discovered them. Felix soon learned that the treacherous Turk, for whom he and his family had suffered such extreme oppression, had betrayed all good feeling and honor. When the Turk discovered that his rescuer had been reduced to poverty and ruin, he left Italy with his daughter, insultingly sending Felix a small amount of money to help him, as he said, with some plan for his future support.

These were the events that weighed on Felix’s heart and made him, when I first saw him, the most miserable member of his family. He could have endured poverty, and while this hardship had been the reward of his virtue, he took pride in it. But the ingratitude of the Turk and the loss of his beloved Safie were misfortunes more bitter and impossible to repair. The arrival of the Arabian woman now brought new life to his soul.

When the news reached Leghorn that Felix had lost his wealth and rank, the merchant ordered his daughter to think no more of her lover and to prepare to return to her native country. Safie’s generous nature was outraged by this command. She tried to reason with her father, but he left her angrily, repeating his tyrannical order.

A few days later, the Turk entered his daughter’s room and told her hastily that he had reason to believe his presence in Leghorn had been revealed and that he would soon be handed over to the French government. He had therefore hired a ship to take him to Constantinople, and he would sail in a few hours. He intended to leave his daughter in the care of a trusted servant, to follow later at her leisure with the greater part of his property, which had not yet arrived in Leghorn.

When she was alone, Safie thought carefully about what she should do in this emergency. Living in Turkey was hateful to her; both her religion and her feelings were against it. Through some papers of her father’s that fell into her hands, she learned of her lover’s exile and the name of the place where he now lived. She hesitated for some time, but at last she made up her mind. Taking some jewels that belonged to her and a sum of money, she left Italy with an attendant, a native of Leghorn who understood the common language of Turkey, and departed for Germany.

“She arrived safely at a town about twenty leagues from De Lacey’s cottage, when her attendant fell dangerously ill. Safie nursed her with devoted affection, but the poor girl died. The Arabian was left alone, unfamiliar with the language of the country and completely ignorant of its customs. She fell, however, into good hands. The Italian had mentioned the name of the place they were heading for, and after her death the woman of the house where they had stayed made sure that Safie arrived safely at her lover’s cottage.”

###CHAPTER### Chapter 15

Such was the history of my beloved cottagers. It impressed me deeply. From the views of social life it presented, I learned to admire their virtues and to disapprove of the vices of mankind.

As yet I looked upon crime as a distant evil. Benevolence and generosity were ever present before me, stirring within me a desire to become an actor in the busy scene where so many admirable qualities were called forth and displayed. But in giving an account of the progress of my intellect, I must not omit something that happened in the beginning of August of that same year.

One night during my usual visit to the neighbouring wood, where I collected my own food and brought home firewood for my protectors, I found on the ground a leather suitcase containing several articles of clothing and some books. I eagerly seized the prize and returned with it to my hovel. Fortunately the books were written in the language whose basics I had learned at the cottage. They consisted of Paradise Lost, a volume of Plutarch's Lives, and The Sorrows of Werter. Possessing these treasures gave me extreme delight. I now continually studied and exercised my mind upon these histories while my friends were busy with their ordinary occupations.

I can hardly describe to you the effect of these books. They produced in me an endless stream of new images and feelings that sometimes raised me to ecstasy but more frequently sank me into the lowest dejection. In The Sorrows of Werter, besides the interest of its simple and affecting story, so many opinions are discussed and so much light is thrown upon subjects that had until then been obscure to me, that I found in it a never-ending source of speculation and astonishment. The gentle and domestic manners it described, combined with lofty sentiments and feelings that had for their object something beyond the self, matched well with my experience among my protectors and with the needs that were always alive in my own heart. But I thought Werter himself a more divine being than I had ever seen or imagined. His character contained no pretension, but it sank deep. The discussions upon death and suicide were calculated to fill me with wonder. I did not pretend to judge the merits of the case, yet I inclined towards the opinions of the hero, whose death I wept over without precisely understanding it.

As I read, however, I applied much of it personally to my own feelings and condition. I found myself similar yet at the same time strangely unlike the beings I read about and whose conversation I overheard. I sympathised with and partly understood them, but my mind was unformed. I depended on no one and was related to no one. The path of my departure was free, and there was no one to mourn my destruction. My appearance was hideous and my stature gigantic. What did this mean? Who was I? What was I? Where did I come from? What was my destination? These questions continually returned, but I was unable to answer them.

The volume of Plutarch’s Lives which I possessed contained the histories of the first founders of the ancient republics. This book had a far different effect upon me from the Sorrows of Werter. I learned despondency and gloom from Werter’s imaginations, but Plutarch taught me high thoughts. He elevated me above the wretched sphere of my own reflections, to admire and love the heroes of past ages. Many things I read surpassed my understanding and experience. I had a very confused knowledge of kingdoms, wide extents of country, mighty rivers, and boundless seas. But I was perfectly unacquainted with towns and large assemblages of men. The cottage of my protectors had been the only school in which I had studied human nature, but this book developed new and mightier scenes of action. I read of men concerned in public affairs, governing or massacring their species. I felt the greatest ardour for virtue rise within me, and abhorrence for vice, as far as I understood the signification of those terms, relative as they were, as I applied them, to pleasure and pain alone. Induced by these feelings, I was of course led to admire peaceable lawgivers, Numa, Solon, and Lycurgus, in preference to Romulus and Theseus. The patriarchal lives of my protectors caused these impressions to take a firm hold on my mind. Perhaps, if my first introduction to humanity had been made by a young soldier, burning for glory and slaughter, I should have been imbued with different sensations.

But Paradise Lost excited different and far deeper emotions. I read it, as I had read the other volumes which had fallen into my hands, as a true history. It moved every feeling of wonder and awe that the picture of an omnipotent God warring with his creatures was capable of exciting. I often referred the several situations, as their similarity struck me, to my own. Like Adam, I was apparently united by no link to any other being in existence. But his state was far different from mine in every other respect. He had come forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the especial care of his Creator. He was allowed to converse with and acquire knowledge from beings of a superior nature, but I was wretched, helpless, and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition, for often, like him, when I viewed the bliss of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.

Another circumstance strengthened and confirmed these feelings. Soon after I arrived in the hovel, I discovered some papers in the pocket of the coat I had taken from your laboratory. At first I had ignored them, but now that I could read the writing, I began to study them carefully. It was your journal of the four months before my creation. You described in these papers every step you took in the progress of your work, and this account was mixed with records of daily events. You surely remember these papers. Here they are. Everything relating to my cursed origin is written in them; the whole series of disgusting circumstances that produced it is laid out; the most detailed description of my hateful and repulsive body is given, in language that expressed your own horrors and made mine permanent. I felt sick as I read. "Hateful day when I received life!" I cried out in agony. "Cursed creator! Why did you make a monster so hideous that even you turned from me in disgust? God, in pity, made man beautiful and attractive, in his own image; but my form is a filthy copy of yours, more horrid even because of the resemblance. Satan had his companions, fellow devils, to admire and encourage him, but I am alone and hated." [8] These were the thoughts of my hours of despair and loneliness; but when I considered the virtues of the cottagers, their kind and generous natures, I convinced myself that when they came to know my admiration of their virtues, they would pity me and overlook my physical deformity. Could they turn away from their door someone, however monstrous, who asked for their compassion and friendship? I resolved, at least, not to give up hope, but to prepare myself in every way for a meeting with them that would decide my fate. I postponed this attempt for several more months, because the importance of its success filled me with fear of failure. Besides, I found that my understanding improved so much with every day's experience that I was unwilling to begin this undertaking until a few more months had added to my wisdom. [9] Several changes, in the meantime, took place in the cottage. The presence of Safie spread happiness among its inhabitants, and I also found that a greater degree of plenty existed there. Felix and Agatha spent more time in amusement and conversation, and were helped in their work by servants. They did not appear rich, but they were contented and happy; their feelings were calm and peaceful, while mine became every day more disturbed. Increased knowledge only showed me more clearly what a wretched outcast I was. I held on to hope, it is true, but it vanished when I saw my body reflected in water or my shadow in the moonlight, as frail as that image and as shifting as that shade.

“These were the reflections of my hours of despondency and solitude; but when I contemplated the virtues of the cottagers, their amiable and benevolent dispositions, I persuaded myself that when they should become acquainted with my admiration of their virtues they would compassionate me and overlook my personal deformity. Could they turn from their door one, however monstrous, who solicited their compassion and friendship? I resolved, at least, not to despair, but in every way to fit myself for an interview with them which would decide my fate. I postponed this attempt for some months longer, for the importance attached to its success inspired me with a dread lest I should fail. Besides, I found that my understanding improved so much with every day’s experience that I was unwilling to commence this undertaking until a few more months should have added to my sagacity.

“Several changes, in the meantime, took place in the cottage. The presence of Safie diffused happiness among its inhabitants, and I also found that a greater degree of plenty reigned there. Felix and Agatha spent more time in amusement and conversation, and were assisted in their labours by servants. They did not appear rich, but they were contented and happy; their feelings were serene and peaceful, while mine became every day more tumultuous. Increase of knowledge only discovered to me more clearly what a wretched outcast I was. I cherished hope, it is true, but it vanished when I beheld my person reflected in water or my shadow in the moonshine, even as that frail image and that inconstant shade.

I tried to crush these fears and to strengthen myself for the trial I had resolved to undergo in a few months. Sometimes I let my thoughts wander unchecked into the fields of Paradise, and I dared to imagine lovely, kind creatures sympathizing with my feelings and cheering my gloom. Their angelic faces breathed smiles of consolation. But it was all a dream; no Eve soothed my sorrows or shared my thoughts. I was alone. I remembered Adam’s prayer to his Creator. But where was mine? He had abandoned me, and in the bitterness of my heart I cursed him.

Autumn passed this way. I saw, with surprise and grief, the leaves decay and fall, and nature again take on the barren, bleak appearance it had worn when I first saw the woods and the lovely moon. Yet I did not mind the bleakness of the weather; my body was better suited to endure cold than heat. My chief delights were the sight of the flowers, the birds, and all the bright beauty of summer. When those left me, I turned with more attention to the cottagers. Their happiness was not lessened by the absence of summer. They loved and sympathized with one another, and their joys, depending on each other, were not interrupted by the chance events that took place around them. The more I saw of them, the greater became my desire to claim their protection and kindness. My heart yearned to be known and loved by these gentle people; to see their sweet looks directed toward me with affection was the utmost limit of my ambition. I dared not think that they would turn from me with disdain and horror. The poor who stopped at their door were never driven away. It is true that I asked for greater treasures than a little food or rest: I required kindness and sympathy. But I did not believe myself utterly unworthy of it.

Winter advanced, and a full revolution of the seasons had taken place since I awoke into life. My attention at this time was directed solely toward my plan of introducing myself into the cottage of my protectors. I turned over many ideas, but the one I finally settled on was to enter the house when the blind old man should be alone. I had sense enough to discover that the unnatural hideousness of my person was the chief object of horror for those who had seen me before. My voice, although harsh, had nothing terrible in it. I thought, therefore, that if I could gain the good will and mediation of the old De Lacey in the absence of his children, I might through him be tolerated by my younger protectors.

One day, when the sun shone on the red leaves that strewed the ground and spread cheerfulness, although it gave no warmth, Safie, Agatha, and Felix left on a long country walk, and the old man, at his own wish, was left alone in the cottage. When his children had gone, he took up his guitar and played several mournful but sweet tunes, more sweet and mournful than I had ever heard him play before. At first his face was lit with pleasure, but as he continued, thoughtfulness and sadness took its place. At length, laying aside the instrument, he sat absorbed in reflection.

My heart beat fast. This was the hour and moment of trial that would decide my hopes or confirm my fears. The servants had gone to a nearby fair. All was silent in and around the cottage. It was an excellent opportunity. Yet when I moved to carry out my plan, my limbs failed me and I sank to the ground. I rose again, and summoning all the firmness I could command, I removed the planks I had placed before my hovel to hide my retreat. The fresh air revived me, and with renewed determination I approached the door of their cottage.

I knocked. “Who is there?” said the old man. “Come in.”

I entered. “Pardon this intrusion,” I said. “I am a traveller in need of a little rest. You would greatly oblige me if you would allow me to remain a few minutes before the fire.”

“Enter,” said De Lacey, “and I will try in whatever way I can to relieve your needs. But unfortunately, my children are away from home, and as I am blind, I am afraid I shall find it difficult to get food for you.”

“Do not trouble yourself, my kind host. I have food. It is warmth and rest only that I need.”

I sat down, and a silence followed. I knew that every minute was precious to me, yet I remained uncertain how to begin the interview. Then the old man spoke to me.

“By your language, stranger, I suppose you are my countryman. Are you French?”

“No. But I was educated by a French family and understand only that language. I am now going to seek the protection of some friends whom I sincerely love, and of whose favour I have some hopes.”

“Are they Germans?”

“No, they are French. But let us change the subject. I am an unfortunate and deserted creature. I look around and I have no relation or friend on earth. These kind people to whom I am going have never seen me and know little of me. I am full of fears, for if I fail there, I am an outcast in the world forever.”

“Do not despair. To be friendless is indeed to be unfortunate, but the hearts of men, when not clouded by obvious self-interest, are full of brotherly love and charity. Rely, therefore, on your hopes. And if these friends are good and kind, do not despair.”

“They are kind—they are the most excellent creatures in the world. But unfortunately, they are prejudiced against me. I have good intentions. My life has so far been harmless and in some degree beneficial. But a fatal prejudice clouds their eyes, and where they ought to see a feeling and kind friend, they see only a detestable monster.”

“That is indeed unfortunate. But if you are truly blameless, cannot you undeceive them?”

“I am about to undertake that task. And it is on that account that I feel so many overwhelming terrors. I tenderly love these friends. I have, unknown to them, been in the habit of showing them daily kindness for many months. But they believe that I wish to injure them, and it is that prejudice which I wish to overcome.”

“Where do these friends live?”

“Near this spot.”

The old man paused and then continued. “If you will freely confide to me the details of your story, I may perhaps be of use in undeceiving them. I am blind and cannot judge your face, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere. I am poor and an exile, but it will give me true pleasure to be in any way useful to a fellow human being.”

“Excellent man! I thank you and accept your generous offer. You raise me from the dust by this kindness; and I trust that, by your aid, I shall not be driven from the society and sympathy of your fellow creatures.”

“Heaven forbid! Even if you were really criminal, that can only drive you to desperation, and not lead you to virtue. I am also unfortunate; I and my family have been condemned, although innocent. Judge, therefore, if I do not feel for your misfortunes.”

“How can I thank you, my best and only benefactor? From your lips I first heard the voice of kindness directed towards me. I shall be forever grateful, and your present humanity assures me of success with those friends whom I am on the point of meeting.”

“May I know the names and residence of those friends?”

I paused. This, I thought, was the moment of decision, which was to rob me of happiness or bestow it on me forever. I struggled vainly for firmness sufficient to answer him, but the effort destroyed all my remaining strength. I sank onto the chair and sobbed aloud. At that moment I heard the steps of my younger protectors. I had not a moment to lose, but seizing the hand of the old man, I cried, “Now is the time! Save and protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not desert me in the hour of trial!”

“Great God!” exclaimed the old man. “Who are you?”

At that instant the cottage door opened, and Felix, Safie, and Agatha entered. Who can describe their horror and consternation on seeing me? Agatha fainted, and Safie, unable to attend to her friend, rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force tore me from his father, to whose knees I clung. In a transport of fury, he dashed me to the ground and struck me violently with a stick. I could have torn him limb from limb, as the lion rends the antelope. But my heart sank within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained. My travels were long and the sufferings I endured intense. It was late in autumn when I left the district where I had so long resided. I travelled only at night, fearful of encountering the face of a human being. Nature decayed around me, and the sun became heatless. Rain and snow poured around me. Mighty rivers were frozen. No incident occurred from which my rage and misery could not extract its food. But a circumstance that happened when I arrived on the borders of Switzerland, when the sun had recovered its warmth and the earth again began to look green, confirmed in an especial manner the bitterness and horror of my feelings.

I generally rested during the day and travelled only when I was protected by night from being seen by people. One morning, however, finding that my path lay through a deep wood, I ventured to continue my journey after the sun had risen. The day, which was one of the first of spring, cheered even me with its lovely sunshine and the mildness of the air. I felt emotions of gentleness and pleasure, which had long seemed dead, revive within me. Half surprised by the novelty of these sensations, I allowed myself to be carried away by them, and forgetting my solitude and deformity, I dared to be happy. Soft tears again wet my cheeks, and I even raised my moist eyes with thankfulness towards the blessed sun, which gave me such joy.

I continued to wind among the paths of the wood until I came to its edge, which was bordered by a deep and rapid river. Many of the trees bent their branches, now budding with fresh spring, into the water. Here I paused, not exactly knowing which path to take, when I heard the sound of voices that made me hide myself under the shade of a cypress. I was scarcely hidden when a young girl came running towards the spot where I was concealed, laughing as if she were running from someone in play. She continued along the steep sides of the river, when suddenly her foot slipped and she fell into the rapid stream. I rushed from my hiding place and, with extreme effort against the force of the current, saved her and dragged her to shore. She was unconscious, and I was trying by every means in my power to revive her when I was suddenly interrupted by the approach of a countryman, who was probably the person from whom she had playfully fled. On seeing me, he darted towards me and, tearing the girl from my arms, hurried towards the deeper parts of the wood. I followed quickly, hardly knowing why. But when the man saw me draw near, he aimed a gun he was carrying at my body and fired. I sank to the ground, and my attacker escaped into the wood with increased speed.

This, then, was the reward for my kindness! I had saved a human being from destruction, and as payment I now writhed under the miserable pain of a wound that had shattered flesh and bone. The feelings of kindness and gentleness I had felt only a few moments before gave way to hellish rage and gnashing of teeth. Inflamed by pain, I vowed eternal hatred and vengeance against all mankind. But the agony of my wound overcame me; my pulse stopped, and I fainted.

For some weeks I led a miserable life in the woods, trying to cure the wound I had received. The bullet had entered my shoulder, and I did not know whether it had remained there or passed through; in any case I had no way of removing it. My sufferings were increased also by the oppressive sense of the injustice and ingratitude of their infliction. My daily vows rose for revenge—a deep and deadly revenge, such as would alone compensate for the outrages and anguish I had endured.

After some weeks my wound healed, and I continued my journey. The labours I endured were no longer to be eased by the bright sun or gentle breezes of spring. All joy was but a mockery that insulted my desolate state and made me feel more painfully that I was not made for the enjoyment of pleasure.

But my struggles now drew near a close, and in two months from this time I reached the outskirts of Geneva.

It was evening when I arrived, and I hid myself among the fields that surround the house to think about how I should approach you. I was worn out with tiredness and hunger, and far too unhappy to enjoy the gentle evening breeze or the sight of the sun setting behind the towering Jura mountains.

At that moment, a light sleep eased my troubled thoughts, but I was woken by the approach of a beautiful child, who came running into the spot I had chosen with all the playfulness of a young child. Suddenly, as I looked at him, an idea came to me: this little creature was without prejudice and had not lived long enough to have learned a horror of deformity. If I could seize him and raise him as my companion and friend, I would not be so alone in this crowded world.

Driven by this impulse, I grabbed the boy as he passed and pulled him toward me. As soon as he saw my appearance, he put his hands over his eyes and let out a piercing scream. I pulled his hand forcibly away from his face and said, "Child, what is the meaning of this? I do not intend to hurt you; listen to me."

He struggled violently. "Let me go," he cried;

"monster! Ugly wretch! You wish to eat me and tear me to pieces. You are an ogre. Let me go, or I will tell my papa."

"Boy, you will never see your father again; you must come with me."

"Hideous monster! Let me go. My papa is a syndic—he is M. Frankenstein—he will punish you. You dare not keep me."

"Frankenstein! Then you belong to my enemy—to him against whom I have sworn eternal revenge; you shall be my first victim."

The child still struggled and called me names that filled my heart with despair. I grasped his throat to silence him, and in a moment he lay dead at my feet.

I looked at my victim, and my heart swelled with exultation and hellish triumph. Clapping my hands, I exclaimed, "I too can create desolation; my enemy is not invulnerable; this death will bring despair to him, and a thousand other miseries shall torment and destroy him."

As I fixed my eyes on the child, I saw something glittering on his chest. I took it; it was a portrait of a most lovely woman. In spite of my wickedness, it softened and attracted me. For a few moments I gazed with delight on her dark eyes, fringed by deep lashes, and her lovely lips; but soon my rage returned. I remembered that I was forever deprived of the delights that such beautiful creatures could give, and that she whose likeness I was looking at would, upon seeing me, have changed that expression of divine kindness to one of disgust and terror.

Can you wonder that such thoughts carried me away with rage? I only wonder that at that moment, instead of expressing my feelings in cries and agony, I did not rush out among mankind and die in the attempt to destroy them.

While I was overcome by these feelings, I left the spot where I had committed the murder, and looking for a more secluded hiding place, I entered a barn that had seemed to me to be empty. A woman was sleeping on some straw; she was young, not indeed as beautiful as the woman whose portrait I held, but pleasant-looking and glowing with the loveliness of youth and health. Here, I thought, is one of those whose joy-giving smiles are given to everyone but me. And then I bent over her and whispered, "Awake, fairest one, your lover is near—he who would give his life just to receive one look of affection from your eyes; my beloved, awake!"

The sleeper stirred, and a thrill of terror ran through me. Should she indeed awake, and see me, and curse me, and denounce the murderer? Thus would she assuredly act if her darkened eyes opened and she beheld me. The thought was madness; it stirred the fiend within me—not I, but she, shall suffer. The murder I have committed because I am forever robbed of all that she could give me, she shall atone. The crime had its source in her; be hers the punishment! Thanks to the lessons of Felix and the bloody laws of man, I had learned now to work mischief. I bent over her and placed the portrait securely in one of the folds of her dress. She moved again, and I fled.

For some days I haunted the spot where these scenes had taken place, sometimes wishing to see you, sometimes resolved to quit the world and its miseries forever. At length I wandered towards these mountains, and have ranged through their immense recesses, consumed by a burning passion which you alone can gratify. We may not part until you have promised to comply with my demand. I am alone and miserable; man will not associate with me. But one as deformed and horrible as myself would not deny herself to me. My companion must be of the same species and have the same defects. This being you must create.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 17

The being finished speaking and fixed his eyes on me, waiting for a reply. But I was bewildered and confused, unable to organize my thoughts enough to understand the full extent of his proposal. He continued,

“You must create a female for me, someone I can live with and share the sympathy I need to exist. Only you can do this, and I demand it as a right you must not refuse to grant.”

The latter part of his story had rekindled the anger in me that had faded while he described his peaceful life among the cottagers. As he said this, I could no longer hold back the rage burning inside me.

“I do refuse it,” I replied. “No torture will ever force my consent. You may make me the most miserable of men, but you will never make me despicable in my own eyes. Should I create another like you, whose combined wickedness might destroy the world? Go away! I have answered you. You may torture me, but I will never consent.”

“You are wrong,” replied the fiend. “Instead of threatening you, I am willing to reason with you. I am malicious because I am miserable. Am I not shunned and hated by all mankind? You, my creator, would tear me to pieces and celebrate. Remember that, and tell me why I should pity man more than he pities me. You would not call it murder if you could throw me into one of those ice crevasses and destroy my body, the work of your own hands. Should I respect man when he condemns me? Let him live with me in mutual kindness, and instead of harm I would give him every benefit with tears of gratitude for his acceptance. But that cannot happen; the human senses are impossible barriers to our union. Yet I will not submit like a wretched slave. I will avenge my injuries. If I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear—and chiefly toward you, my arch-enemy, because you are my creator, I swear undying hatred. Be careful: I will work toward your destruction, and I will not stop until I have devastated your heart, so that you curse the hour you were born.”

A fiendish rage animated him as he said this. His face twisted into contortions too horrible for human eyes to see. But presently he calmed himself and continued—

“I meant to reason with you. This passion harms me, because you do not realize that you are the cause of its excess. If any being felt kindness toward me, I would return it a hundred times and a hundred times more. For that one creature’s sake, I would make peace with all of humankind! But I am now indulging in dreams of happiness that cannot come true. What I ask of you is reasonable and modest. I demand a creature of another sex, but as hideous as myself. The satisfaction is small, but it is all I can receive, and it will content me. It is true that we will be monsters, cut off from all the world. But for that very reason, we will be more attached to one another. Our lives will not be happy, but they will be harmless and free from the misery I now feel. Oh, my creator, make me happy! Let me feel gratitude toward you for one benefit! Let me see that I can stir the sympathy of some living thing. Do not deny me my request!”

I was moved. I shuddered when I thought of the possible consequences of my consent, but I felt that there was some justice in his argument. His tale and the feelings he now expressed proved him to be a creature of fine sensations, and did I not, as his maker, owe him all the portion of happiness that it was in my power to bestow? He saw my change of feeling and continued,

“If you consent, neither you nor any other human being shall ever see us again; I will go to the vast wilds of South America. My food is not that of man; I do not destroy the lamb and the kid to glut my appetite; acorns and berries afford me sufficient nourishment. My companion will be of the same nature as myself and will be content with the same fare. We shall make our bed of dried leaves; the sun will shine on us as on man and will ripen our food. The picture I present to you is peaceful and human, and you must feel that you could deny it only in the wantonness of power and cruelty. Pitiless as you have been towards me, I now see compassion in your eyes; let me seize the favourable moment and persuade you to promise what I so ardently desire.”

“You propose,” replied I, “to fly from the habitations of man, to dwell in those wilds where the beasts of the field will be your only companions. How can you, who long for the love and sympathy of man, persevere in this exile? You will return and again seek their kindness, and you will meet with their detestation; your evil passions will be renewed, and you will then have a companion to aid you in the task of destruction. This may not be; cease to argue the point, for I cannot consent.”

“How inconstant are your feelings! But a moment ago you were moved by my representations, and why do you again harden yourself to my complaints? I swear to you, by the earth which I inhabit, and by you that made me, that with the companion you bestow, I will quit the neighbourhood of man and dwell, as it may chance, in the most savage of places. My evil passions will have fled, for I shall meet with sympathy! My life will flow quietly away, and in my dying moments I shall not curse my maker.”

His words had a strange effect upon me. I compassionated him and sometimes felt a wish to console him, but when I looked upon him, when I saw the filthy mass that moved and talked, my heart sickened and my feelings were altered to those of horror and hatred. I tried to stifle these sensations; I thought that as I could not sympathise with him, I had no right to withhold from him the small portion of happiness which was yet in my power to bestow.

“You swear,” I said, “to be harmless; but have you not already shown a degree of malice that should reasonably make me distrust you? May not even this be a feint that will increase your triumph by affording a wider scope for your revenge?”

“How is this? I must not be trifled with, and I demand an answer. If I have no ties and no affections, hatred and vice must be my portion; the love of another will destroy the cause of my crimes, and I shall become a thing of whose existence everyone will be ignorant. My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I abhor, and my virtues will necessarily arise when I live in communion with an equal. I shall feel the affections of a sensitive being and become linked to the chain of existence and events from which I am now excluded.”

I paused for some time to reflect on all he had told me and the various arguments he had used. I thought of the promise of good qualities he had shown at the beginning of his life, and the later destruction of all kindly feeling by the disgust and scorn his protectors had shown toward him. His power and threats were not left out of my thinking. A creature who could live in the ice-caves of the glaciers and hide from pursuit among the ridges of inaccessible cliffs was a being with abilities it would be useless to fight against. After a long pause of reflection, I concluded that the justice owed both to him and to my fellow creatures required me to agree to his request. Turning to him, therefore, I said,

“I agree to your demand, on your solemn oath to leave Europe forever, and every other place near human beings, as soon as I place in your hands a female who will accompany you in your exile.”

“I swear,” he cried, “by the sun, and by the blue sky of heaven, and by the fire of love that burns my heart, that if you grant my prayer, as long as they exist you shall never see me again. Go to your home and begin your work. I shall watch its progress with unutterable anxiety; and do not fear that when you are ready I will not appear.”

Saying this, he suddenly left me, afraid, perhaps, that I might change my mind. I saw him descend the mountain with greater speed than the flight of an eagle, and he was quickly lost among the waves of the sea of ice.

His story had taken up the whole day, and the sun was at the edge of the horizon when he left. I knew I ought to hurry down toward the valley, as I would soon be surrounded by darkness; but my heart was heavy, and my steps slow. The work of winding among the little paths of the mountain and fixing my feet firmly as I moved forward confused me, occupied as I was by the emotions that the events of the day had produced. Night was far advanced when I came to the halfway resting-place and seated myself beside the fountain. The stars shone at intervals as the clouds passed over them; the dark pines rose before me, and here and there a broken tree lay on the ground. It was a scene of wonderful solemnity and stirred strange thoughts within me. I wept bitterly, and clasping my hands in agony, I exclaimed, “Oh! stars and clouds and winds, you are all about to mock me; if you really pity me, crush sensation and memory; let me become as nothing; but if not, depart, depart, and leave me in darkness.”

These were wild and miserable thoughts, but I cannot describe to you how the eternal twinkling of the stars weighed upon me, and how I listened to every blast of wind as if it were a dull ugly sirocco on its way to consume me.

Morning came before I reached the village of Chamounix; I did not rest, but went straight back to Geneva. Even in my own heart I could not put my feelings into words—they pressed on me with the weight of a mountain, and their excess crushed my agony beneath them. So I returned home, and entering the house, presented myself to the family. My haggard and wild appearance caused intense alarm, but I answered no question and barely spoke. I felt as if I were under a curse—as if I had no right to claim their sympathy—as if I could never again enjoy their company. Yet even so, I loved them to the point of adoration; and to save them, I resolved to devote myself to my most hated task. The prospect of such work made every other circumstance of life pass before me like a dream, and that thought alone had the reality of life for me.

###CHAPTER### Chapter 18

Day after day, week after week, passed after my return to Geneva, and I could not gather the courage to begin my work again. I feared the revenge of the disappointed creature, yet I could not overcome my disgust for the task I had been ordered to do. I found that I could not create a female without again spending several months in deep study and difficult research. I had heard of some discoveries made by an English philosopher, knowledge of which was essential to my success, and I sometimes thought of getting my father’s consent to visit England for this purpose. But I clung to every excuse for delay and shrank from taking the first step in a project whose immediate necessity began to seem less absolute to me. A change had indeed taken place in me. My health, which had been declining, was now much improved, and my spirits, when not weighed down by the memory of my unhappy promise, rose accordingly. My father saw this change with pleasure, and he turned his thoughts toward the best way of removing the remains of my melancholy, which would still return from time to time, casting a devouring darkness over the approaching sunshine. At these moments I took refuge in complete solitude. I spent whole days alone on the lake in a little boat, watching the clouds and listening to the rippling of the waves, silent and listless. But the fresh air and bright sun seldom failed to restore me to some degree of calm, and on my return I met my friends’ greetings with a readier smile and a more cheerful heart.

It was after my return from one of these outings that my father, calling me aside, spoke to me as follows:

“I am happy to see, my dear son, that you have taken up your former pleasures again and seem to be returning to yourself. And yet you are still unhappy and still avoid our company. For some time I was lost in guessing at the cause of this, but yesterday an idea struck me, and if it is well founded, I beg you to admit it. Silence on such a point would not only be useless, but would bring triple misery on us all.”

I trembled violently at his opening words, and my father continued—

“I confess, my son, that I have always looked forward to your marriage with our dear Elizabeth as the bond of our domestic comfort and the support of my declining years. You were attached to each other from your earliest childhood. You studied together and seemed, in character and tastes, entirely suited to one another. But human judgment is so blind that what I imagined to be the best supports for my plan may have entirely destroyed it. You perhaps regard her as your sister, without any wish that she might become your wife. Or you may have met another woman whom you love, and considering yourself bound in honor to Elizabeth, this struggle may be causing the sharp misery you appear to feel.”

“My dear father, set your mind at rest. I love my cousin tenderly and sincerely. I have never seen any woman who inspired, as Elizabeth does, my warmest admiration and affection. My future hopes and prospects are entirely tied to the expectation of our union.”

“The expression of your sentiments on this subject, my dear Victor, gives me more pleasure than I have felt for some time. If you feel this way, we shall certainly be happy, however much present events may cast a gloom over us. But it is this gloom, which appears to have taken so strong a hold of your mind, that I wish to dispel. Tell me, therefore, whether you object to an immediate marriage. We have been unfortunate, and recent events have drawn us away from that everyday tranquillity suited to my years and infirmities. You are younger; yet I do not suppose, possessed as you are of a competent fortune, that an early marriage would at all interfere with any future plans of honour and usefulness that you may have formed. Do not suppose, however, that I wish to dictate happiness to you or that a delay on your part would cause me any serious uneasiness. Interpret my words with openness and answer me, I beg you, with confidence and sincerity.”

I listened to my father in silence and remained for some time incapable of offering any reply. I turned over rapidly in my mind a multitude of thoughts and tried to reach some conclusion. Alas! To me the idea of an immediate union with my Elizabeth was one of horror and dismay. I was bound by a solemn promise which I had not yet fulfilled and dared not break. Or if I did, what countless miseries might not hang over me and my devoted family! Could I enter into a celebration with this deadly weight still hanging round my neck and bowing me to the ground? I must perform my engagement and let the monster depart with his mate before I allowed myself to enjoy the delight of a union from which I expected peace.

I remembered also the necessity imposed upon me of either journeying to England or entering into a long correspondence with those philosophers of that country whose knowledge and discoveries were of indispensable use to me in my present undertaking. The latter method of obtaining the desired information was slow and unsatisfactory. Besides, I had an insurmountable aversion to the idea of engaging in my loathsome task in my father’s house while living in familiar contact with those I loved. I knew that a thousand fearful accidents might occur, the slightest of which would reveal a tale to thrill all connected with me with horror. I was aware also that I should often lose all self-command, all capacity of hiding the harrowing sensations that would possess me during the progress of my unearthly occupation. I must absent myself from all I loved while thus employed. Once begun, it would quickly be achieved, and I might be restored to my family in peace and happiness. My promise fulfilled, the monster would depart for ever. Or (so my fond fancy imagined) some accident might meanwhile occur to destroy him and put an end to my slavery for ever.

These feelings dictated my answer to my father. I expressed a wish to visit England, but concealing the true reasons of this request, I clothed my desires under a guise which excited no suspicion, while I urged my desire with an earnestness that easily induced my father to comply. After so long a period of an absorbing melancholy that resembled madness in its intensity and effects, he was glad to find that I was capable of taking pleasure in the idea of such a journey, and he hoped that change of scene and varied amusement would, before my return, have restored me entirely to myself.

I was free to decide how long I would be away; the plan was for a few months, or at most a year. He had taken one kind, fatherly precaution to make sure I had a companion. Without telling me beforehand, he and Elizabeth had arranged for Clerval to meet me at Strasburgh. This interfered with the solitude I wanted for my work; yet at the start of my journey my friend's presence could not be an obstacle, and I was truly glad to be saved many hours of lonely, maddening thought. Indeed, Henry might stand between me and my enemy's intrusion. If I were alone, would the creature not sometimes force his hated presence on me to remind me of my task or to watch its progress?

To England, therefore, I was bound, and it was understood that my marriage to Elizabeth would take place immediately on my return. My father's age made him extremely unwilling to delay. For myself, there was one reward I promised myself from my hated labors—one comfort for my unparalleled suffering; it was the hope of that day when, freed from my miserable slavery, I might claim Elizabeth and forget the past in my union with her.

I now made arrangements for my journey, but one feeling haunted me and filled me with fear and agitation. During my absence I would leave my friends unaware of their enemy's existence and unprotected from his attacks, enraged as he might be by my departure. But he had promised to follow me wherever I went, and would he not accompany me to England? This thought was dreadful in itself, but comforting because it assumed my friends would be safe. I was tormented by the idea that the opposite might happen. But through the whole period during which I was the slave of my creature, I allowed myself to be governed by the impulses of the moment; and my present feelings strongly suggested that the fiend would follow me and spare my family from the danger of his schemes.

It was in the latter part of September that I again left my native country. The journey had been my own idea, and Elizabeth therefore agreed, but she was filled with unease at the thought of my suffering misery and grief away from her. It had been her care that provided me with Clerval as a companion—and yet a man is blind to a thousand small details that call forth a woman's devoted attention. She longed to urge me to return quickly; a thousand conflicting emotions made her silent as she gave me a tearful, wordless farewell.

I threw myself into the carriage that was to carry me away, hardly knowing where I was going, and careless of what was passing around me. I remembered only, and it was with bitter anguish that I thought of it, to order that my chemical instruments should be packed to go with me. Filled with gloomy thoughts, I passed through many beautiful and majestic scenes, but my eyes were fixed and unseeing. I could think only of the destination of my travels and the work that was to occupy me while they lasted.

After some days spent in lazy idleness, during which I traveled many miles, I arrived at Strasburgh, where I waited two days for Clerval. He came. Alas, how great was the contrast between us! He was alive to every new scene, joyful when he saw the beauties of the setting sun, and even happier when he watched it rise and begin a new day. He pointed out to me the shifting colors of the landscape and the changing appearances of the sky. “This is what it is to live,” he cried; “now I enjoy existence! But you, my dear Frankenstein, why are you so downcast and sorrowful?” In truth, I was occupied by gloomy thoughts and saw neither the descent of the evening star nor the golden sunrise reflected in the Rhine. And you, my friend, would be far more entertained by the journal of Clerval, who observed the scenery with a feeling and delighted eye, than by listening to my reflections. I was a miserable wretch, haunted by a curse that shut up every path to enjoyment.

We had agreed to travel down the Rhine in a boat from Strasburgh to Rotterdam, where we could take a ship for London. During this voyage we passed many willowy islands and saw several beautiful towns. We stayed a day at Mannheim, and on the fifth day after leaving Strasburgh, arrived at Mainz. The course of the Rhine below Mainz becomes much more picturesque. The river flows rapidly and winds between hills that are not high, but steep and beautifully shaped. We saw many ruined castles standing on the edges of cliffs, surrounded by dark woods, high and inaccessible. This part of the Rhine, indeed, presents a remarkably varied landscape. In one spot you see rugged hills, ruined castles overlooking tremendous cliffs, with the dark Rhine rushing beneath; and at the sudden turn of a headland, flourishing vineyards with green sloping banks, a winding river, and populous towns fill the scene.

We travelled at the time of the grape harvest and heard the song of the workers as we glided down the stream. Even I, depressed in mind and with my spirits continually agitated by gloomy feelings, even I was pleased. I lay at the bottom of the boat, and as I gazed on the cloudless blue sky, I seemed to drink in a tranquillity to which I had long been a stranger. And if these were my feelings, who can describe those of Henry? He felt as if he had been transported to Fairy-land and enjoyed a happiness seldom tasted by man. “I have seen,” he said, “the most beautiful scenes of my own country. I have visited the lakes of Lucerne and Uri, where the snowy mountains descend almost straight down to the water, casting black and impenetrable shadows, which would cause a gloomy and mournful appearance were it not for the most verdant islands that relieve the eye by their bright appearance. I have seen this lake agitated by a storm, when the wind tore up whirlwinds of water and gave you an idea of what the waterspout must be on the great ocean; and the waves dash with fury at the base of the mountain, where the priest and his mistress were overwhelmed by an avalanche and where their dying voices are still said to be heard amid the pauses of the nightly wind. I have seen the mountains of La Valais, and the Pays de Vaud; but this country, Victor, pleases me more than all those wonders. The mountains of Switzerland are more majestic and strange, but there is a charm in the banks of this divine river that I never before saw equalled. Look at that castle which overhangs that precipice; and that one also on the island, almost concealed amongst the foliage of those lovely trees; and now that group of workers coming from among their vines; and that village half hidden in the recess of the mountain. Oh, surely the spirit that inhabits and guards this place has a soul more in harmony with man than those who pile the glacier or retire to the inaccessible peaks of the mountains of our own country.”

Clerval! Beloved friend! Even now it delights me to record your words and to dwell on the praise of which you are so eminently deserving. He was a being formed in the “very poetry of nature.” His wild and enthusiastic imagination was tempered by the sensitivity of his heart. His soul overflowed with ardent affections, and his friendship was of that devoted and wondrous nature that the worldly-minded teach us to look for only in the imagination. But even human sympathies were not sufficient to satisfy his eager mind. The scenery of external nature, which others regard only with admiration, he loved with passion:—

——The sounding waterfall

Haunted him like a passion: the tall rock,

The mountain, and the deep and gloomy wood,

Their colours and their forms, were then to him an appetite; a feeling, and a love,

That had no need of a remoter charm,

By thought supplied, or any interest

Unborrowed from the eye.

[Wordsworth’s “Tintern Abbey”.]

And where does he now exist? Is this gentle and lovely being lost forever? Has this mind, so full of ideas, fanciful and magnificent imaginations, which formed a world whose existence depended on the life of its creator—has this mind perished? Does it now only exist in my memory? No, it is not so; your form so divinely made, and beaming with beauty, has decayed, but your spirit still visits and consoles your unhappy friend.

Forgive this flood of sorrow; these useless words are only a small tribute to Henry’s unmatched goodness, but they comfort my heart, which is overflowing with the pain his memory brings. I will go on with my story.

Beyond Cologne we came down to the plains of Holland, and we decided to travel the rest of the way by coach, since the wind was against us and the river’s current was too slow to help us.

Our journey here lost the interest that came from beautiful scenery, but in a few days we arrived at Rotterdam, and from there we went by sea to England. It was on a clear morning in the last days of December that I first saw the white cliffs of Britain. The banks of the Thames offered a new scene; they were flat but fertile, and almost every town was connected to the memory of some story. We saw Tilbury Fort and remembered the Spanish Armada, and then Gravesend, Woolwich, and Greenwich—places I had heard of even in my own country.

At last we saw the many steeples of London, with St. Paul’s towering above them all, and the Tower, famous in English history.

London was our present stopping place, and we decided to stay for several months in this wonderful and celebrated city. Clerval wanted to meet the men of genius and talent who were active at this time, but for me this was a secondary concern. I was mainly occupied with getting the information I needed to keep my promise, and I quickly made use of the letters of introduction I had brought with me, addressed to the most distinguished natural philosophers.

If this journey had taken place during my days of study and happiness, it would have given me inexpressible pleasure. Company was tiresome to me; when alone, I could fill my mind with the sights of heaven and earth. Henry's voice soothed me, and I could thus deceive myself into a temporary peace. But busy, uninteresting, cheerful faces brought despair back to my heart. I saw an insurmountable barrier placed between me and my fellow men; this barrier was sealed with the blood of William and Justine, and to think about the events connected with those names filled my soul with anguish.

But in Clerval I saw the image of my former self; he was curious and eager to gain experience and knowledge. The difference of customs he observed was to him an endless source of instruction and amusement. He was also pursuing a goal he had long had in mind. His plan was to visit India, believing that his knowledge of its various languages, and his views on its society, would allow him to materially assist the progress of European colonization and trade. Only in Britain could he further the execution of his plan. He was forever busy, and the only check on his enjoyment was my sorrowful and dejected state of mind. I tried to hide this as much as possible, so that I might not deprive him of the pleasures natural to someone entering a new stage of life, undisturbed by any care or bitter memory. I often refused to go with him, claiming another appointment, so that I might remain alone. I now also began to collect the materials necessary for my new creation, and this was to me like the torture of single drops of water continually falling on the head. Every thought devoted to it was an extreme anguish, and every word I spoke in reference to it caused my lips to quiver and my heart to pound.

After spending some months in London, we received a letter from a person in Scotland who had formerly been our visitor in Geneva. He mentioned the beauties of his native country and asked us if those were not enough to tempt us to extend our journey as far north as Perth, where he lived. Clerval eagerly wanted to accept this invitation, and I, although I hated society, wished to see again mountains and streams and all the wondrous works with which Nature adorns her chosen dwelling-places.

We had arrived in England at the beginning of October, and it was now February. We therefore decided to begin our journey north after another month. On this trip we did not plan to take the main road to Edinburgh, but to visit Windsor, Oxford, Matlock, and the Cumberland lakes, aiming to finish the tour around the end of July. I packed up my chemical instruments and the materials I had collected, determined to complete my work in some quiet corner of the northern highlands of Scotland.

We left London on the 27th of March and stayed a few days at Windsor, wandering in its beautiful forest. This was a new scene for us mountaineers; the majestic oaks, the abundance of game, and the herds of stately deer were all novelties to us.

From there we went on to Oxford. As we entered the city, our minds were filled with memories of the events that had taken place there more than a century and a half before. It was here that Charles I had gathered his forces. The city had remained loyal to him after the whole nation had abandoned his cause to join the side of Parliament and liberty. The memory of that unfortunate king and his companions—the gracious Falkland, the arrogant Goring, his queen, and son—gave a special interest to every part of the city they might have lived in. The spirit of earlier days found a home here, and we delighted in tracing its footsteps. Even if these feelings had not found an imagined satisfaction, the appearance of the city itself had enough beauty to earn our admiration. The colleges are ancient and picturesque; the streets are almost magnificent; and the lovely Isis, which flows beside it through meadows of exquisite greenness, spreads out into a calm expanse of water that reflects its majestic collection of towers, spires, and domes, nestled among ancient trees.

I enjoyed this scene, yet my enjoyment was soured both by the memory of the past and the anticipation of the future. I was made for peaceful happiness. During my younger days discontent never entered my mind, and if I was ever overcome by boredom, the sight of what is beautiful in nature or the study of what is excellent and sublime in human creations could always capture my heart and restore energy to my spirits. But I am a blasted tree; the lightning has struck my soul; and I felt then that I would survive only to show what I will soon cease to be—a miserable spectacle of wrecked humanity, pitiable to others and unbearable to myself.

We spent a considerable time at Oxford, wandering through its surroundings and trying to identify every spot that might relate to the most stirring period of English history. Our little voyages of discovery were often extended by the successive objects that appeared before us. We visited the tomb of the illustrious Hampden and the field where that patriot fell. For a moment my soul was lifted from its degrading and miserable fears to contemplate the divine ideas of liberty and self-sacrifice of which these sights were the monuments and reminders. For an instant I dared to shake off my chains and look around me with a free and noble spirit, but the iron had eaten into my flesh, and I sank again, trembling and hopeless, into my miserable self.

We left Oxford with regret and went on to Matlock, which was our next place of rest. The countryside around this village resembled the scenery of Switzerland, but on a smaller scale. The green hills lacked the distant white Alps that always crown the pine-covered mountains of my native country. We visited the remarkable cave and the small natural history collections, where the curiosities are arranged in the same way as at Servox and Chamounix. The latter name made me tremble when Henry said it, and I hurried to leave Matlock, since that terrible scene was now connected with it.

From Derby, still traveling north, we spent two months in Cumberland and Westmorland. I could now almost imagine myself among the Swiss mountains. The small patches of snow still remaining on the northern sides of the mountains, the lakes, and the rushing of the rocky streams were all familiar and dear sights to me. Here we also made some acquaintances who nearly managed to make me happy. Clerval's delight was much greater than mine. His mind grew in the company of talented people, and he discovered greater abilities and resources in himself than he had imagined while spending time with those less gifted. "I could spend my life here," he said to me, "and among these mountains I would hardly miss Switzerland and the Rhine."

But he found that a traveler's life includes much pain along with its pleasures. His feelings are always on edge, and when he begins to settle into rest, he finds he must leave what he enjoys for something new, which again captures his attention, and which he also abandons for other novelties.

We had barely visited the various lakes of Cumberland and Westmorland and grown fond of some of the people there when the time of our appointment with our Scottish friend approached, and we left to continue traveling. For my part I was not sorry. I had now neglected my promise for some time, and I feared the effects of the demon's disappointment. He might remain in Switzerland and take his revenge on my relatives. This idea pursued and tormented me at every moment when I might otherwise have found rest and peace. I waited for my letters with feverish impatience. If they were delayed I was miserable and overwhelmed by a thousand fears. When they arrived and I saw the handwriting of Elizabeth or my father, I hardly dared to read and learn my fate. Sometimes I thought the fiend followed me and might punish my delay by murdering my companion. When these thoughts took hold of me, I would not leave Henry for a moment, but followed him like his shadow to protect him from the imagined rage of his destroyer. I felt as if I had committed some great crime, and the awareness of it haunted me. I was innocent, but I had indeed brought a horrible curse upon my head, as deadly as that of any crime.

I visited Edinburgh with little interest or energy, and yet that city might have interested even the most unfortunate person. Clerval did not like it as well as Oxford, because he preferred the ancient character of that city. But the beauty and order of the new town of Edinburgh, its romantic castle and its surroundings—the most delightful in the world—Arthur’s Seat, St. Bernard’s Well, and the Pentland Hills, made up for the change and filled him with cheerfulness and admiration. But I was impatient to reach the end of my journey.

We left Edinburgh after a week, passing through Coupar, St. Andrew’s, and along the banks of the Tay to Perth, where our friend was expecting us. But I was in no mood to laugh and talk with strangers or to enter into their feelings and plans with the good humor expected from a guest. So I told Clerval that I wished to tour Scotland alone. “Enjoy yourself,” I said, “and let this be our meeting place. I may be away for a month or two, but please do not interfere with my movements. Leave me to peace and solitude for a short time. When I return, I hope it will be with a lighter heart, more suited to your own temperament.”

Henry tried to talk me out of it, but when he saw I was determined, he stopped objecting. He begged me to write often. “I would rather be with you,” he said, “on your solitary walks than with these Scottish people, whom I do not know. Hurry back, then, my dear friend, so that I may feel somewhat at home again, which I cannot do in your absence.”

After parting from my friend, I decided to visit some remote spot in Scotland and finish my work in solitude. I did not doubt that the monster was following me and would reveal himself to me once I had finished, so that he might receive his companion.

With this plan in mind, I crossed the northern highlands and chose one of the most remote of the Orkney Islands as the place for my work. It was suited to such a task, being little more than a rock whose high sides were constantly beaten by the waves. The soil was barren, barely providing pasture for a few miserable cows and oatmeal for its inhabitants, who numbered five persons. Their thin and bony limbs showed how poorly they were fed. Vegetables and bread, when they allowed themselves such luxuries, and even fresh water, had to be brought from the mainland, which was about five miles away.

On the whole island there were only three miserable huts, and one of these was empty when I arrived. I rented it. It had only two rooms, and these showed all the filth of the most extreme poverty. The thatch had fallen in, the walls were unplastered, and the door was off its hinges. I ordered it repaired, bought some furniture, and moved in. This would no doubt have caused some surprise if the senses of the cottagers had not been dulled by want and squalid poverty. As it was, I lived unnoticed and undisturbed, hardly thanked for the small amount of food and clothes I gave them—so much does suffering blunt even the coarsest feelings of men.

In this retreat I devoted the morning to work; but in the evening, when the weather allowed, I walked on the stony beach of the sea to listen to the waves as they roared and dashed at my feet. It was a monotonous yet ever-changing scene. I thought of Switzerland; it was far different from this desolate and appalling landscape. Its hills are covered with vines, and its cottages are scattered thickly in the plains. Its fair lakes reflect a blue and gentle sky, and when troubled by the winds, their disturbance is but like the play of a lively infant compared to the roarings of the giant ocean.

In this manner I divided my time when I first arrived, but as I proceeded in my work, it became every day more horrible and tiresome to me. Sometimes I could not bring myself to enter my laboratory for several days, and at other times I worked day and night in order to complete my task. It was, indeed, a filthy process in which I was engaged. During my first experiment, a kind of enthusiastic frenzy had blinded me to the horror of my employment; my mind was intently fixed on the completion of my work, and my eyes were shut to the horror of what I was doing. But now I went to it in cold blood, and my heart often sickened at the work of my hands.

In this situation, employed in the most detestable occupation, immersed in a solitude where nothing could for a moment call my attention from the actual scene in which I was engaged, my spirits became uneven; I grew restless and nervous. Every moment I feared to meet my persecutor. Sometimes I sat with my eyes fixed on the ground, fearing to raise them in case they should encounter the object which I so much dreaded to behold. I feared to wander from the sight of my fellow creatures in case, when alone, he should come to claim his companion.

In the meantime I worked on, and my labour was already considerably advanced. I looked towards its completion with a trembling and eager hope, which I dared not trust myself to question but which was mixed with vague forebodings of evil that made my heart sicken in my chest.

I sat one evening in my laboratory. The sun had set, and the moon was just rising from the sea. I did not have enough light for my work, so I remained idle, pausing to consider whether I should leave my labor for the night or hurry its conclusion by giving it my unbroken attention. As I sat, a train of reflection came to me which led me to consider the effects of what I was now doing. Three years before, I had been engaged in the same manner and had created a fiend whose unparalleled barbarity had desolated my heart and filled it forever with the bitterest remorse. I was now about to form another being whose dispositions I was equally ignorant of. She might become ten thousand times more malignant than her mate and delight, for its own sake, in murder and wretchedness. He had sworn to leave the neighborhood of man and hide himself in deserts, but she had not. And she, who in all probability was to become a thinking and reasoning animal, might refuse to comply with an agreement made before her creation. They might even hate each other. The creature who already lived loathed his own deformity, and might he not conceive a greater horror for it when it came before his eyes in the female form? She also might turn with disgust from him to the superior beauty of man. She might leave him, and he would be again alone, exasperated by the fresh provocation of being deserted by one of his own species.

Even if they were to leave Europe and inhabit the deserts of the new world, one of the first results of those sympathies for which the demon thirsted would be children. A race of devils would be propagated upon the earth who might make the very existence of the species of man a condition precarious and full of terror. Did I have the right, for my own benefit, to inflict this curse upon everlasting generations? I had before been moved by the sophisms of the being I had created. I had been struck senseless by his fiendish threats. But now, for the first time, the wickedness of my promise burst upon me. I shuddered to think that future ages might curse me as their pest, whose selfishness had not hesitated to buy its own peace at the price, perhaps, of the existence of the whole human race.

I trembled and my heart failed within me when, on looking up, I saw by the light of the moon the demon at the window. A ghastly grin wrinkled his lips as he gazed on me, where I sat fulfilling the task which he had assigned to me. Yes, he had followed me in my travels. He had loitered in forests, hidden himself in caves, or taken refuge in wide and desert heaths. And he now came to mark my progress and claim the fulfillment of my promise.

As I looked on him, his countenance expressed the utmost extent of malice and treachery. I thought with a sensation of madness on my promise of creating another like him, and trembling with passion, tore to pieces the thing on which I was engaged. The wretch saw me destroy the creature on whose future existence he depended for happiness, and with a howl of devilish despair and revenge, withdrew.

I left the room, and locking the door, made a solemn vow in my own heart never to resume my labors. Then, with trembling steps, I sought my own apartment. I was alone. None were near me to dissipate the gloom and relieve me from the sickening oppression of the most terrible reveries.

Several hours passed, and I stayed near my window gazing at the sea. It was almost motionless, for the winds were quiet, and all of nature rested under the calm moon. A few fishing boats alone dotted the water, and now and then the gentle breeze carried the sound of voices as the fishermen called to one another. I felt the silence, though I was hardly aware of its extreme depth, until my ear was suddenly caught by the sound of oars near the shore, and a person landed close to my house.

A few minutes later, I heard the creaking of my door, as if someone was trying to open it softly. I trembled from head to foot. I had a feeling of who it was and wished to wake one of the peasants who lived in a cottage not far from mine; but I was overcome by that helplessness so often felt in frightful dreams, when you try in vain to escape from approaching danger, and I was rooted to the spot.

Soon I heard the sound of footsteps along the passage; the door opened, and the wretch I dreaded appeared. Shutting the door, he came toward me and said in a muffled voice,

“You have destroyed the work you began; what do you intend to do? Do you dare to break your promise? I have endured toil and misery. I left Switzerland with you; I crept along the shores of the Rhine, among its willow islands and over the tops of its hills. I have lived for many months on the heaths of England and among the deserts of Scotland. I have endured endless fatigue, and cold, and hunger; do you dare destroy my hopes?”

“Go! I do break my promise; I will never create another like yourself, equal in deformity and wickedness.”

“Slave, I reasoned with you before, but you have shown yourself unworthy of my kindness. Remember that I have power. You believe yourself miserable, but I can make you so wretched that the light of day will be hateful to you. You are my creator, but I am your master; obey!”

“The hour of my hesitation is past, and the time of your power has arrived. Your threats cannot move me to commit an act of wickedness; instead, they confirm my determination not to create a companion in vice for you. Shall I, in cold blood, set loose upon the earth a demon whose delight is in death and misery? Go! I am firm, and your words will only inflame my rage.”

The monster saw my determination in my face and gnashed his teeth in helpless anger. “Shall each man,” he cried, “find a wife for his heart, and each beast have its mate, and I be alone? I had feelings of affection, and they were answered with hatred and scorn. Man! You may hate, but beware! Your hours will pass in dread and misery, and soon the blow will fall that must tear your happiness from you forever. Are you to be happy while I crawl in the depth of my wretchedness? You can destroy my other passions, but revenge remains—revenge, from now on dearer than light or food! I may die, but first you, my tyrant and tormentor, shall curse the sun that looks upon your misery. Beware, for I am fearless and therefore powerful. I will watch with the cunning of a snake, so that I may sting with its venom. Man, you shall repent of the injuries you inflict.”

“Devil, stop; do not poison the air with these sounds of malice. I have declared my decision to you, and I am no coward to bend beneath words. Leave me; I am unyielding.”

“Very well. I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding night.”

I started forward and exclaimed, “Villain! Before you sign my death-warrant, be sure that you are yourself safe.”

I would have seized him, but he eluded me and left the house in haste. In a few moments I saw him in his boat, which shot across the waters with arrow-like swiftness and was soon lost amidst the waves.

All was again silent, but his words rang in my ears. I burned with rage to pursue the murderer of my peace and throw him into the ocean. I walked up and down my room hastily and disturbed, while my imagination conjured up a thousand images to torment and sting me. Why had I not followed him and closed with him in mortal struggle? But I had allowed him to depart, and he had directed his course towards the mainland. I shuddered to think who might be the next victim sacrificed to his insatiable revenge. And then I thought again of his words—“I will be with you on your wedding-night.” That, then, was the time fixed for the fulfilment of my destiny. In that hour I should die and at once satisfy and extinguish his malice. The prospect did not move me to fear; yet when I thought of my beloved Elizabeth, of her tears and endless sorrow when she should find her lover so barbarously snatched from her, tears—the first I had shed for many months—streamed from my eyes, and I resolved not to fall before my enemy without a bitter struggle.

The night passed away, and the sun rose from the ocean; my feelings became calmer, if it may be called calmness when the violence of rage sinks into the depths of despair. I left the house, the horrid scene of the last night’s conflict, and walked on the beach of the sea, which I almost regarded as an impassable barrier between me and my fellow creatures; indeed, a wish that such should prove the fact stole over me. I desired that I might pass my life on that barren rock, wearily, it is true, but uninterrupted by any sudden shock of misery. If I returned, it was to be sacrificed or to see those whom I most loved die under the grasp of a demon whom I had myself created.

I walked about the isle like a restless spectre, separated from all it loved and miserable in the separation. When it became noon and the sun rose higher, I lay down on the grass and was overpowered by a deep sleep. I had been awake the whole of the preceding night, my nerves were agitated, and my eyes inflamed by watching and misery. The sleep into which I now sank refreshed me; and when I awoke, I again felt as if I belonged to a race of human beings like myself, and I began to reflect upon what had passed with greater composure; yet still the words of the fiend rang in my ears like a death-knell; they appeared like a dream, yet distinct and oppressive as a reality.

The sun had set, and I still sat on the shore, satisfying my ravenous appetite with an oaten cake, when I saw a fishing-boat land close to me, and one of the men brought me a packet. It contained letters from Geneva, and one from Clerval entreating me to join him. He said that he was wearing away his time fruitlessly where he was, and that letters from the friends he had formed in London desired his return to complete the negotiation they had entered into for his Indian enterprise. He could not any longer delay his departure; but as his journey to London might be followed, even sooner than he now expected, by his longer voyage, he entreated me to give him as much of my company as I could spare. He begged me, therefore, to leave my solitary isle and to meet him at Perth, so that we might travel southwards together. This letter in a degree recalled me to life, and I decided to leave my island in two days.

Yet, before I left, there was a task to perform that I shuddered to think about. I had to pack up my chemical instruments, and for that purpose I had to enter the room that had been the scene of my odious work, and I had to handle those utensils the sight of which was sickening to me. The next morning, at daybreak, I gathered enough courage and unlocked the door of my laboratory. The remains of the half-finished creature, whom I had destroyed, lay scattered on the floor, and I almost felt as if I had mangled the living flesh of a human being. I paused to collect myself and then entered the room. With trembling hands I carried the instruments out, but I realized that I should not leave the remains of my work to excite the horror and suspicion of the peasants. I therefore put them into a basket with a great quantity of stones, and decided to throw them into the sea that very night. In the meantime I sat on the beach, cleaning and arranging my chemical apparatus.

Nothing could be more complete than the change that had taken place in my feelings since the night of the appearance of the demon. I had before regarded my promise with gloomy despair as a thing that, whatever the consequences, must be fulfilled. But now I felt as if a film had been taken from before my eyes and that I saw clearly for the first time. The idea of renewing my labours did not occur to me for one instant. The threat I had heard weighed on my thoughts, but I did not think that a voluntary act of mine could prevent it. I had resolved in my own mind that to create another like the fiend I had first made would be an act of the basest and most atrocious selfishness, and I banished from my mind every thought that could lead to a different conclusion.

Between two and three in the morning the moon rose. I then put my basket aboard a little skiff and sailed out about four miles from the shore. The scene was perfectly solitary; a few boats were returning towards land, but I sailed away from them. I felt as if I was about to commit a dreadful crime and avoided any encounter with my fellow creatures with shuddering anxiety. At one time the moon, which had before been clear, was suddenly covered by a thick cloud, and I took advantage of the moment of darkness and cast my basket into the sea. I listened to the gurgling sound as it sank and then sailed away from the spot. The sky became clouded, but the air was pure, although chilled by the northeast breeze that was then rising. But it refreshed me and filled me with such agreeable sensations that I resolved to prolong my stay on the water. Fixing the rudder in a direct position, I stretched myself at the bottom of the boat. Clouds hid the moon, everything was obscure, and I heard only the sound of the boat as its keel cut through the waves. The murmur lulled me, and in a short time I slept soundly.

I do not know how long I remained in this situation, but when I awoke I found that the sun had already risen considerably. The wind was high, and the waves continually threatened the safety of my little skiff. I found that the wind was northeast and must have driven me far from the coast from which I had embarked. I tried to change my course but quickly found that if I made the attempt again the boat would be instantly filled with water. In this situation, my only resource was to drive before the wind. I confess that I felt a few sensations of terror. I had no compass with me and was so slightly acquainted with the geography of this part of the world that the sun was of little benefit to me. I might be driven into the wide Atlantic and feel all the tortures of starvation or be swallowed up in the immeasurable waters that roared and buffeted around me. I had already been out many hours and felt the torment of a burning thirst, a prelude to my other sufferings. I looked on the heavens, which were covered by clouds that flew before the wind, only to be replaced by others. I looked upon the sea; it was to be my grave. “Fiend,” I exclaimed, “your task is already fulfilled!” I thought of Elizabeth, of my father, and of Clerval—all left behind, on whom the monster might satisfy his bloodthirsty and merciless passions. This idea plunged me into a reverie so despairing and frightful that even now, when the scene is on the point of closing before me forever, I shudder to reflect on it.

Some hours passed thus; but by degrees, as the sun declined towards the horizon, the wind died away into a gentle breeze and the sea became free from breakers. But these gave place to a heavy swell; I felt sick and hardly able to hold the rudder, when suddenly I saw a line of high land towards the south.

Almost spent, as I was, by fatigue and the dreadful suspense I endured for several hours, this sudden certainty of life rushed like a flood of warm joy to my heart, and tears gushed from my eyes.

How changeable our feelings are, and how strange is that clinging love of life we have even in the depths of misery! I made another sail out of part of my clothing and eagerly steered toward the land. It looked wild and rocky, but as I got nearer I could easily see signs of cultivation. I saw ships near the shore and found myself suddenly carried back to the neighborhood of civilized people. I carefully followed the curves of the coastline and spotted a steeple that I finally saw rising from behind a small headland. Since I was extremely weak, I decided to sail directly toward the town, as a place where I could most easily find food. Fortunately I had money with me. As I rounded the headland I saw a small, tidy town and a good harbor, which I entered, my heart leaping with joy at my unexpected escape.

As I was busy securing the boat and arranging the sails, several people crowded toward the spot. They seemed very surprised at my appearance, but instead of offering me any help, they whispered together with gestures that at any other time might have caused me a slight feeling of alarm. As it was, I simply noticed that they spoke English, and so I addressed them in that language. "My good friends," I said, "will you be so kind as to tell me the name of this town and tell me where I am?"

"You will know that soon enough," replied a man with a rough voice. "Maybe you have come to a place that will not prove much to your taste, but you will not be asked where you want to stay, I promise you."

I was extremely surprised to receive such a rude answer from a stranger, and I was also unsettled to see the frowning and angry faces of his companions. "Why do you answer me so harshly?" I replied. "Surely it is not the custom of Englishmen to receive strangers so coldly."

"I do not know," said the man, "what the custom of the English may be, but it is the custom of the Irish to hate villains."

While this strange conversation continued, I saw the crowd rapidly grow larger. Their faces showed a mixture of curiosity and anger, which annoyed me and to some degree alarmed me. I asked the way to the inn, but no one answered. I then moved forward, and a murmuring sound rose from the crowd as they followed and surrounded me, when a rough-looking man came up, tapped me on the shoulder, and said, "Come, sir, you must follow me to Mr. Kirwin's to give an account of yourself."

"Who is Mr. Kirwin? Why must I give an account of myself? Is this not a free country?"

"Yes, sir, free enough for honest people. Mr. Kirwin is a magistrate, and you are to give an account of the death of a gentleman who was found murdered here last night."

This answer startled me, but I quickly recovered myself. I was innocent; that could easily be proved; so I followed my guide in silence and was led to one of the best houses in the town. I was ready to collapse from fatigue and hunger, but being surrounded by a crowd, I thought it wise to summon all my strength, so that no physical weakness might be interpreted as fear or conscious guilt. Little did I then expect the disaster that was in a few moments to overwhelm me and extinguish in horror and despair all fear of disgrace or death.

I must pause here, for it takes all my courage to recall the memory of the frightful events that I am about to relate, in proper detail, to my mind.

I was soon introduced to the magistrate, an old, kind man with calm and gentle manners. He looked at me, however, with some degree of severity, and then, turning to my conductors, he asked who appeared as witnesses on this occasion.

About half a dozen men came forward. He walked on first, carrying part of the fishing tackle, and his companions followed him at some distance. As he was walking along the sands, he struck his foot against something and fell flat on the ground. His companions came up to help him, and by the light of their lantern they found that he had fallen on the body of a man, who was to all appearance dead. Their first thought was that it was the corpse of someone who had been drowned and was thrown on shore by the waves, but on examination they found that the clothes were not wet and even that the body was not then cold. They instantly carried it to the cottage of an old woman near the spot and tried, but in vain, to restore it to life. It appeared to be a handsome young man, about twenty-five years of age. He had apparently been strangled, for there was no sign of any violence except the black mark of fingers on his neck.

The first part of this testimony did not interest me in the least, but when the mark of the fingers was mentioned I remembered the murder of my brother and felt myself extremely agitated. My limbs trembled, and a mist came over my eyes, which forced me to lean on a chair for support. The magistrate observed me with a keen eye and of course drew an unfavorable conclusion from my manner.

The son confirmed his father's account, but when Daniel Nugent was called he swore positively that just before his companion fell, he saw a boat, with a single man in it, at a short distance from the shore. As far as he could judge by the light of a few stars, it was the same boat in which I had just landed.

A woman testified that she lived near the beach and was standing at the door of her cottage, waiting for the return of the fishermen, about an hour before she heard of the discovery of the body, when she saw a boat with only one man in it push off from that part of the shore where the corpse was afterwards found.

Another woman confirmed the account of the fishermen having brought the body into her house; it was not cold. They put it into a bed and rubbed it, and Daniel went to the town for an apothecary, but life was quite gone.

Several other men were examined concerning my landing, and they agreed that, with the strong north wind that had arisen during the night, it was very probable that I had been tossed about for many hours and had been forced to return nearly to the same spot from which I had departed. Besides, they observed that it appeared that I had brought the body from another place, and it was likely that as I did not appear to know the shore, I might have put into the harbour ignorant of the distance of the town of —— from the place where I had deposited the corpse.

Mr. Kirwin, on hearing this evidence, asked that I be taken into the room where the body lay awaiting burial, so that he might observe what effect the sight of it would have on me. This idea was probably suggested by the extreme agitation I had shown when the manner of the murder had been described. I was therefore led, by the magistrate and several other people, to the inn. I could not help being struck by the strange coincidences that had taken place during this eventful night; but, knowing that I had been talking with several people on the island where I had been staying around the time the body had been found, I was perfectly calm about the consequences of the affair.

I entered the room where the corpse lay and was led up to the coffin. How can I describe my feelings on seeing it? I still feel dried up with horror, and I cannot think back on that terrible moment without shuddering and agony. The examination, the presence of the magistrate and witnesses, passed like a dream from my memory when I saw the lifeless form of Henry Clerval stretched before me. I gasped for breath, and throwing myself on the body, I cried out, “Have my murderous schemes deprived you too, my dearest Henry, of life? Two I have already destroyed; other victims await their fate; but you, Clerval, my friend, my benefactor—”

My body could no longer support the agonies that I endured, and I was carried out of the room in violent convulsions.

A fever followed this. I lay for two months on the point of death; my delirious ravings, as I later heard, were frightful. I called myself the murderer of William, of Justine, and of Clerval. Sometimes I begged my attendants to help me destroy the fiend by whom I was tormented; and at other times I felt the fingers of the monster already grasping my neck, and screamed aloud with agony and terror. Fortunately, since I spoke my native language, only Mr. Kirwin understood me; but my gestures and bitter cries were enough to frighten the other witnesses.

Why did I not die? More miserable than any man had ever been before, why did I not sink into forgetfulness and rest? Death snatches away many blooming children, the only hopes of their doting parents; how many brides and youthful lovers have been one day in the bloom of health and hope, and the next a prey for worms and the decay of the tomb! Of what materials was I made that I could resist so many shocks, which, like the turning of a wheel, continually renewed the torture?

But I was doomed to live, and in two months I found myself as if waking from a dream, in a prison, stretched on a wretched bed, surrounded by jailers, turnkeys, bolts, and all the miserable equipment of a dungeon. It was morning, I remember, when I thus awoke to understanding; I had forgotten the details of what had happened and only felt as if some great misfortune had suddenly overwhelmed me. But when I looked around and saw the barred windows and the squalor of the room I was in, everything flashed across my memory and I groaned bitterly.

This sound disturbed an old woman who was sleeping in a chair beside me. She was a hired nurse, the wife of one of the turnkeys, and her face showed all those bad qualities which often mark that class of person. The lines of her face were hard and rough, like those of people used to seeing misery without feeling for it. Her tone showed complete indifference. She spoke to me in English, and the voice struck me as one I had heard during my sufferings.

“Are you better now, sir?” she said.

I replied in the same language, with a feeble voice, “I believe I am; but if it is all true, if indeed I did not dream, I am sorry that I am still alive to feel this misery and horror.”

“As for that,” replied the old woman, “if you mean about the gentleman you murdered, I believe it would be better for you if you were dead, for I think it will go hard with you! However, that is none of my business. I am sent to nurse you and get you well. I do my duty with a clear conscience; it would be well if everyone did the same.”

I turned with disgust from the woman who could say such an unfeeling thing to a person just saved from the very edge of death. But I felt weak and unable to reflect on all that had happened. The whole series of my life appeared to me as a dream. I sometimes doubted if it were all true, for it never presented itself to my mind with the force of reality.

As the images that floated before me became more distinct, I grew feverish. A darkness pressed around me. No one was near me to soothe me with the gentle voice of love; no dear hand supported me. The physician came and prescribed medicines, and the old woman prepared them for me. But utter carelessness was visible in the first, and the expression of brutality was strongly marked in the face of the second. Who could be interested in the fate of a murderer but the hangman who would gain his fee?

These were my first reflections, but I soon learned that Mr. Kirwin had shown me extreme kindness. He had caused the best room in the prison to be prepared for me (wretched indeed was the best); and it was he who had provided a physician and a nurse. It is true, he seldom came to see me, for although he ardently desired to relieve the sufferings of every human creature, he did not wish to be present at the agonies and miserable ravings of a murderer. He came, therefore, sometimes to see that I was not neglected, but his visits were short and with long intervals.

One day, while I was gradually recovering, I was seated in a chair, my eyes half open and my cheeks pale like those in death. I was overcome by gloom and misery and often reflected I had better seek death than desire to remain in a world which to me was filled with wretchedness. At one time I considered whether I should not declare myself guilty and suffer the penalty of the law, less innocent than poor Justine had been. Such were my thoughts when the door of my apartment was opened and Mr. Kirwin entered. His face expressed sympathy and compassion. He drew a chair close to mine and addressed me in French,

“I fear that this place is very shocking to you; can I do anything to make you more comfortable?”

“I thank you, but all that you mention is nothing to me; on the whole earth there is no comfort which I am capable of receiving.”

“I know that the sympathy of a stranger can be but little relief to one weighed down as you are by so strange a misfortune. But you will, I hope, soon leave this gloomy place, for surely evidence can easily be brought to free you from the criminal charge.”

“That is my least concern; I am, through a course of strange events, become the most miserable of mortals. Persecuted and tortured as I am and have been, can death be any evil to me?”

“Nothing indeed could be more unfortunate and agonizing than the strange chances that have lately occurred. You were thrown, by some surprising accident, on this shore, renowned for its hospitality, seized immediately, and charged with murder. The first sight that was presented to your eyes was the body of your friend, murdered in so unaccountable a manner and placed, as it were, by some fiend across your path.”

As Mr. Kirwin said this, despite the agitation I felt at this review of my sufferings, I also felt considerable surprise at the knowledge he seemed to possess concerning me. I suppose some astonishment was shown in my face, for Mr. Kirwin hastened to say,

“Immediately upon your being taken ill, all the papers that were on your person were brought to me, and I examined them so that I might discover some clue by which I could send to your relations an account of your misfortune and illness. I found several letters, and, among others, one which I discovered from its opening to be from your father. I instantly wrote to Geneva; nearly two months have passed since the departure of my letter. But you are ill; even now you tremble; you are unfit for agitation of any kind.”

“This suspense is a thousand times worse than the most horrible event; tell me what new scene of death has been acted, and whose murder I am now to mourn?”

“Your family is perfectly well,” said Mr. Kirwin with gentleness; “and someone, a friend, has come to visit you.”

I know not by what chain of thought the idea presented itself, but it instantly darted into my mind that the murderer had come to mock at my misery and taunt me with the death of Clerval, as a new incitement for me to comply with his hellish desires. I put my hand before my eyes and cried out in agony,

“Oh! Take him away! I cannot see him; for God’s sake, do not let him enter!”

Mr. Kirwin regarded me with a troubled expression. He could not help regarding my exclamation as a presumption of my guilt and said in rather a severe tone,

“I should have thought, young man, that the presence of your father would have been welcome instead of inspiring such violent repugnance.”

“My father!” cried I, while every feature and every muscle relaxed from anguish to pleasure. “Is my father indeed come? How kind, how very kind! But where is he, why does he not hasten to me?”

My change of manner surprised and pleased the magistrate; perhaps he thought that my former exclamation was a momentary return of delirium, and now he instantly resumed his former kindness. He rose and left the room with my nurse, and in a moment my father entered it.

Nothing, at this moment, could have given me greater pleasure than the arrival of my father. I stretched out my hand to him and cried,

“Are you then safe—and Elizabeth—and Ernest?”

My father calmed me with assurances of their welfare and tried, by dwelling on these subjects so interesting to my heart, to raise my desponding spirits; but he soon felt that a prison cannot be the home of cheerfulness. "What a place this is that you live in, my son!" he said, looking mournfully at the barred windows and wretched appearance of the room. "You travelled to seek happiness, but a fatality seems to pursue you. And poor Clerval—"

The name of my unfortunate and murdered friend was an agitation too great to be endured in my weak state; I shed tears.

"Alas! Yes, my father," I replied; "some destiny of the most horrible kind hangs over me, and I must live to fulfil it, or surely I should have died on the coffin of Henry."

We were not allowed to converse for any length of time, for the precarious state of my health made every precaution necessary that could ensure tranquillity. Mr. Kirwin came in and insisted that my strength should not be exhausted by too much exertion. But the appearance of my father was to me like that of my good angel, and I gradually recovered my health.

As my sickness left me, I was absorbed by a gloomy and black melancholy that nothing could dispel. The image of Clerval was forever before me, ghastly and murdered. More than once the agitation into which these reflections threw me made my friends dread a dangerous relapse. Alas! Why did they preserve so miserable and detested a life? It was surely that I might fulfil my destiny, which is now drawing to a close. Soon, oh, very soon, death will extinguish these throbbings and relieve me from the mighty weight of anguish that bears me to the dust; and, in executing the award of justice, I shall also sink to rest. Then the appearance of death was distant, although the wish was ever present to my thoughts; and I often sat for hours motionless and speechless, wishing for some mighty revolution that might bury me and my destroyer in its ruins.

The season of the assizes approached. I had already been three months in prison, and although I was still weak and in continual danger of a relapse, I was obliged to travel nearly a hundred miles to the country town where the court was held. Mr. Kirwin took upon himself every care of collecting witnesses and arranging my defence. I was spared the disgrace of appearing publicly as a criminal, as the case was not brought before the court that decides on life and death. The grand jury rejected the bill, on its being proved that I was on the Orkney Islands at the hour the body of my friend was found; and a fortnight after my removal I was released from prison.

My father was overjoyed on finding me freed from the vexations of a criminal charge, that I was again allowed to breathe the fresh atmosphere and permitted to return to my native country. I did not share in these feelings, for to me the walls of a dungeon or a palace were alike hateful. The cup of life was poisoned forever, and although the sun shone upon me, as upon the happy and gay of heart, I saw around me nothing but a dense and frightful darkness, penetrated by no light but the glimmer of two eyes that glared upon me. Sometimes they were the expressive eyes of Henry, languishing in death, the dark orbs nearly covered by the lids and the long black lashes that fringed them; sometimes it was the watery, clouded eyes of the monster, as I first saw them in my chamber at Ingolstadt.

My father tried to awaken in me the feelings of affection. He talked of Geneva, which I should soon visit, of Elizabeth and Ernest; but these words only drew deep groans from me. Sometimes, indeed, I felt a wish for happiness and thought with melancholy delight of my beloved cousin or longed, with a devouring homesickness, to see once more the blue lake and rapid Rhone, that had been so dear to me in early childhood; but my general state of feeling was a torpor in which a prison was as welcome a residence as the divinest scene in nature; and these fits were seldom interrupted except by paroxysms of anguish and despair. At these moments I often tried to put an end to the existence I loathed, and it required unceasing attendance and vigilance to restrain me from committing some dreadful act of violence.

Yet one duty remained to me, the recollection of which finally triumphed over my selfish despair. It was necessary that I should return without delay to Geneva, there to watch over the lives of those I so fondly loved and to lie in wait for the murderer, that if any chance led me to the place of his concealment, or if he dared again to blast me by his presence, I might, with unfailing aim, put an end to the existence of the monstrous image which I had endued with the mockery of a soul still more monstrous. My father still desired to delay our departure, fearful that I could not sustain the fatigues of a journey, for I was a shattered wreck—the shadow of a human being. My strength was gone. I was a mere skeleton, and fever night and day preyed upon my wasted frame.

Still, as I urged our leaving Ireland with such inquietude and impatience, my father thought it best to yield. We took our passage on board a vessel bound for Havre-de-Grace and sailed with a fair wind from the Irish shores. It was midnight. I lay on the deck looking at the stars and listening to the dashing of the waves. I hailed the darkness that shut Ireland from my sight, and my pulse beat with a feverish joy when I reflected that I should soon see Geneva. The past appeared to me in the light of a frightful dream; yet the vessel in which I was, the wind that blew me from the detested shore of Ireland, and the sea which surrounded me, told me too forcibly that I was deceived by no vision and that Clerval, my friend and dearest companion, had fallen a victim to me and the monster of my creation. I repassed, in my memory, my whole life; my quiet happiness while residing with my family in Geneva, the death of my mother, and my departure for Ingolstadt. I remembered, shuddering, the mad enthusiasm that hurried me on to the creation of my hideous enemy, and I called to mind the night in which he first lived. I was unable to pursue the train of thought; a thousand feelings pressed upon me, and I wept bitterly.

Ever since my recovery from the fever, I had been in the habit of taking a small amount of laudanum every night, for it was only by means of this drug that I could get the rest necessary to stay alive. Weighed down by the memory of my various misfortunes, I now swallowed double my usual amount and soon slept deeply. But sleep did not give me any relief from thought and misery; my dreams presented a thousand objects that scared me. Towards morning, I was seized by a kind of nightmare; I felt the fiend’s grip on my neck and could not free myself from it; groans and cries rang in my ears. My father, who was watching over me, saw my restlessness and woke me. The waves were dashing around, the cloudy sky was above, and the fiend was not here. A sense of security, a feeling that a truce had been established between the present hour and the irresistible, disastrous future, gave me a kind of calm forgetfulness, of which the human mind is by its nature especially susceptible.

The voyage came to an end. We landed and went on to Paris. I soon found that I had pushed myself too hard and that I needed to rest before I could continue my journey. My father’s care and attention were tireless, but he did not know the source of my suffering and tried the wrong ways to cure an incurable illness. He wanted me to find amusement in society. I hated the sight of people. Oh, not hated! They were my brothers, my fellow beings, and I felt drawn even to the most repulsive among them, as to creatures of an angelic nature and heavenly design. But I felt that I had no right to share their company. I had unleashed an enemy among them whose joy it was to shed their blood and to delight in their groans. How they would all hate me and hunt me from the world if they knew my unholy acts and the crimes that had their source in me!

My father at last gave in to my wish to avoid society and tried with various arguments to drive away my despair. Sometimes he thought that I felt deeply the shame of being forced to answer a charge of murder, and he tried to prove to me the uselessness of pride.

“Alas! My father,” I said, “how little you know me. Human beings, their feelings and passions, would indeed be degraded if such a wretch as I felt pride. Justine, poor unhappy Justine, was as innocent as I, and she faced the same charge. She died for it, and I am the cause of this—I murdered her. William, Justine, and Henry—they all died by my hands.”

My father had often, during my imprisonment, heard me make the same claim. When I accused myself in this way, he sometimes seemed to want an explanation, and at other times he appeared to consider it as the product of delirium, and that during my illness some idea of this kind had come into my imagination, the memory of which I kept during my recovery. I avoided explanation and stayed continually silent about the wretch I had created. I was convinced that I would be thought mad, and this in itself would have silenced me forever. But besides that, I could not bring myself to reveal a secret that would fill my listener with shock and make fear and unnatural horror live in his heart. I held back, therefore, my impatient thirst for sympathy and was silent when I would have given the world to confide the fatal secret. Yet still, words like those I have recorded would burst uncontrollably from me. I could offer no explanation of them, but their truth in part relieved the burden of my mysterious grief.

On this occasion my father said, with an expression of unbounded wonder, “My dearest Victor, what madness is this? My dear son, I beg you never to make such a claim again.”

“I am not mad,” I cried with energy. “The sun and the heavens, who have seen my works, can bear witness to my truth. I am the murderer of those most innocent victims; they died because of my schemes. A thousand times I would have shed my own blood, drop by drop, to save their lives. But I could not, my father, indeed I could not sacrifice the whole human race.”

The ending of this speech convinced my father that my thoughts were disordered, and he immediately changed the subject of our conversation and tried to redirect the course of my thoughts. He wished as much as possible to erase the memory of the scenes that had taken place in Ireland and never mentioned them or allowed me to speak of my misfortunes.

As time passed, I became calmer. Misery still lived in my heart, but I no longer talked in the same confused way about my crimes; being conscious of them was enough for me. With great effort, I controlled the overwhelming voice of my suffering, which sometimes wanted to declare itself to the whole world. My manner was calmer and more composed than it had been since my journey to the sea of ice.

A few days before we left Paris for Switzerland, I received the following letter from Elizabeth:

“My dear Friend,

“It gave me the greatest pleasure to receive a letter from my uncle dated at Paris. You are no longer at such a formidable distance, and I may hope to see you in less than two weeks. My poor cousin, how much you must have suffered! I expect to see you looking even more ill than when you left Geneva. This winter has been passed most miserably, tortured as I have been by anxious suspense. Yet I hope to see peace in your face and to find that your heart is not completely without comfort and calm.

“Yet I fear that the same feelings now exist that made you so miserable a year ago, perhaps even increased by time. I would not disturb you at this period, when so many misfortunes weigh upon you, but a conversation that I had with my uncle before he left makes some explanation necessary before we meet.

Explanation! You may possibly say, What can Elizabeth have to explain? If you really say this, my questions are answered and all my doubts are satisfied. But you are distant from me, and it is possible that you may dread and yet be pleased with this explanation. Since this may be the case, I dare not any longer postpone writing what, during your absence, I have often wished to express to you but have never had the courage to begin.

“You well know, Victor, that our union has been the favorite plan of your parents ever since our childhood. We were told this when young, and taught to look forward to it as an event that would certainly take place. We were affectionate playmates during childhood, and, I believe, dear and valued friends to one another as we grew older. But as brother and sister often feel a lively affection for each other without desiring a more intimate union, may not such also be our case? Tell me, dearest Victor. Answer me, I beg you by our mutual happiness, with simple truth—Do you not love another?

You have traveled; you spent several years of your life at Ingolstadt. And I confess to you, my friend, that when I saw you last autumn so unhappy, fleeing into solitude from everyone, I could not help thinking that you might regret our connection and believe yourself bound by honor to fulfill your parents’ wishes, even though they opposed your own desires. But that is false reasoning. I confess to you, my friend, that I love you, and that in my hopeful dreams of the future you have been my constant friend and companion. But it is your happiness I desire as well as my own when I tell you that our marriage would make me eternally miserable unless it came from your own free choice. Even now I weep to think that, weighed down as you are by the cruelest misfortunes, you might stifle, by the word honor, all hope of that love and happiness which alone would restore you to yourself. I, who have such a selfless affection for you, might increase your miseries tenfold by being an obstacle to your wishes. Ah! Victor, be assured that your cousin and playmate loves you too sincerely not to be made miserable by this thought. Be happy, my friend; and if you obey me in this one request, rest assured that nothing on earth will have the power to disturb my peace.

Do not let this letter disturb you; do not answer tomorrow, or the next day, or even until you come, if it will give you pain. My uncle will send me news of your health, and if I see but one smile on your lips when we meet, caused by this or any other effort of mine, I shall need no other happiness.

Elizabeth Lavenza.

Geneva, May 18th, 17—

This letter revived in my memory what I had forgotten before: the threat of the fiend—“I will be with you on your wedding-night!” Such was my sentence, and on that night the demon would use every art to destroy me and tear me from the glimpse of happiness which promised partly to console my sufferings. On that night he had determined to complete his crimes by my death. Well, so be it; a deadly struggle would then surely take place, in which if he were victorious I would be at peace and his power over me would be at an end. If he were defeated, I would be a free man. Alas! What freedom? Such as the peasant enjoys when his family have been massacred before his eyes, his cottage burned, his lands laid waste, and he is turned adrift, homeless, penniless, and alone, but free. Such would be my liberty, except that in my Elizabeth I possessed a treasure—alas, balanced by those horrors of remorse and guilt which would pursue me until death.

He had vowed to be with me on my wedding-night, yet he did not consider that threat as binding him to peace in the meantime, for as if to show me that he was not yet satisfied with blood, he had murdered Clerval immediately after making his threats. I resolved, therefore, that if my immediate union with my cousin would add to either her happiness or my father’s, my adversary’s designs against my life should not delay it a single hour.

In this state of mind I wrote to Elizabeth. My letter was calm and affectionate. “I fear, my beloved girl,” I said, “little happiness remains for us on earth; yet all that I may one day enjoy is centred in you. Chase away your idle fears; to you alone do I dedicate my life and my efforts for contentment. I have one secret, Elizabeth, a dreadful one; when revealed to you, it will chill your body with horror, and then, far from being surprised at my misery, you will only wonder that I survive what I have endured. I will confide this tale of misery and terror to you the day after our marriage takes place, for, my sweet cousin, there must be perfect confidence between us. But until then, I beg you, do not mention or allude to it. This I most earnestly entreat, and I know you will comply.”

In about a week after the arrival of Elizabeth’s letter we returned to Geneva. The sweet girl welcomed me with warm affection, yet tears were in her eyes as she saw my emaciated frame and feverish cheeks. I saw a change in her also. She was thinner and had lost much of that heavenly liveliness that had before charmed me; but her gentleness and soft looks of compassion made her a more fit companion for one as blasted and miserable as I was.

The tranquillity which I now enjoyed did not endure. Memory brought madness with it, and when I thought of what had passed, a real insanity possessed me; sometimes I was furious and burnt with rage, sometimes low and despondent. I neither spoke nor looked at anyone, but sat motionless, bewildered by the multitude of miseries that overcame me.

Elizabeth alone had the power to draw me from these fits; her gentle voice would soothe me when I was carried away by passion and inspire me with human feelings when I sank into numbness. She wept with me and for me. When reason returned, she would gently protest and try to inspire me with acceptance. Ah! It is well for the unfortunate to be resigned, but for the guilty there is no peace. The agonies of remorse poison the comfort that is otherwise sometimes found in giving way to excessive grief.

Soon after my arrival my father spoke of my immediate marriage with Elizabeth. I remained silent.

“Have you, then, some other attachment?”

“None on earth. I love Elizabeth and look forward to our union with delight. Let the day therefore be fixed; and on it I will dedicate myself, in life or death, to the happiness of my cousin.”

“My dear Victor, do not speak like that. Heavy misfortunes have befallen us, but let us only cling closer to what remains and transfer our love for those whom we have lost to those who yet live. Our circle will be small but bound close by the ties of affection and mutual misfortune. And when time shall have softened your despair, new and dear objects of care will be born to replace those of whom we have been so cruelly deprived.”

Such were the lessons of my father. But to me the remembrance of the threat returned. Nor can you wonder that, omnipotent as the fiend had yet been in his deeds of blood, I should almost regard him as invincible, and that when he had pronounced the words “_I shall be with you on your wedding-night_,” I should regard the threatened fate as unavoidable. But death was no evil to me if the loss of Elizabeth were balanced with it. I therefore, with a contented and even cheerful countenance, agreed with my father that if my cousin would consent, the ceremony should take place in ten days, and thus put, as I imagined, the seal to my fate.

Great God! If for one instant I had thought what might be the hellish intention of my fiendish adversary, I would rather have banished myself forever from my native country and wandered a friendless outcast over the earth than have consented to this miserable marriage. But, as if possessed of magic powers, the monster had blinded me to his real intentions. And when I thought that I had prepared only my own death, I hastened that of a far dearer victim.

As the period fixed for our marriage drew nearer, whether from cowardice or a prophetic feeling, I felt my heart sink within me. But I concealed my feelings by an appearance of hilarity that brought smiles and joy to the countenance of my father, but hardly deceived the ever-watchful and nicer eye of Elizabeth. She looked forward to our union with placid contentment, not unmingled with a little fear, which past misfortunes had impressed, that what now appeared certain and tangible happiness might soon dissipate into an airy dream and leave no trace but deep and everlasting regret.

Preparations were made for the event, congratulatory visits were received, and all wore a smiling appearance. I shut up, as well as I could, in my own heart the anxiety that preyed there and entered with seeming earnestness into the plans of my father, although they might only serve as the decorations of my tragedy. Through my father’s exertions a part of the inheritance of Elizabeth had been restored to her by the Austrian government. A small possession on the shores of Como belonged to her. It was agreed that, immediately after our union, we should proceed to Villa Lavenza and spend our first days of happiness beside the beautiful lake near which it stood.

In the meantime I took every precaution to defend my person in case the fiend should openly attack me. I carried pistols and a dagger constantly about me and was ever on the watch to prevent artifice. By these means I gained a greater degree of tranquillity. Indeed, as the period approached, the threat appeared more as a delusion, not to be regarded as worthy to disturb my peace, while the happiness I hoped for in my marriage wore a greater appearance of certainty as the day fixed for its solemnisation drew nearer and I heard it continually spoken of as an occurrence which no accident could possibly prevent.

Elizabeth seemed happy; my calm manner helped greatly to ease her mind. But on the day that was to fulfill my wishes and my destiny, she was sad, and a feeling of coming evil filled her; and perhaps she also thought of the dreadful secret I had promised to reveal to her on the following day. My father was meanwhile overjoyed, and in the bustle of preparation, he saw only the shyness of a bride in his niece's sadness.

After the ceremony was performed, a large party gathered at my father's house, but it was agreed that Elizabeth and I should begin our journey by water, sleeping that night at Evian and continuing our voyage on the following day. The day was fair, the wind favorable; everything smiled on our wedding departure.

Those were the last moments of my life during which I enjoyed the feeling of happiness. We passed rapidly along; the sun was hot, but we were sheltered from its rays by a kind of canopy while we enjoyed the beauty of the scene. Sometimes we were on one side of the lake, where we saw Mont Salêve, the pleasant banks of Montalègre, and at a distance, rising above all, the beautiful Mont Blanc and the gathering of snowy mountains that try in vain to rival her. Sometimes coasting the opposite banks, we saw the mighty Jura opposing its dark side to any ambition that would leave its native country, and forming an almost insurmountable barrier to any invader who should wish to enslave it.

I took the hand of Elizabeth. "You are sorrowful, my love. Ah! If you knew what I have suffered and what I may yet endure, you would try to let me taste the quiet and freedom from despair that this one day at least permits me to enjoy."

"Be happy, my dear Victor," replied Elizabeth; "there is, I hope, nothing to distress you; and be assured that if a lively joy is not painted on my face, my heart is content. Something whispers to me not to depend too much on the prospect that is opened before us, but I will not listen to such a sinister voice. Observe how fast we move along and how the clouds, which sometimes obscure and sometimes rise above the dome of Mont Blanc, make this scene of beauty still more interesting. Look also at the innumerable fish that are swimming in the clear waters, where we can distinguish every pebble that lies at the bottom. What a divine day! How happy and serene all nature appears!"

Thus Elizabeth tried to turn her thoughts and mine away from all reflection on sad subjects. But her mood was changeable; joy for a few moments shone in her eyes, but it continually gave way to distraction and deep thought.

The sun sank lower in the sky; we passed the river Drance and observed its path through the chasms of the higher hills and the valleys of the lower hills. The Alps here come closer to the lake, and we approached the amphitheater of mountains that forms its eastern boundary. The spire of Evian shone under the woods that surrounded it and the range of mountain above mountain by which it was overhung.

The wind, which had until now carried us along with amazing speed, sank at sunset to a light breeze; the soft air just ruffled the water and caused a pleasant motion among the trees as we approached the shore, from which it carried the most delightful scent of flowers and hay. The sun sank beneath the horizon as we landed, and as I touched the shore I felt those cares and fears revive which soon were to clasp me and cling to me forever.

It was eight o’clock when we landed; we walked for a short time on the shore, enjoying the fading light, and then went back to the inn and gazed at the lovely scene of waters, woods, and mountains, now hidden in darkness but still showing their black outlines.

I had been calm during the day, but as soon as night hid the shapes of objects, a thousand fears rose in my mind. It came from the room into which Elizabeth had retired. As I heard it, the whole truth rushed into my mind, my arms dropped, the motion of every muscle and fibre stopped; I could feel the blood trickling in my veins and tingling in the ends of my limbs. This state lasted only for an instant; the scream was repeated, and I rushed into the room.

Great God! Why did I not die then! Why am I here to tell of the destruction of the best hope and the purest creature on earth? She was there, lifeless and still, thrown across the bed, her head hanging down and her pale and distorted features half covered by her hair. Life is stubborn and clings closest where it is most hated. For a moment only did I lose consciousness; I fell senseless to the ground.

When I came to, I found myself surrounded by the people of the inn. Their faces showed breathless terror, but the horror of others seemed only a mockery, a shadow of the feelings that crushed me. I escaped from them to the room where the body of Elizabeth lay—my love, my wife, so recently alive, so dear, so worthy. She had been moved from the position in which I had first seen her, and now, as she lay with her head upon her arm and a handkerchief thrown across her face and neck, I might have supposed her asleep. I rushed toward her and embraced her with passion, but the deadly stillness and coldness of her limbs told me that what I now held in my arms had ceased to be the Elizabeth whom I had loved and cherished. The murderous mark of the fiend's grasp was on her neck, and breath no longer came from her lips.

The windows of the room had been darkened before, and I felt a kind of panic on seeing the pale yellow light of the moon illuminate the chamber. After passing several hours, we returned hopeless, most of my companions believing it to have been a form conjured up by my imagination. In this state I was carried back and placed on a bed, hardly conscious of what had happened; my eyes wandered round the room as if to seek something that I had lost.

I was bewildered, in a cloud of wonder and horror. The death of William, the execution of Justine, the murder of Clerval, and lastly of my wife; even at that moment I did not know that my only remaining friends were safe from the malice of the fiend. My father even now might be writhing under his grasp, and Ernest might be dead at his feet. This idea made me shudder and recalled me to action. There were no horses to be found, and I had to return by the lake. But the wind was against me, and the rain fell in torrents. Still, it was hardly morning, and I could reasonably hope to arrive by night. I hired men to row and took an oar myself, for I had always found relief from mental torment in physical exercise. But the overwhelming misery I now felt, and the extreme agitation I endured, made me incapable of any effort. I threw down the oar and, leaning my head on my hands, gave way to every dark thought that arose. If I looked up, I saw scenes that were familiar to me in happier times and which I had viewed only the day before in the company of her who was now only a shadow and a memory. Tears streamed from my eyes. The rain had stopped for a moment, and I saw the fish play in the waters as they had done a few hours before. Elizabeth had noticed them then. Nothing is so painful to the human mind as a great and sudden change. The sun might shine or the clouds might lower, but nothing could appear to me as it had done the day before. A fiend had snatched from me every hope of future happiness. No creature had ever been as miserable as I was. So frightful an event is unique in the history of man.

But why should I dwell on the events that followed this last overwhelming blow? Mine has been a tale of horrors. I have reached their peak, and what I must now relate can only be tedious to you. Know that, one by one, my friends were taken from me, and I was left desolate. My own strength is exhausted, and I must tell, in a few words, what remains of my hideous story.

I arrived at Geneva. My father and Ernest still lived, but the former collapsed under the news that I brought. I see him now, excellent and venerable old man! His eyes wandered blankly, for they had lost their charm and their delight—his Elizabeth, his more than daughter, whom he loved with all the affection that a man feels when, in the decline of life, having few attachments, he clings more earnestly to those that remain. Cursed, cursed be the fiend that brought misery on his grey hairs and doomed him to waste away in wretchedness! He could not live under the horrors that had accumulated around him. The springs of existence suddenly gave way. He was unable to rise from his bed, and in a few days he died in my arms.

What then became of me? I do not know. I lost consciousness, and chains and darkness were the only things that pressed upon me. Sometimes, indeed, I dreamed that I wandered in flowery meadows and pleasant valleys with the friends of my youth, but I awoke and found myself in a dungeon. Melancholy followed, but by degrees I gained a clear understanding of my miseries and situation and was then released from my prison. For they had called me mad, and for many months, as I understood, a solitary cell had been my home.

Liberty, however, would have been a useless gift to me had I not, as I awakened to reason, at the same time awakened to revenge. As the memory of past misfortunes pressed upon me, I began to reflect on their cause—the monster whom I had created, the miserable demon whom I had sent out into the world for my destruction. I was possessed by a maddening rage when I thought of him, and I desired and ardently prayed that I might have him within my grasp to inflict a great and signal revenge on his cursed head.

Nor did my hate long confine itself to useless wishes. I began to think about the best way to capture him. For this purpose, about a month after my release, I went to a criminal judge in the town and told him that I had an accusation to make, that I knew the destroyer of my family, and that I required him to use his full authority to arrest the murderer.

The magistrate listened to me with attention and kindness. “Be assured, sir,” he said, “no effort on my part shall be spared to discover the villain.”

“I thank you,” I replied. “Listen, therefore, to the statement that I have to make. It is indeed a tale so strange that I would fear you would not believe it, were there not something in truth which, however wonderful, forces conviction. The story is too connected to be mistaken for a dream, and I have no motive for falsehood.” My manner as I addressed him was impressive but calm. I had formed in my own heart a resolution to pursue my destroyer to death, and this purpose quieted my agony and for a time reconciled me to life. I now related my history briefly but with firmness and precision, marking the dates with accuracy and never drifting into insults or exclamations.

The magistrate appeared at first completely incredulous, but as I continued he became more attentive and interested. I saw him sometimes shudder with horror; at other times a lively surprise, unmixed with disbelief, was painted on his face.

When I had concluded my narration, I said, “This is the being whom I accuse and for whose seizure and punishment I call upon you to use your full power. It is your duty as a magistrate, and I believe and hope that your feelings as a man will not turn away from carrying out those duties on this occasion.”

This address caused a considerable change in the expression of my listener. He had heard my story with that half kind of belief that is given to a tale of spirits and supernatural events; but when he was called upon to act officially in consequence, the whole tide of his disbelief returned. He, however, answered mildly, “I would willingly give you every aid in your pursuit, but the creature of whom you speak appears to have powers which would put all my efforts to defiance. Who can follow an animal which can cross the sea of ice and live in caves and dens where no man would dare to go? As I spoke, rage sparkled in my eyes; the magistrate was intimidated. “You are mistaken,” he said. “I will exert myself, and if it is in my power to seize the monster, be assured that he shall suffer punishment proportionate to his crimes. But I fear, from what you have yourself described to be his properties, that this will prove impossible; and so, while every proper measure is pursued, you should prepare yourself for disappointment.”

That cannot be; but all that I can say will be of little use. My revenge means nothing to you; yet, while I admit it is a fault, I confess it is the consuming and only passion of my soul. My rage is beyond words when I think that the murderer, whom I have set loose upon society, still lives. You refuse my just demand; I have only one option left, and I dedicate myself, whether in life or death, to his destruction.

I trembled with extreme agitation as I said this; there was a wild intensity in my manner, and something, I am sure, of that proud fierceness which the martyrs of old are said to have possessed. But to a Genevan magistrate, whose mind was occupied with far different ideas than those of devotion and heroism, this elevation of mind looked very much like madness. He tried to calm me as a nurse does a child and returned to my story as the effects of delirium.

“Man,” I cried, “how ignorant you are in your pride of wisdom! Stop; you do not know what you are saying.”

I left the house angry and disturbed and withdrew to think of some other course of action.

My present situation was one in which all voluntary thought was swallowed up and lost. I was hurried away by fury; revenge alone gave me strength and composure. It shaped my feelings and allowed me to be calculating and calm at times when otherwise delirium or death would have been my fate.

I have traveled across a vast portion of the earth and have endured all the hardships that travelers in deserts and uncivilized countries are accustomed to meet. How I have lived I hardly know; many times I have stretched my failing limbs upon the sandy plain and prayed for death. But revenge kept me alive; I dared not die and leave my enemy still living.

When I left Geneva my first task was to gain some clue by which I might trace the steps of my fiendish enemy. The blue Mediterranean appeared, and by a strange chance, I saw the fiend enter by night and hide himself in a vessel bound for the Black Sea. To you first entering on life, to whom care is new and agony unknown, how can you understand what I have felt and still feel? Cold, want, and fatigue were the least pains which I was destined to endure; I was cursed by some devil and carried about with me my eternal hell; yet still a spirit of good followed and directed my steps, and when I most complained would suddenly free me from seemingly insurmountable difficulties. Sometimes, when nature, overcome by hunger, sank under exhaustion, a meal was prepared for me in the desert that restored and encouraged me. Often, when all was dry, the heavens cloudless, and I was parched by thirst, a slight cloud would dim the sky, shed the few drops that revived me, and vanish.

In other places human beings were seldom seen, and I generally lived on the wild animals that crossed my path. My life, as it passed in this way, was truly hateful to me, and it was only during sleep that I could taste joy. O blessed sleep! Often, when I was most miserable, I sank into rest, and my dreams lulled me even to rapture. Often, when wearied by a difficult march, I persuaded myself that I was dreaming until night should come, and that I would then enjoy reality in the arms of my dearest friends. What agonizing fondness I felt for them! How I clung to their dear forms, as sometimes they haunted even my waking hours, and persuaded myself that they still lived! At such moments, the vengeance that burned within me died in my heart, and I pursued my path toward the destruction of the demon more as a task commanded by heaven, as the mechanical impulse of some power of which I was unconscious, than as the passionate desire of my soul.

My courage and perseverance were strengthened by these mocking words; I resolved not to fail in my purpose, and calling on Heaven to support me, I continued with undiminished energy to cross vast deserts, until the ocean appeared at a distance and formed the farthest boundary of the horizon. The Greeks wept for joy when they saw the Mediterranean from the hills of Asia, and greeted with delight the end of their hardships. He had escaped me, and I must begin a destructive and almost endless journey across the mountainous ice of the ocean, amid cold that few of the inhabitants could long endure and which I, a native of a warm and sunny climate, could not hope to survive. You have read this strange and terrifying story, Margaret; and do you not feel your blood congeal with horror, like that which even now curdles mine? Sometimes, seized with sudden agony, he could not continue his tale; at others, his voice broken, yet piercing, uttered with difficulty the words so full of anguish. His fine and lovely eyes were now lighted up with indignation, now subdued to downcast sorrow and quenched in infinite wretchedness. His tale is connected and told with an appearance of the simplest truth, yet I admit to you that the letters of Felix and Safie, which he showed me, and the apparition of the monster seen from our ship, brought to me a greater conviction of the truth of his narrative than his declarations, however earnest and connected. Such a monster has, then, real existence! I cannot doubt it, yet I am lost in surprise and admiration. Sometimes I tried to gain from Frankenstein the details of his creature's formation, but on this point he was impenetrable.

"Are you mad, my friend?" said he. "Or where does your senseless curiosity lead you? Would you also create for yourself and the world a demoniacal enemy? Peace, peace! Learn my miseries and do not seek to increase your own."

Frankenstein discovered that I made notes concerning his history; he asked to see them and then himself corrected and expanded them in many places, but principally in giving the life and spirit to the conversations he held with his enemy. "Since you have preserved my narration," said he, "I would not that a mutilated one should go down to posterity."

So a week has passed while I have listened to the strangest tale that imagination ever formed. My thoughts and every feeling of my soul have been absorbed by my concern for my guest, which this tale and his own noble and gentle manner have created. I wish to comfort him, yet can I advise someone so endlessly miserable, so without any hope of consolation, to live? Oh, no! The only joy he can now know will be when he settles his broken spirit into peace and death. Yet he has one comfort, born of solitude and delirium. He believes that when in dreams he speaks with his friends and receives from that communion consolation for his miseries or encouragement for his revenge, they are not creations of his imagination, but the beings themselves who visit him from regions of a distant world. This faith gives a solemnity to his daydreams that makes them almost as imposing and interesting to me as truth.

Our conversations are not always limited to his own history and misfortunes. On every point of general literature he shows unlimited knowledge and a quick and piercing understanding. His eloquence is powerful and moving; nor can I hear him, when he tells a sad incident or tries to stir the passions of pity or love, without tears. What a magnificent creature he must have been in the days of his success, when he is thus noble and godlike even in ruin! He seems to feel his own worth and the greatness of his fall.

“When I was younger,” he said, “I believed I was destined for some great enterprise. My feelings are deep, but I had a coolness of judgment that suited me for remarkable achievements. This sense of the worth of my nature supported me when others would have been crushed, for I thought it criminal to throw away in useless grief those talents that might be useful to my fellow creatures. When I reflected on the work I had completed—no less than the creation of a sensitive and rational animal—I could not rank myself with the crowd of ordinary inventors. But this thought, which supported me at the beginning of my career, now serves only to plunge me lower in the dust. All my plans and hopes are as nothing, and like the archangel who aspired to omnipotence, I am chained in an eternal hell. My imagination was vivid, yet my powers of analysis and application were intense. By the union of these qualities I conceived the idea and carried out the creation of a man. Even now I cannot remember without passion my daydreams while the work was incomplete. I walked through heaven in my thoughts, now rejoicing in my powers, now burning with the idea of their effects. From my infancy I was filled with high hopes and a lofty ambition. But how I have sunk! Oh, my friend, if you had known me as I once was, you would not recognize me in this state of degradation. Despair rarely visited my heart; a high destiny seemed to carry me forward, until I fell, never, never again to rise.”

Must I then lose this admirable being? I have longed for a friend; I have sought one who would sympathize with and love me. Look—on these empty seas I have found such a one, but I fear I have gained him only to know his value and lose him. I would reconcile him to life, but he rejects the idea.

“I thank you, Walton,” he said, “for your kind intentions towards so miserable a wretch; but when you speak of new ties and fresh affections, do you think any can replace those who are gone? Can any man be to me as Clerval was, or any woman another Elizabeth? Even where the affections are not strongly moved by any superior excellence, the companions of our childhood always possess a certain power over our minds which hardly any later friend can obtain. They know our infantine dispositions, which, however they may be afterwards modified, are never eradicated; and they can judge of our actions with more certain conclusions as to the integrity of our motives. A sister or a brother can never, unless indeed such symptoms have been shown early, suspect the other of fraud or false dealing, when another friend, however strongly he may be attached, may, in spite of himself, be contemplated with suspicion. But I enjoyed friends, dear not only through habit and association, but from their own merits; and wherever I am, the soothing voice of my Elizabeth and the conversation of Clerval will be ever whispered in my ear. They are dead, and but one feeling in such a solitude can persuade me to preserve my life. If I were engaged in any high undertaking or design, fraught with extensive utility to my fellow creatures, then could I live to fulfil it. But such is not my destiny; I must pursue and destroy the being to whom I gave existence; then my lot on earth will be fulfilled and I may die.”

My beloved Sister,

September 2d.

I write to you, encompassed by peril and ignorant whether I am ever doomed to see again dear England and the dearer friends that inhabit it. I am surrounded by mountains of ice which admit of no escape and threaten every moment to crush my vessel. The brave fellows whom I have persuaded to be my companions look towards me for aid, but I have none to bestow. There is something terribly appalling in our situation, yet my courage and hopes do not desert me. Yet it is terrible to reflect that the lives of all these men are endangered through me. If we are lost, my mad schemes are the cause.

And what, Margaret, will be the state of your mind? You will not hear of my destruction, and you will anxiously await my return. Years will pass, and you will have visitings of despair and yet be tortured by hope. Oh! My beloved sister, the sickening failing of your heart-felt expectations is, in prospect, more terrible to me than my own death. But you have a husband and lovely children; you may be happy. Heaven bless you and make you so!

My unfortunate guest regards me with the tenderest compassion. He endeavours to fill me with hope and talks as if life were a possession which he valued. He reminds me how often the same accidents have happened to other navigators who have attempted this sea, and in spite of myself, he fills me with cheerful auguries. Even the sailors feel the power of his eloquence; when he speaks, they no longer despair; he rouses their energies, and while they hear his voice they believe these vast mountains of ice are mole-hills which will vanish before the resolutions of man. These feelings are transitory; each day of expectation delayed fills them with fear, and I almost dread a mutiny caused by this despair.

September 5th.

A scene has just passed of such uncommon interest that, although it is highly probable that these papers may never reach you, yet I cannot forbear recording it.

We are still surrounded by mountains of ice, still in constant danger of being crushed if they collide. The cold is extreme, and many of my unfortunate companions have already found a grave in this desolate place. Frankenstein has grown weaker every day; a feverish fire still glimmers in his eyes, but he is exhausted, and whenever he is suddenly called to make any effort, he quickly sinks back into what looks like lifelessness.

In my last letter I mentioned my fears of a mutiny. This morning, as I sat watching the pale face of my friend—his eyes half closed and his limbs hanging limply—I was startled by half a dozen of the sailors, who demanded to come into the cabin. They entered, and their leader spoke to me. He told me that he and his companions had been chosen by the other sailors to come to me as representatives and make a demand which, in fairness, I could not refuse. We were trapped in ice and would probably never escape, but they feared that if the ice should break up and a free passage open, I might be reckless enough to continue the voyage and lead them into new dangers, even after we had happily overcome this one. They insisted, therefore, that I make a solemn promise that if the ship were freed I would immediately turn our course southward.

This speech troubled me. I had not given up hope, nor had I yet considered the idea of returning if we were set free. Yet could I, in fairness, or even practically, refuse this demand? I hesitated before answering, when Frankenstein, who had at first been silent and indeed seemed hardly to have enough strength to pay attention, now roused himself; his eyes sparkled, and his cheeks flushed with a brief burst of energy. Turning toward the men, he said,

“What do you mean? What do you demand of your captain? Are you so easily turned from your purpose? Did you not call this a glorious expedition? And why was it glorious? Not because the way was smooth and calm as a southern sea, but because it was full of dangers and terror, because at every new challenge your courage was to be tested and shown, because danger and death surrounded it, and these you were to face and overcome. For this it was a glorious, for this it was an honorable undertaking. You were to be hailed as the benefactors of your species, your names honored as belonging to brave men who faced death for honor and the benefit of mankind. And now, look—at the first hint of danger, or, if you prefer, the first great and terrifying test of your courage, you shrink away and are content to be remembered as men who did not have the strength to endure cold and peril; and so, poor souls, they were chilly and returned to their warm firesides. Why, that requires no such preparation; you need not have come this far and dragged your captain to the shame of defeat merely to prove yourselves cowards. Oh! Be men, or be more than men. Be steady in your purpose and firm as a rock. This ice is not made of the same stuff as your hearts may be; it is changeable and cannot withstand you if you decide that it shall not. Do not return to your families with the mark of disgrace on your brows. Return as heroes who have fought and conquered and who do not know what it is to turn their backs on the enemy.”

He spoke with a voice so perfectly matched to the different feelings in his words, and with a look so full of noble purpose and heroism, that can you wonder these men were moved? They looked at one another and could not reply. I spoke. I told them to withdraw and think over what had been said. I said I would not lead them farther north if they strongly wished the opposite, but I hoped that, after reflection, their courage would return.

They withdrew, and I turned to my friend, but he was sunk in exhaustion and almost lifeless.

How all this will end, I do not know, but I would rather die than return in shame with my purpose unfulfilled. Yet I fear that will be my fate. The men, unsupported by ideas of glory and honour, can never willingly continue to endure their present hardships.

September 7th.

The die is cast. I have agreed to return if we are not destroyed. So my hopes are blasted by cowardice and indecision. I come back ignorant and disappointed. It requires more philosophy than I possess to bear this injustice with patience.

September 12th.

It is over. I am returning to England. I have lost my hopes of usefulness and glory. I have lost my friend. But I will try to describe these bitter circumstances to you, my dear sister. And while I am carried toward England and toward you, I will not despair.

On September 9th, the ice began to move, and roarings like thunder were heard at a distance as the islands split and cracked in every direction. We were in the most immediate peril, but since we could only remain passive, my main attention was occupied by my unfortunate guest, whose illness had increased so much that he was entirely confined to his bed. The ice cracked behind us and was driven with force toward the north. A breeze sprang from the west, and on the 11th the passage toward the south became perfectly clear. When the sailors saw this, and saw that their return to their native country was apparently assured, a shout of wild joy broke from them, loud and long-continued. Frankenstein, who was dozing, awoke and asked the cause of the uproar. “They shout,” I said, “because they will soon return to England.”

“Do you, then, really return?”

“Alas, yes. I cannot withstand their demands. I cannot lead them unwillingly into danger, and I must return.”

“Do so, if you will, but I will not. You may give up your purpose, but mine is assigned to me by Heaven, and I dare not. I am weak, but surely the spirits who assist my vengeance will give me sufficient strength.” Saying this, he tried to spring from the bed, but the effort was too great for him. He fell back and fainted.

It was long before he recovered, and I often thought that life was entirely gone. At length he opened his eyes. He breathed with difficulty and was unable to speak. The surgeon gave him a calming drink and ordered us to leave him undisturbed. In the meantime, he told me that my friend certainly had not many hours to live.

His sentence was pronounced, and I could only grieve and be patient. I sat by his bed, watching him; his eyes were closed, and I thought he slept; but presently he called to me in a feeble voice, and bidding me come near, said, “Alas! The strength I relied on is gone; I feel that I shall soon die, and he, my enemy and persecutor, may still be alive. Do not think, Walton, that in the last moments of my life I feel that burning hatred and fierce desire for revenge I once expressed; but I do feel justified in desiring the death of my adversary. During these last days I have been examining my past conduct, and I do not find it blameworthy. In a fit of passionate madness I created a rational creature, and I was bound to ensure his happiness and well-being as far as I could. This was my duty, but there was another still more important. My duties toward the beings of my own species had greater claims to my attention, because they involved a greater amount of happiness or misery. Driven by this view, I refused—and I did right in refusing—to create a companion for the first creature. He showed unmatched evil and selfishness; he destroyed my friends; he gave over to destruction beings who possessed exquisite feelings, happiness, and wisdom; and I do not know where this thirst for vengeance may end. Miserable himself, so that he may make no one else wretched, he ought to die. The task of destroying him was mine, but I have failed. When I was driven by selfish and vicious motives, I asked you to take up my unfinished work, and I renew that request now, when I am moved only by reason and virtue.

“Yet I cannot ask you to give up your country and friends to carry out this task; and now that you are returning to England, you will have little chance of meeting him. But I leave to you the consideration of these points, and the careful weighing of what you may consider your duties; my judgment and ideas are already disturbed by the near approach of death. I dare not ask you to do what I think right, for I may still be misled by passion.

“That he should live to be an instrument of mischief disturbs me; in other respects, this hour, when I expect my release at any moment, is the only happy one I have enjoyed for several years. The forms of my beloved dead drift before me, and I hurry to their arms. Farewell, Walton! Seek happiness in tranquillity and avoid ambition, even if it is only the apparently innocent one of distinguishing yourself in science and discoveries. Yet why do I say this? I myself have been destroyed by these hopes, yet another may succeed.”

His voice became fainter as he spoke, and at last, exhausted by his effort, he sank into silence. About half an hour later he tried to speak again but could not; he pressed my hand feebly, and his eyes closed forever, while the glow of a gentle smile faded from his lips.

Margaret, what comment can I make on the untimely death of this glorious spirit? What can I say that will help you understand the depth of my sorrow? Everything I could express would be inadequate and weak. My tears flow; my mind is overshadowed by a cloud of disappointment. But I am journeying toward England, and I may find consolation there.

I am interrupted. What do these sounds mean? It is midnight; the breeze blows steadily, and the watch on deck barely stirs. Again there is a sound like a human voice, but rougher; it comes from the cabin where the remains of Frankenstein still lie. I must get up and look. Good night, my sister.

Great God! What a scene has just taken place! I am still dizzy remembering it. I hardly know whether I will be able to describe it; yet the story I have recorded would be incomplete without this final and astonishing catastrophe.

I entered the cabin where the remains of my unfortunate and admirable friend lay. Over him hung a form which I cannot find words to describe—gigantic in size, yet clumsy and distorted in its proportions. As he hung over the coffin, his face was hidden by long locks of tangled hair; but one huge hand was extended, in color and apparent texture like that of a mummy. When he heard the sound of my approach, he stopped uttering cries of grief and horror and sprang toward the window. Never have I seen a vision so horrible as his face, of such loathsome yet appalling hideousness. I shut my eyes involuntarily and tried to remember what my duties were regarding this destroyer. I called on him to stay.

He paused, looking at me with wonder, and again turning toward the lifeless form of his creator, he seemed to forget my presence. Every feature and gesture seemed driven by the wildest rage of some uncontrollable passion.

"That is also my victim!" he exclaimed. "In his murder my crimes are completed; the miserable series of my existence is wound to its close! Oh, Frankenstein! Generous and self-sacrificing being! What good does it do that I now ask you to pardon me? I, who destroyed you beyond recovery by destroying all you loved. Alas! He is cold, he cannot answer me."

His voice seemed choked, and my first impulses, which had suggested to me the duty of obeying the dying request of my friend in destroying his enemy, were now suspended by a mixture of curiosity and compassion. I approached this tremendous being; I dared not again raise my eyes to his face, there was something so frightening and unearthly in his ugliness. I attempted to speak, but the words died away on my lips. The monster continued to utter wild and incoherent self-reproaches. At length I gathered the resolve to address him in a pause of the storm of his passion.

"Your repentance," I said, "is now pointless. If you had listened to the voice of conscience and heeded the stings of remorse before you had driven your devilish vengeance to this extreme, Frankenstein would still be alive."

"And do you dream?" said the demon. "Do you think that I was then dead to agony and remorse? He," he continued, pointing to the corpse, "he did not suffer in the completion of the deed. Oh! Not the ten-thousandth part of the anguish that was mine during the lingering detail of its execution. A frightful selfishness hurried me on, while my heart was poisoned with remorse. Do you think that the groans of Clerval were music to my ears? My heart was made to be capable of love and sympathy, and when wrenched by misery into vice and hatred, it did not endure the violence of the change without torture such as you cannot even imagine."

After the murder of Clerval I returned to Switzerland, heartbroken and overcome. I pitied Frankenstein; my pity amounted to horror; I hated myself. But when I discovered that he—the author both of my existence and of its unspeakable torments—dared to hope for happiness, that while he piled wretchedness and despair upon me he sought his own enjoyment in feelings and passions from which I was forever barred, then helpless envy and bitter indignation filled me with an insatiable thirst for vengeance. I remembered my threat and resolved to carry it out. I knew I was preparing a deadly torture for myself, but I was the slave, not the master, of an impulse I detested yet could not disobey. Yet when she died—no, then I was not miserable. I had cast off all feeling, subdued all anguish, to revel in the excess of my despair. Evil from then on became my good. Driven this far, I had no choice but to adapt my nature to an element I had willingly chosen. The completion of my demonic design became an insatiable passion. And now it is ended; there is my last victim!

I was at first touched by the expressions of his misery; yet when I remembered what Frankenstein had said of his powers of eloquence and persuasion, and when I again looked at the lifeless form of my friend, indignation was rekindled within me. “Wretch!” I said. “It is fitting that you come here to whine over the desolation you have caused. You throw a torch into a pile of buildings, and when they are consumed, you sit among the ruins and lament the collapse. Hypocritical fiend! If the one you mourn still lived, he would still be the object—again he would become the prey—of your accursed vengeance. It is not pity that you feel; you lament only because the victim of your malice has been removed from your power.”

“Oh, it is not so—not so,” interrupted the being. “Yet such must be the impression conveyed to you by what appears to be the purpose of my actions. Still, I do not seek fellow feeling in my misery. No sympathy may I ever find. When I first sought it, it was the love of virtue, the feelings of happiness and affection with which my whole being overflowed, that I wished to share. But now that virtue has become a shadow to me, and happiness and affection are turned into bitter and loathing despair, in what should I seek sympathy? I am content to suffer alone while my sufferings last; when I die, I am well satisfied that abhorrence and disgrace should burden my memory. Once my imagination was soothed with dreams of virtue, of fame, and of enjoyment. Once I falsely hoped to meet with beings who, pardoning my outward form, would love me for the excellent qualities I was capable of revealing. I was nourished with high thoughts of honor and devotion. But now crime has degraded me beneath the meanest animal. No guilt, no mischief, no malice, no misery can be found comparable to mine. When I run over the frightful catalogue of my sins, I cannot believe that I am the same creature whose thoughts were once filled with sublime and transcendent visions of the beauty and majesty of goodness. But it is even so; the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil. Yet even that enemy of God and man had friends and associates in his desolation; I am alone.”

You, who call Frankenstein your friend, seem to know about my crimes and his misfortunes. But in the account he gave you, he could not express the hours and months of misery I endured, wasting away in helpless passions. For while I destroyed his hopes, I did not satisfy my own desires. They were forever burning and craving. I still wanted love and companionship, and I was still rejected. Was there no injustice in this? Am I to be thought the only criminal, when all humankind sinned against me? Why do you not hate Felix, who drove his friend from his door with contempt? Why do you not curse the rustic who tried to destroy the savior of his child? No, these are virtuous and pure beings! I, the miserable and the abandoned, am a monster, to be rejected, kicked, and trampled on. Even now my blood boils at the memory of this injustice.

But it is true that I am a wretch. I have murdered the lovely and the helpless. I have strangled the innocent as they slept and choked to death the man who never injured me or any other living thing. I have doomed my creator, the finest example of all that is worthy of love and admiration among men, to misery. I have pursued him even to that final ruin. There he lies, white and cold in death. You hate me, but your hatred cannot equal the hatred I feel for myself. I look at the hands that carried out the deed. I think of the heart in which the idea of it was born, and I long for the moment when these hands will meet my eyes, when that idea will haunt my thoughts no more.

Do not fear that I will be the cause of future harm. My work is nearly complete. Neither your death nor any man's death is needed to finish the course of my existence and accomplish what must be done; it requires only my own. Do not think that I will hesitate to make this sacrifice. I will leave your ship on the ice raft that brought me here and seek the northernmost point of the globe. I will build my funeral pyre and burn this miserable body to ashes, so that its remains may give no help to any curious and unholy wretch who would create another being like I have been. I will die. I will no longer feel the agonies that now consume me or be the victim of feelings unsatisfied yet unquenched. He who called me into being is dead; and when I am no more, the very memory of us both will quickly vanish. I will no longer see the sun or stars or feel the winds play on my cheeks. Light, feeling, and sense will pass away; and in this condition I must find my happiness. Some years ago, when the images this world offers first opened before me, when I felt the cheering warmth of summer and heard the rustling of the leaves and the singing of the birds, and these were everything to me, I would have wept to die. Now it is my only comfort. Polluted by crimes and torn by the bitterest remorse, where can I find rest but in death?

“Farewell! I leave you, and in you the last of humankind whom these eyes will ever see. Farewell, Frankenstein! If you were still alive and still wanted revenge against me, it would be better satisfied by my life than by my death. But that was not the case; you sought my destruction so that I might not cause greater misery. And if, in some way unknown to me, you had not stopped thinking and feeling, you would not wish on me a greater punishment than the one I already feel. Ruined as you were, my agony was still worse than yours, for the bitter sting of remorse will not stop burning in my wounds until death closes them forever.

“But soon,” he cried with sad and solemn passion, “I shall die, and what I now feel will no longer be felt. Soon these burning miseries will be gone. I shall climb onto my funeral pyre triumphantly and rejoice in the agony of the torturing flames. The light of that fire will fade away; my ashes will be swept into the sea by the winds. My spirit will sleep in peace, or if it thinks, it will surely not think like this. Farewell.”

As he said this, he sprang from the cabin window onto the ice raft that lay close to the ship. He was soon carried away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance.
