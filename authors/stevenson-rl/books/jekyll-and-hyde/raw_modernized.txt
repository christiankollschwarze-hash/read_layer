###TITLE### The Strange Case of Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde
###AUTHOR### Robert Louis Stevenson

###CHAPTER### Story of the Door

Mr. Utterson the lawyer had a rugged face that was never lit by a smile. He was cold, hesitant and awkward in conversation; reserved in feeling; lean, tall, dusty, dreary, and yet somehow lovable. At friendly meetings, when the wine was to his taste, something deeply human shone from his eye. This something never found its way into his talk, but it spoke not only in these silent signals of his after-dinner face, but more often and loudly in the acts of his life. He was strict with himself. He drank gin alone to suppress a taste for fine wines. And though he enjoyed the theatre, he had not set foot in one for twenty years. But he had a genuine tolerance for others. Sometimes he wondered, almost with envy, at the high spirits behind their misdeeds. In any extreme, he was inclined to help rather than to scold. "I incline to Cain's heresy," he used to say in his odd way: "I let my brother go to the devil in his own way." In this character, it was often his lot to be the last respectable friend and the last good influence in the lives of men on their way down. And to such men, as long as they came to his rooms, he never showed a shade of change in his manner.

No doubt this feat was easy for Mr. Utterson; for he was undemonstrative at the best of times, and even his friendship seemed based on a similar broad good nature. A modest man accepts his circle of friends ready-made from the hands of opportunity; and that was the lawyer’s way. His friends were his own blood relations or those he had known the longest. His affections, like ivy, were the growth of time, implying no particular fitness in the object. Hence, no doubt, the bond that united him to Mr. Richard Enfield, his distant kinsman, the well-known man about town. Many found it a puzzle what these two could see in each other, or what subject they could share. It was said by those who met them on their Sunday walks that they said nothing, looked singularly dull, and hailed with obvious relief the appearance of a friend. For all that, the two men put the greatest store by these excursions, counted them the chief jewel of each week, and not only set aside pleasures, but even resisted business calls, that they might enjoy them uninterrupted.

It happened on one of these walks that their way led them down a side street in a busy quarter of London. The street was small and what is called quiet, but it carried on a thriving trade on weekdays. All the inhabitants seemed to be doing well, and each was eagerly hoping to do even better, spending their extra gains on showy decoration. So the shop fronts along the street had an inviting air, like rows of smiling saleswomen. Even on Sunday, when it hid its more showy charms and lay nearly empty of passers-by, the street shone out in contrast to its dingy neighbourhood, like a fire in a forest. With its freshly painted shutters, well-polished brass work, and general cleanliness and bright cheerfulness, it instantly caught and pleased the eye of the passer-by.

Two doors from the corner, on the left-hand side going east, the row of buildings was broken by a courtyard entrance. Just at that point, a sinister building thrust its gable forward onto the street. It was two storeys high and had no window—nothing but a door on the lower storey and a blank, discoloured forehead of wall on the upper. Every feature showed the marks of long, sordid neglect. The door had neither bell nor knocker and was blistered and stained. Tramps slouched into the recess and struck matches on the panels; children played shop on the steps; a schoolboy had tried his knife on the mouldings. For nearly a generation, no one had appeared to drive away these casual visitors or repair the damage.

Mr. Enfield and the lawyer were on the other side of the side street. When they came opposite the entry, Mr. Enfield lifted his cane and pointed.

“Did you ever notice that door?” he asked. When his companion said yes, he added, “It is connected in my mind with a very odd story.”

“Indeed?” said Mr. Utterson, with a slight change of voice. “And what was that?”

“Well, it was this way,” returned Mr. Enfield: “I was coming home from somewhere at the end of the world, about three o’clock on a black winter morning, and I had to go through a part of town where you literally saw nothing but streetlamps. Street after street, everyone asleep—street after street, all lit up as if for a procession and all as empty as a church—until at last I got into that state of mind where you listen and listen and begin to long for the sight of a policeman. Suddenly, I saw two figures: one a little man who was stumping along eastward at a brisk walk, and the other a girl of maybe eight or ten who was running as fast as she could down a cross street. Well, sir, the two naturally collided at the corner; and then came the horrible part of the thing: the man calmly trampled over the child’s body and left her screaming on the ground. It sounds like nothing to hear about, but it was hellish to see. It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut. I gave a shout, took to my heels, collared my gentleman, and dragged him back to where a small crowd had already gathered around the screaming child. He was perfectly cool and made no resistance, but gave me one look so ugly that it made the sweat pour out of me as if I’d been running. The people who had come out were the girl’s own family; and soon enough, the doctor they had sent for appeared. Well, the child was not much the worse— more frightened, according to the sawbones; and you might have supposed that would be the end of it. But there was one curious circumstance. I had taken an instant loathing to my gentleman. So had the child’s family, which was only natural. But the doctor’s case was what struck me. He was the usual cut-and-dried apothecary, of no particular age or colouring, with a strong Edinburgh accent and about as emotional as a bagpipe. Well, sir, he was like the rest of us; every time he looked at my prisoner, I saw that sawbones turn sick and white with the desire to kill him. I knew what was in his mind, just as he knew what was in mine; and killing being out of the question, we did the next best thing. We told the man we could and would make such a scandal out of this that his name would stink from one end of London to the other. If he had any friends or any credit, we promised he would lose them. And all the while, as we were pitching it in red hot, we were keeping the women off him as best we could, for they were as wild as harpies. I never saw a circle of such hateful faces; and there was the man in the middle, with a kind of black sneering coolness—frightened too, I could see that—but carrying it off, sir, really like Satan. ‘If you choose to make capital out of this accident,’ said he, ‘I am naturally helpless. No gentleman wishes to cause a scene,’ says he. ‘Name your figure.’ Well, we screwed him up to a hundred pounds for the child’s family; he would clearly have liked to hold out, but there was something about the lot of us that meant mischief, and at last he gave in. The next thing was to get the money; and where do you think he took us but to that place with the door?—whipped out a key, went in, and presently came back with about ten pounds in gold and a cheque for the balance on Coutts’s, drawn payable to bearer and signed with a name that I can’t mention, though it’s one of the points of my story, but it was a name at least very well known and often printed. The figure was stiff; but the signature was good for more than that if it was only genuine. I took the liberty of pointing out to my gentleman that the whole business looked apocryphal, and that no man does, in real life, walk into a cellar door at four in the morning and come out with another man’s cheque for close upon a hundred pounds. But he was quite easy and sneering. ‘Set your mind at rest,’ says he, ‘I will stay with you till the banks open and cash the cheque myself.’ So we all set off—the doctor, the child’s father, our friend, and myself—and passed the rest of the night in my chambers; and next day, when we had breakfasted, went in a body to the bank. I handed in the cheque myself and said I had every reason to believe it was a forgery. Not a bit of it. The cheque was genuine.”

“Tut-tut!” said Mr. Utterson.

“I see you feel as I do,” said Mr. Enfield. “Yes, it’s a bad story. My man was a fellow nobody could have anything to do with—a really damnable man. The person who drew the cheque is the very model of respectability, celebrated too, and (what makes it worse) one of your fellows who do what they call good. Blackmail, I suppose; an honest man paying through the nose for some of the escapades of his youth. That’s why I call the place with the door Black Mail House. Though even that, you know, is far from explaining everything,” he added, and with those words he fell into a thoughtful silence.

He was pulled out of this by Mr. Utterson asking rather suddenly: “And you don’t know if the person who signed the cheque lives there?”

“A likely place, isn’t it?” returned Mr. Enfield. “But I happen to have noticed his address; he lives in some square or other.”

“And you never asked about the—place with the door?” said Mr. Utterson.

“No, sir; I felt it would be indelicate,” was the reply. “I feel very strongly about asking questions; it feels too much like the day of judgment. You start a question, and it’s like starting a stone. You sit quietly on top of a hill; the stone goes, starting others; and before you know it, some bland old bird (the last you’d have thought of) is hit on the head in his own back garden and the family have to change their name. No, sir, I make it a rule: the more it looks like Queer Street, the less I ask.”

“A very good rule, too,” said the lawyer.

“But I have studied the place for myself,” continued Mr. Enfield. “It seems scarcely a house. There is no other door, and nobody goes in or out of that one except, once in a great while, the gentleman from my adventure. On the first floor, there are three windows looking onto the court; none below. The windows are always shut, but they’re clean. And there’s a chimney that is usually smoking, so somebody must live there. And yet it’s not certain, because the buildings are so packed together around the court that it’s hard to say where one ends and another begins.”

The pair walked on again for a while in silence; and then “Enfield,” said Mr. Utterson, “that’s a good rule of yours.”

“Yes, I think it is,” returned Enfield.

“But for all that,” continued the lawyer, “there’s one point I want to ask. I want to ask the name of the man who walked over the child.”

“Well,” said Mr. Enfield, “I can’t see what harm it would do. It was a man by the name of Hyde.”

“Hm,” said Mr. Utterson. “What sort of a man is he, to look at?”

“He is not easy to describe. There is something wrong with his appearance; something displeasing, something downright detestable. I never saw a man I so disliked, and yet I scarcely know why. He must be deformed somewhere; he gives a strong feeling of deformity, although I couldn’t specify the point. He’s an extraordinary-looking man, and yet I really can name nothing odd about him. No, sir; I can make nothing of it; I can’t describe him. And it’s not for lack of memory; I declare I can see him this very moment.”

Mr. Utterson again walked some way in silence and obviously deep in thought. “You are sure he used a key?” he inquired at last.

“My dear sir...” began Enfield, startled out of his composure.

“Yes, I know,” said Utterson. “I know it must seem strange. The fact is, if I don’t ask you the name of the other party, it’s because I already know it. You see, Richard, your story has hit home. If you’ve been inaccurate about anything, you’d better correct it.”

“I think you might have warned me,” the other replied with a touch of sullenness. “But I have been meticulously exact, as you call it. The fellow had a key; and what’s more, he still has it. I saw him use it not even a week ago.”

Mr. Utterson sighed deeply but said nothing; and the young man soon continued. “Here is another lesson in saying nothing,” he said. “I’m ashamed of my loose tongue. Let’s agree never to mention this again.”

“With all my heart,” said the lawyer. “Let’s shake on that, Richard.”

###CHAPTER### Search for Mr. Hyde

That evening Mr. Utterson came home to his bachelor house in low spirits and sat down to dinner without appetite. It was his Sunday custom, once the meal was over, to sit close by the fire with a volume of some dull theological work on his reading desk, until the clock of the neighbouring church struck twelve. Then he would go soberly and gratefully to bed. On this night, however, as soon as the cloth was taken away, he picked up a candle and went into his business room. There he opened his safe, took from its most private part a document labeled on the envelope as Dr. Jekyll’s Will, and sat down with a clouded brow to study its contents. The will was in Jekyll’s own handwriting, for Mr. Utterson, though he took charge of it now that it was made, had refused to give the slightest help in the making of it. It provided not only that, in case of the death of Henry Jekyll, M.D., D.C.L., L.L.D., F.R.S., etc., all his possessions were to pass into the hands of his “friend and benefactor Edward Hyde,” but that in case of Dr. Jekyll’s “disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months,” the said Edward Hyde should step into the said Henry Jekyll’s shoes without further delay and free from any burden or obligation beyond the payment of a few small sums to the members of the doctor’s household. This document had long been an eyesore to the lawyer. It offended him both as a lawyer and as a lover of the sane and customary sides of life, to whom the fanciful was unseemly. And until now it was his ignorance of Mr. Hyde that had swelled his indignation; now, by a sudden turn, it was his knowledge. It was already bad enough when the name was only a name of which he could learn no more. It was worse when it began to be clothed with detestable traits; and out of the shifting, insubstantial mists that had so long baffled his eye, there leaped up the sudden, definite image of a devil.

“I thought it was madness,” he said, as he replaced the hateful paper in the safe, “and now I’m beginning to fear it’s a disgrace.”

With that he blew out his candle, put on an overcoat, and set off in the direction of Cavendish Square, that citadel of medicine, where his friend, the great Dr. Lanyon, had his house and received his many patients. “If anyone knows, it will be Lanyon,” he had thought.

The grave butler knew and welcomed him; he was subjected to no delay, but ushered directly from the door to the dining-room where Dr. Lanyon sat alone over his wine. This was a hearty, healthy, neat, red-faced gentleman, with a thick head of hair prematurely white, and a boisterous, decided manner. At sight of Mr. Utterson, he sprang up from his chair and welcomed him with both hands. The warmth, as was the man’s way, was somewhat theatrical to the eye; but it rested on genuine feeling. For these two were old friends, old mates both at school and college, both men who thoroughly respected themselves and each other, and—what does not always follow—men who thoroughly enjoyed each other’s company.

After a little aimless talk, the lawyer brought up the subject that so unpleasantly preoccupied his mind.

“I suppose, Lanyon,” said he, “you and I must be the two oldest friends that Henry Jekyll has?”

“I wish the friends were younger,” chuckled Dr. Lanyon. “But I suppose we are. And what of that? I see little of him now.”

“Indeed?” said Utterson. “I thought you had a bond of common interest.”

“We were,” was the reply. “But it is more than ten years since Henry Jekyll became too fanciful for me. He began to go wrong—wrong in mind; and though, of course, I still take an interest in him for old times’ sake, as they say, I see and have seen devilish little of the man. Such unscientific nonsense,” added the doctor, his face suddenly flushing purple, “would have estranged even Damon and Pythias.”

This little flash of temper was something of a relief to Mr. Utterson. “They have only had a difference of opinion on some scientific point,” he thought; and being a man of no scientific passions (except in the matter of conveyancing), he even added: “It is nothing worse than that!” He gave his friend a few seconds to recover his composure, and then approached the question he had come to put. “Did you ever come across a protégé of his—a man named Hyde?” he asked.

“Hyde?” repeated Lanyon. “No. Never heard of him. Not since my time.”

That was all the information the lawyer carried back with him to his large, dark bed, on which he tossed to and fro until the small hours of the morning began to grow large. It was a night of little ease to his toiling mind, toiling in sheer darkness and besieged by questions.

Six o’clock struck on the bells of the church so conveniently near Mr. Utterson’s dwelling, and still he was digging at the problem. Until then, it had touched him only on the intellectual side; but now his imagination was engaged—or rather, enslaved. As he lay and tossed in the thick darkness of the night and the curtained room, Mr. Enfield’s tale went before his mind in a scroll of lighted pictures. He would be aware of the great field of lamps of a nocturnal city; then of the figure of a man walking swiftly; then of a child running from the doctor’s surgery; and then these met, and that human Juggernaut trod the child down and passed on regardless of her screams. Or else he would see a room in a rich house, where his friend lay asleep, dreaming and smiling at his dreams; and then the door of that room would be opened, the bed-curtains pulled apart, the sleeper awakened, and—look!—there would stand by his side a figure to whom power was given, and even at that dead hour, he must rise and do its bidding. The figure in these two forms haunted the lawyer all night; and if at any time he dozed off, it was only to see it glide more stealthily through sleeping houses, or move ever more swiftly, even to dizziness, through wider labyrinths of lamp-lit city, and at every street corner crush a child and leave her screaming. And still the figure had no face by which he might know it; even in his dreams, it had no face, or one that baffled him and melted before his eyes. And so there sprang up and grew rapidly in the lawyer’s mind a singularly strong, almost an inordinate, curiosity to behold the features of the real Mr. Hyde. If he could but once set eyes on him, he thought the mystery would lighten and perhaps roll entirely away, as was the habit of mysterious things when well examined. He might see a reason for his friend’s strange preference or bondage (call it which you please) and even for the startling clauses of the will. At least it would be a face worth seeing: the face of a man without a shred of mercy; a face which had but to show itself to raise up, in the mind of the unimpressionable Enfield, a spirit of enduring hatred.

From that time on, Mr. Utterson began to haunt the door in the side street of shops. He was there in the morning before office hours, at noon when business was heavy and time was tight, and at night under the foggy city moon. By every light and at all hours, whether the street was empty or crowded, the lawyer could be found at his chosen post.

“If he is Mr. Hyde,” he had thought, “I shall be Mr. Seek.”

And at last his patience was rewarded. It was a fine, dry night, with frost in the air. The streets were as clean as a ballroom floor. The lamps, unshaken by any wind, drew a regular pattern of light and shadow. By ten o’clock, when the shops were closed, the side street was very empty. Despite the low growl of London all around, it was very silent. Small sounds carried far. Sounds from inside the houses were clearly audible on both sides of the road. The murmur of an approaching passer-by could be heard long before he arrived. Mr. Utterson had been at his post for some minutes when he became aware of a strange light footstep drawing near. During his nightly patrols, he had long grown used to the curious way the footsteps of a single person, while still far off, suddenly spring out sharply from the vast hum and clatter of the city. Yet his attention had never been so sharply and decisively caught before. It was with a strong, superstitious sense of success that he withdrew into the entry of the court.

The steps drew swiftly nearer and swelled suddenly louder as they turned the corner at the end of the street. The lawyer, peering out from the entry, could soon see what kind of man he was dealing with. He was small and very plainly dressed. Even at that distance, the look of him went strongly against the watcher’s instincts. But the man made straight for the door, crossing the road to save time. As he came, he drew a key from his pocket like someone approaching home.

Mr. Utterson stepped out and touched him on the shoulder as he passed. “Mr. Hyde, I think?”

Mr. Hyde shrank back with a hissing intake of breath. But his fear was only momentary; and though he did not look the lawyer in the face, he answered coolly enough: “That is my name. What do you want?”

“I see you are going in,” the lawyer replied. “I am an old friend of Dr. Jekyll’s—Mr. Utterson of Gaunt Street. You must have heard my name. Since I’ve met you so conveniently, I thought you might let me in.”

“You will not find Dr. Jekyll; he is not at home,” replied Mr. Hyde, blowing in the key. And then suddenly, but still without looking up, he asked, “How did you know me?”

“On your side,” said Mr. Utterson, “will you do me a favour?”

“With pleasure,” replied the other. “What shall it be?”

“Will you let me see your face?” asked the lawyer.

Mr. Hyde appeared to hesitate. Then, as if on a sudden thought, he turned around with an air of defiance. The pair stared at each other fixedly for a few seconds. “Now I shall know you again,” said Mr. Utterson. “It may be useful.”

“Yes,” returned Mr. Hyde, “it is as well we have met; and by the way, you should have my address.” And he gave a number of a street in Soho.

“Good God!” thought Mr. Utterson, “can he, too, have been thinking of the will?” But he kept his feelings to himself and only grunted in acknowledgment of the address.

“And now,” said the other, “how did you know me?”

“By description,” was the reply.

“Whose description?”

“We have common friends,” said Mr. Utterson.

“Common friends,” echoed Mr. Hyde, a little hoarsely. “Who are they?”

“Jekyll, for instance,” said the lawyer.

“He never told you,” cried Mr. Hyde, with a flush of anger. “I didn’t think you would lie.”

“Come,” said Mr. Utterson, “that is not fitting language.”

The other snarled, and the sound twisted into a savage laugh; the next moment, with extraordinary quickness, he had unlocked the door and disappeared into the house.

The lawyer stood a while after Mr. Hyde had left him, the picture of unease. Then he began to walk slowly up the street, pausing every few steps and putting his hand to his forehead like a man in mental confusion. The problem he was turning over as he walked was one of a kind that is rarely solved. Mr. Hyde was pale and dwarfish; he gave an impression of deformity without any specific malformation; he had an unpleasant smile; he had behaved towards the lawyer with a murderous blend of timidity and boldness; and he spoke with a husky, whispering, and somewhat broken voice. All these counted against him, but even all together they could not explain the disgust, loathing, and fear—feelings utterly new to him—with which Mr. Utterson regarded him. “There must be something else,” said the perplexed gentleman. “There is something more, if I could only name it. God bless me, the man hardly seems human! Something troglodytic, shall we say? Or can it be the old story of Dr. Fell? Or is it simply the radiance of a foul soul that shines through, and transfigures, its clay vessel? The last, I think; for, oh my poor old Harry Jekyll, if ever I read Satan’s signature upon a face, it is on that of your new friend.”

Around the corner from the side street, there was a square of ancient, handsome houses. For the most part, they had fallen from their high estate and were let as flats and chambers to all sorts and conditions of men—map-engravers, architects, shady lawyers, and the agents of obscure enterprises. One house, however, the second from the corner, was still occupied entirely. At its door, which wore a great air of wealth and comfort though now plunged in darkness except for the fanlight, Mr. Utterson stopped and knocked. A well-dressed, elderly servant opened the door.

“Is Dr. Jekyll at home, Poole?” asked the lawyer.

“I will see, Mr. Utterson,” said Poole, admitting the visitor as he spoke into a large, low-roofed, comfortable hall. It was paved with flagstones, warmed after the fashion of a country house by a bright, open fire, and furnished with costly cabinets of oak. “Will you wait here by the fire, sir? Or shall I give you a light in the dining-room?”

“Here, thank you,” said the lawyer, and he drew near and leaned on the tall fender. This hall, in which he was now left alone, was a pet favourite of his friend the doctor’s; and Utterson himself used to speak of it as the pleasantest room in London. But tonight there was a shudder in his blood; the face of Hyde sat heavy on his memory; he felt (what was rare for him) a nausea and distaste for life; and in the gloom of his spirits, he seemed to read a menace in the flickering of the firelight on the polished cabinets and the uneasy shifting of the shadow on the ceiling. He was ashamed of his relief when Poole presently returned to announce that Dr. Jekyll had gone out.

“I saw Mr. Hyde go in by the old dissecting room door, Poole,” he said. “Is that right, when Dr. Jekyll is out?”

“Quite right, Mr. Utterson, sir,” replied the servant. “Mr. Hyde has a key.”

“Your master seems to place a great deal of trust in that young man, Poole,” Utterson went on, thoughtfully.

“Yes, sir, he does indeed,” said Poole. “We all have orders to obey him.”

“I don’t think I‘ve ever met Mr. Hyde?” Utterson asked.

“Oh, dear no, sir. He never dines here,” replied the butler. “Indeed, we see very little of him on this side of the house. He mostly comes and goes by the laboratory.”

“Well, good-night, Poole.”

“Good-night, Mr. Utterson.”

And the lawyer set out for home with a very heavy heart. “Poor Harry Jekyll,” he thought, “I fear he is in deep trouble! He was wild when he was young—a long while ago, to be sure—but in the law of God, there is no statute of limitations. Ah, it must be that: the ghost of some old sin, the cancer of some concealed disgrace. Punishment comes, pede claudo, years after memory has forgotten and self-love has condoned the fault.” And the lawyer, scared by the thought, brooded awhile on his own past. He groped in all the corners of memory, afraid that some jack-in-the-box of an old iniquity might leap to light there. His past was fairly blameless. Few men could read the rolls of their life with less apprehension. Yet he was humbled to the dust by the many wrongs he had done. And he was raised up again into a sober and fearful gratitude by the many things he had come so near to doing, yet avoided. And then, turning back to his former subject, he conceived a spark of hope. “This Master Hyde, if he were studied,” he thought, “must have secrets of his own—black secrets, by the look of him. Secrets compared to which poor Jekyll’s worst would be like sunshine. Things cannot continue as they are. It turns me cold to think of this creature stealing like a thief to Harry’s bedside. Poor Harry, what a wakening! And the danger of it! For if this Hyde suspects the existence of the will, he may grow impatient to inherit. Ah, I must put my shoulder to the wheel—if Jekyll will only let me,” he added, “if Jekyll will only let me.” For once more he saw before his mind’s eye, as clear as crystal, the strange clauses of the will.

###CHAPTER### Dr. Jekyll Was Quite at Ease

Two weeks later, by very good fortune, the doctor gave one of his pleasant dinners to five or six old friends—all intelligent, reputable men and all good judges of wine. Mr. Utterson arranged to stay behind after the others had left. This was nothing new, but something that had happened many times before. Where people liked Utterson, they liked him a great deal. Hosts loved to detain the dry lawyer when the light-hearted and talkative guests were already on their way out; they liked to sit a while in his unobtrusive company, preparing for solitude, letting their minds grow calm in his rich silence after the effort and strain of being cheerful. Dr. Jekyll was no exception to this rule. As he now sat on the opposite side of the fire—a large, well-made, smooth-faced man of fifty, with perhaps something of a sly expression, but every mark of sense and kindness—you could see from his looks that he felt a sincere and warm affection for Mr. Utterson.

"I have been wanting to speak to you, Jekyll," Utterson began. "You know that will of yours?"

A close observer might have gathered that the topic was unwelcome, but the doctor carried it off cheerfully. "My poor Utterson," said he, "you are unfortunate in such a client. I never saw a man so distressed as you were by my will—unless it was that narrow-minded pedant, Lanyon, at what he called my scientific heresies. Oh, I know he's a good fellow—you needn't frown—an excellent fellow, and I always mean to see more of him; but a narrow-minded pedant for all that, an ignorant, blatant pedant. I was never more disappointed in any man than Lanyon."

"You know I never approved of it," Utterson went on, ruthlessly disregarding the new topic.

"My will? Yes, certainly, I know that," said the doctor, a little sharply. "You have told me so."

"Well, I tell you so again," continued the lawyer. "I have been learning something of young Hyde."

The large, handsome face of Dr. Jekyll grew pale, even to his lips, and a darkness came about his eyes. "I do not wish to hear any more," said he. "This is a matter I thought we had agreed to drop."

"What I heard was abominable," said Utterson.

"It can make no difference. You do not understand my position," returned the doctor, with a certain confusion of manner. "I am in a painful situation, Utterson; my position is a very strange one—a very strange one. It is one of those affairs that cannot be mended by talking."

"Jekyll," said Utterson, "you know me: I am a man to be trusted. Make a clean breast of this in confidence, and I have no doubt I can get you out of it."

"My good Utterson," said the doctor, "this is very good of you, this is truly good of you, and I cannot find words to thank you. I fully believe you; I would trust you before any man alive, yes, before myself, if I could make the choice. But indeed it isn't what you imagine; it is not as bad as that. And just to set your good heart at ease, I will tell you one thing: the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde. I give you my hand on that, and I thank you again and again. I will just add one little word, Utterson, that I am sure you will take in the right spirit: this is a private matter, and I beg you to let it lie."

Utterson thought for a moment, gazing into the fire.

"I have no doubt you are perfectly right," he said at last, getting to his feet.

“Well, since we have discussed this matter, and for the last time I hope,” continued the doctor, “there is one point I’d like you to understand. I really have a very great interest in poor Hyde. I know you have seen him; he told me so; and I’m afraid he was rude. But I sincerely take a great, a very great interest in that young man. If I am taken away, Utterson, I want you to promise me that you will be patient with him and get his rights for him. I think you would, if you knew all; and it would be a weight off my mind if you would promise.”

“I can’t pretend I’ll ever like him,” said the lawyer.

“I don’t ask that,” pleaded Jekyll, laying his hand on the other’s arm. “I only ask for justice. I only ask you to help him for my sake, when I am no longer here.”

Utterson heaved an irrepressible sigh. “Well,” he said, “I promise.”

###CHAPTER### The Carew Murder Case

Nearly a year later, in October 18—, London was shocked by a crime of singular ferocity, made all the more notorious by the high social standing of the victim. The details were few and startling. A maidservant, living alone in a house not far from the river, had gone upstairs to bed around eleven. Although a fog rolled over the city in the early morning hours, the first part of the night was cloudless, and the lane outside her window was brilliantly lit by the full moon. It seems she had a romantic nature, for she sat down on her trunk, which stood directly under the window, and fell into a dreamy state of musing. Never, she used to say with streaming tears when she told the story, never had she felt more at peace with all people or thought more kindly of the world. As she sat there, she became aware of a beautiful old gentleman with white hair drawing near along the lane, and another, very small gentleman advancing to meet him, to whom at first she paid less attention. When they came within speaking distance—right under the maid's eyes—the older man bowed and addressed the other with a very graceful politeness. The subject of his words didn't seem very important; indeed, from his gestures, it sometimes looked as if he was only asking for directions. But the moon shone on his face as he spoke, and the girl liked watching it, for it seemed to radiate such an innocent, old-world kindness of disposition, yet with something noble too, like a well-founded self-contentment. Presently her gaze wandered to the other man, and she was surprised to recognize in him a certain Mr. Hyde, who had once visited her employer and for whom she had formed a dislike. He had a heavy cane in his hand and was toying with it, but he answered not a word, and seemed to listen with barely contained impatience. Then, all of a sudden, he burst out in a great flame of anger, stamping his foot, brandishing the cane, and carrying on—as the maid described it—like a madman. The old gentleman stepped back, looking very much surprised and a little hurt; and at that, Mr. Hyde lost all restraint and clubbed him to the ground. The next moment, with ape-like fury, he was trampling his victim underfoot and raining down a storm of blows, under which the bones shattered audibly and the body jerked upon the roadway. The maid fainted at the horror of these sights and sounds.

It was two o'clock when she came to and called the police. The murderer had long since fled; but there lay his victim in the middle of the lane, incredibly mangled. The cane with which the deed had been done, though made of some rare, very tough, and heavy wood, had broken in the middle under the stress of this senseless cruelty. One splintered half had rolled into the nearby gutter; the other, no doubt, had been carried away by the murderer. A purse and gold watch were found upon the victim, but no cards or papers, except for a sealed and stamped envelope, which he had probably been carrying to the post and which bore the name and address of Mr. Utterson.

This was brought to the lawyer the next morning, before he was out of bed. As soon as he saw it and heard the circumstances, he pursed his lips solemnly. “I shall say nothing till I have seen the body,” he said; “this may be very serious. Please wait while I dress.” And with the same grave expression he hurried through his breakfast and drove to the police station, where the body had been taken. As soon as he entered the cell, he nodded.

“Yes,” he said, “I recognize him. I am sorry to say that this is Sir Danvers Carew.”

“Good God, sir,” exclaimed the officer, “is it possible?” The next moment his eye lit up with professional ambition. “This will cause a great deal of noise,” he said. “And perhaps you can help us find the man.” And he briefly told what the maid had seen, and showed the broken stick.

Mr. Utterson had already recoiled at the name of Hyde; but when the stick was placed before him, he could doubt no longer. Broken and battered as it was, he recognized it as one he had himself given to Henry Jekyll many years before.

“Is this Mr. Hyde a short man?” he asked.

“The maid calls him particularly small and particularly wicked-looking,” said the officer.

Mr. Utterson reflected; then, raising his head, he said, “If you will come with me in my cab, I think I can take you to his house.”

It was by this time about nine in the morning, and the first fog of the season. A great chocolate-coloured shroud hung over the sky, but the wind was continually charging and scattering these battle-ready mists. As the cab crawled from street to street, Mr. Utterson beheld a marvellous range of degrees and hues of twilight. Here it would be dark like the tail-end of evening; there, a glow of a rich, lurid brown, like the light of some strange fire. And here, for a moment, the fog would break up completely, and a gaunt shaft of daylight would glance in between the swirling wisps. The dismal quarter of Soho, seen under these changing glimpses, with its muddy streets, slatternly passers-by, and its lamps that had never been put out or had been lit again to fight this mournful return of darkness, seemed, in the lawyer’s eyes, like a district of some nightmare city. Besides, his thoughts were of the darkest dye; and when he glanced at his companion in the cab, he felt a touch of that fear of the law and the law’s officers which may at times assail the most honest.

As the cab pulled up before the indicated address, the fog lifted a little and showed him a dingy street, a gin palace, a cheap French eating house, a shop selling penny serials and twopenny salads, many ragged children huddled in the doorways, and many women of many different nationalities coming out, key in hand, for a morning glass. The next moment the fog settled down again upon that spot, as brown as umber, and cut him off from his disreputable surroundings. This was the home of Henry Jekyll’s favourite: a man who was heir to a quarter of a million pounds.

An ivory-faced, silvery-haired old woman opened the door. Her face was evil, smoothed over by hypocrisy, but her manners were excellent. Yes, she said, this was Mr. Hyde’s house, but he was not at home. He had come in very late that night, but he had left again in less than an hour. There was nothing unusual about that; his habits were very irregular, and he was often away. For instance, until yesterday she had not seen him for nearly two months.

“Very well, then, we wish to see his rooms,” said the lawyer. When the woman began to protest that it was impossible, he added, “I had better tell you who this person is. This is Inspector Newcomen of Scotland Yard.”

A flash of hateful joy crossed the woman’s face. “Ah!” she said, “he is in trouble! What has he done?”

Mr. Utterson and the inspector exchanged glances. “He don’t seem a very popular character,” the inspector observed. “And now, my good woman, just let me and this gentleman have a look around.”

The whole house was empty except for the old woman. In all that space, Mr. Hyde had only used a couple of rooms, but these were furnished with luxury and good taste. A closet was full of wine; the dishes were silver, the linen elegant. A fine painting hung on the wall—a gift, Utterson supposed, from Henry Jekyll, who was quite a connoisseur—and the carpets were thick and pleasantly colored. At that moment, however, the rooms showed every sign of a recent, hurried ransacking. Clothes lay on the floor, pockets turned inside out; locked drawers stood open; and on the hearth lay a pile of grey ashes, as though many papers had been burned. From these embers the inspector dug out the stub of a green cheque book, which had resisted the fire. The other half of the cheque book was found behind the door, and as this clinched his suspicions, the officer declared himself delighted. A visit to the bank, where several thousand pounds stood to the murderer’s credit, completed his satisfaction.

“You may depend on it, sir,” he told Mr. Utterson. “I have him. He must have lost his head, or he would never have left the stick or, most of all, burned the cheque book. Why, money is life to that man. We have nothing to do but wait for him at the bank and get out the handbills.”

This last, however, was not so easy to accomplish, for Mr. Hyde had very few close acquaintances—even the servant maid’s master had only seen him twice. His family could not be traced anywhere; he had never been photographed; and the few who could describe him gave widely different accounts, as ordinary witnesses often do. They agreed on only one point: the haunting sense of an indescribable deformity that the fugitive left upon everyone who saw him.

###CHAPTER### Incident of the Letter

It was late in the afternoon when Mr. Utterson arrived at Dr. Jekyll’s door. Poole admitted him at once and led him through the kitchen quarters, across a yard that had once been a garden, to the building known variously as the laboratory or the dissecting rooms. The doctor had bought the house from the heirs of a famous surgeon, and his own tastes being more chemical than anatomical, he had changed the purpose of the block at the bottom of the garden. This was the first time the lawyer had been received in that part of his friend’s quarters. He eyed the dingy, windowless structure with curiosity. He gazed around with an uneasy sense of unfamiliarity as he crossed the old lecture theatre, once crowded with eager students and now lying bare and silent. The tables were piled with chemical apparatus, the floor strewn with crates and packing straw, and the light fell dimly through the fogged cupola. At the far end, a flight of stairs led up to a door covered with red baize, and through this Mr. Utterson was at last received into the doctor’s private study. It was a large room lined with glass-fronted cabinets, furnished with a full-length mirror and a writing desk, and it overlooked the courtyard through three dusty, iron-barred windows. A fire burned in the grate; a lamp was lit on the mantelpiece, for the fog was thickening even indoors. There, huddled close to the warmth, sat Dr. Jekyll, looking deathly pale and ill. He did not rise to meet his visitor, but held out a cold hand and welcomed him in a changed voice.

“And now,” said Mr. Utterson, as soon as Poole had left them, “you have heard the news?”

The doctor shuddered. “They were shouting it in the square,” he said. “I heard them from my dining-room.”

“One word,” said the lawyer. “Carew was my client, but so are you, and I want to know what I am doing. You have not been mad enough to hide this fellow?”

“Utterson, I swear to God,” cried the doctor, “I swear to God I will never set eyes on him again. I pledge my honour that I am done with him in this world. It is all over. And indeed he does not want my help; you do not know him as I do. He is safe, he is quite safe; mark my words, he will never be heard of again.”

The lawyer listened gloomily; he did not like his friend’s feverish manner. “You seem pretty sure of him,” he said; “and for your sake, I hope you’re right. If it came to a trial, your name might appear.”

“I am quite sure of him,” replied Jekyll; “I have reasons for my certainty that I cannot share with anyone. But there is one thing on which you may advise me. I have—I have received a letter, and I am unsure whether I should show it to the police. I would like to leave it with you, Utterson; you would judge wisely, I am sure; I have such great trust in you.”

“You fear, I suppose, that it might lead to his detection?” asked the lawyer.

“No,” said the other. “I cannot say that I care what becomes of Hyde; I am quite done with him. I was thinking of my own reputation, which this hateful business has rather exposed.”

Utterson thought for a moment; he was surprised at his friend’s selfishness, and yet relieved by it. “Well,” he said at last, “let me see the letter.”

The letter was written in an odd, upright handwriting and signed “Edward Hyde.” It stated, briefly, that the writer’s benefactor, Dr. Jekyll—whom he had long repaid so poorly for a thousand kindnesses—need not worry about his safety, as he had means of escape he could depend on. The lawyer liked this letter well enough; it put a better light on the intimacy than he had expected, and he blamed himself for some of his past suspicions.

“Do you have the envelope?” he asked.

“I burned it,” replied Jekyll, “before I realized what I was doing. But it had no postmark. The note was delivered by hand.”

“Shall I keep this and sleep on it?” asked Utterson.

“I want you to judge for me entirely,” was the reply. “I have lost confidence in myself.”

“Well, I’ll consider it,” returned the lawyer. “And one more thing: was it Hyde who dictated the terms in your will about that disappearance?”

The doctor seemed overcome by a sudden faintness; he shut his mouth tight and nodded.

“I knew it,” said Utterson. “He meant to murder you. You had a narrow escape.”

“I have had something far more important,” returned the doctor solemnly: “I have had a lesson—O God, Utterson, what a lesson I have had!” And he covered his face for a moment with his hands.

On his way out, the lawyer stopped and had a few words with Poole. “By the way,” said he, “there was a letter delivered by hand today: what was the messenger like?” But Poole was certain nothing had come except by post; “and only junk mail by that,” he added.

This news sent the visitor away with his fears renewed. Clearly the letter had come through the laboratory door; it might even have been written in the cabinet. If that were so, it must be judged differently, and handled with more caution. The newsboys, as he went, were crying themselves hoarse along the pavements: “Special edition. Shocking murder of an M.P.” That was the funeral speech for one friend and client; and he could not help a certain fear that the good name of another might be dragged down in the swirl of the scandal. It was, at least, a delicate decision that he had to make; and though he was habitually self-reliant, he began to long for advice. It could not be had directly; but perhaps, he thought, it might be fished for.

A little later, he sat on one side of his own hearth, with Mr. Guest, his head clerk, on the other. Midway between them, at a carefully calculated distance from the fire, stood a bottle of a particular old wine that had long rested away from sunlight in the foundations of his house. The fog still slept above the drowned city, where the lamps glimmered like glowing red jewels; and through the muffling smother of these fallen clouds, the procession of the town’s life was still rolling in through the great arteries with a sound like a mighty wind. But the room was cheerful with firelight. In the bottle the acids had long ago resolved; the imperial dye had softened with time, as the colour grows richer in stained glass; and the glow of hot autumn afternoons on hillside vineyards was ready to be set free and to disperse the fogs of London. Gradually the lawyer relaxed. There was no one from whom he kept fewer secrets than Mr. Guest; and he was not always sure he kept as many as he meant. Guest had often gone on business to the doctor’s; he knew Poole; he could scarcely have failed to hear of Mr. Hyde’s familiarity about the house; he might draw conclusions: was it not as well, then, that he should see a letter which put that mystery right? And above all, since Guest, being a great student and critic of handwriting, would consider the step natural and obliging? The clerk, besides, was a man of discretion; he could hardly read so strange a document without making a remark; and by that remark Mr. Utterson might shape his future course.

“This is a sad business about Sir Danvers,” he said.

“Yes, sir, indeed. It has stirred up a great deal of public feeling,” replied Guest. “The man, of course, was mad.”

“I should like to hear your views on that,” replied Utterson. “I have a document here in his handwriting; it is between ourselves, for I hardly know what to do about it; it is an ugly business at best. But there it is; quite in your line: a murderer’s autograph.”

Guest’s eyes brightened, and he sat down at once and studied it eagerly. “No, sir,” he said, “not mad; but it is an odd handwriting.”

“And by all accounts a very odd writer,” added the lawyer.

Just then the servant entered with a note.

“Is that from Dr. Jekyll, sir?” asked the clerk. “I thought I recognized the writing. Anything private, Mr. Utterson?”

“Only an invitation to dinner. Why? Do you want to see it?”

“One moment. I thank you, sir,” and the clerk laid the two sheets of paper side by side and carefully compared their contents. “Thank you, sir,” he said at last, returning both, “it’s a very interesting handwriting.”

There was a pause. Mr. Utterson struggled with himself. “Why did you compare them, Guest?” he asked suddenly.

“Well, sir,” replied the clerk, “there’s a rather peculiar resemblance; the two handwritings are in many points identical: only sloped differently.”

“Rather quaint,” said Utterson.

“It is, as you say, rather quaint,” replied Guest.

“I wouldn’t mention this note, you know,” said the master.

“No, sir,” said the clerk. “I understand.”

But no sooner was Mr. Utterson alone that night than he locked the note in his safe, where it stayed from that time forward. “What!” he thought. “Henry Jekyll forge for a murderer!” And his blood ran cold in his veins.

###CHAPTER### Incident of Dr. Lanyon

Time passed. Thousands of pounds were offered as a reward, because the death of Sir Danvers was resented as a public harm; but Mr. Hyde had disappeared out of the police’s sight as though he had never existed. Much of his past was dug up, indeed, and all of it was disreputable: tales came out of the man’s cruelty, at once so callous and violent; of his vile life, of his strange associates, of the hatred that seemed to have surrounded his career; but of his present whereabouts, not a whisper. From the moment he had left the house in Soho on the morning of the murder, he was simply blotted out; and gradually, as time drew on, Mr. Utterson began to recover from the heat of his alarm, and to grow more at peace with himself. The death of Sir Danvers was, to his way of thinking, more than paid for by the disappearance of Mr. Hyde. Now that that evil influence had been withdrawn, a new life began for Dr. Jekyll. He came out of his seclusion, renewed relations with his friends, became once more their familiar guest and entertainer; and while he had always been known for his charities, he was now no less distinguished for his religion. He was busy, he spent much time in the open air, he did good; his face seemed to open and brighten, as if with an inward consciousness of service; and for more than two months, the doctor was at peace.

On the 8th of January Utterson had dined at the doctor’s with a small party; Lanyon had been there; and the face of the host had looked from one to the other as in the old days when the trio were inseparable friends. On the 12th, and again on the 14th, the door was shut against the lawyer. “The doctor was confined to the house,” Poole said, “and saw no one.” On the 15th, he tried again, and was again refused; and having now been used for the last two months to see his friend almost daily, he found this return of solitude to weigh upon his spirits. On the fifth evening, he had Guest to dine with him; and on the sixth he went to Dr. Lanyon’s.

There at least he was not denied admittance; but when he came in, he was shocked at the change which had taken place in the doctor’s appearance. He had his death-warrant written legibly upon his face. The rosy man had grown pale; his flesh had fallen away; he was visibly balder and older; and yet it was not so much these signs of a swift physical decay that caught the lawyer’s notice, as a look in the eye and quality of manner that seemed to testify to some deep-seated terror of the mind. It was unlikely that the doctor should fear death; and yet that was what Utterson was tempted to suspect. “Yes,” he thought; “he is a doctor, he must know his own state and that his days are counted; and the knowledge is more than he can bear.” And yet when Utterson remarked on his ill looks, it was with an air of great firmness that Lanyon declared himself a doomed man.

“I have had a shock,” he said, “and I shall never recover. It is a question of weeks. Well, life has been pleasant; I liked it; yes, sir, I used to like it. I sometimes think if we knew all, we should be more glad to get away.”

“Jekyll is ill, too,” observed Utterson. “Have you seen him?”

But Lanyon’s face changed, and he held up a trembling hand. “I wish to see or hear no more of Dr. Jekyll,” he said in a loud, unsteady voice. “I am quite done with that person; and I beg that you will spare me any allusion to one whom I regard as dead.”

"Tut, tut!" said Mr. Utterson. After a considerable pause, he inquired, "Can't I do anything? We are three very old friends, Lanyon. We shall not live to make new ones."

"Nothing can be done," returned Lanyon. "Ask him yourself."

"He will not see me," said the lawyer.

"I am not surprised at that," was the reply. "Some day, Utterson, after I am dead, you may perhaps come to learn the right and wrong of this. I cannot tell you. In the meantime, if you can sit and talk with me of other things, for God's sake, stay and do so. But if you cannot keep clear of this cursed topic, then in God's name, go, for I cannot bear it."

As soon as he got home, Utterson sat down and wrote to Jekyll, complaining of being shut out of the house and asking the cause of this unhappy break with Lanyon. The next day brought a long answer, often very pathetically worded, and sometimes darkly mysterious in meaning. The quarrel with Lanyon was beyond remedy. "I do not blame our old friend," Jekyll wrote, "but I share his view that we must never meet. I mean from now on to lead a life of extreme seclusion; you must not be surprised, nor must you doubt my friendship, if my door is often shut even to you. You must allow me to go my own dark way. I have brought on myself a punishment and a danger that I cannot name. If I am the chief of sinners, I am the chief of sufferers also. I could not think that this earth contained a place for sufferings and terrors so completely unnerving; and you can do but one thing, Utterson, to lighten this destiny, and that is to respect my silence." Utterson was amazed; the dark influence of Hyde had been withdrawn, the doctor had returned to his old tasks and friendships; a week ago, the prospect had smiled with every promise of a cheerful and an honored age; and now in a moment, friendship, and peace of mind, and the whole tenor of his life were wrecked. So great and unprepared a change pointed to madness; but in view of Lanyon's manner and words, there must be some deeper reason for it.

About a week later Dr. Lanyon took to his bed, and in less than two weeks he was dead. The night after the funeral, at which he had been deeply moved, Utterson locked the door of his business room. Sitting there by the light of a melancholy candle, he drew out an envelope addressed in his dead friend’s hand and sealed with his friend’s seal. “PRIVATE: for the hands of G. J. Utterson ALONE, and in case of his death _to be destroyed unread_,” was emphatically written on it; and the lawyer dreaded seeing the contents. “I have buried one friend today,” he thought, “what if this should cost me another?” Then he condemned the fear as disloyal, and broke the seal. Inside was another enclosure, also sealed, and its cover was marked “not to be opened till the death or disappearance of Dr. Henry Jekyll.” Utterson could not trust his eyes. Yes, it said disappearance; here again—just as in the mad will he had long ago returned to its author—the idea of a disappearance and the name of Henry Jekyll were paired. But in the will, that idea had sprung from the sinister suggestion of the man Hyde; it was set there with a purpose all too plain and horrible. Written in Lanyon’s hand, what could it mean? A great curiosity came upon the trustee to disregard the prohibition and dive at once to the bottom of these mysteries; but professional honour and faith to his dead friend were strict obligations; and the packet slept in the innermost corner of his private safe.

It is one thing to deny curiosity, another to conquer it; and it may be doubted if, from that day forward, Utterson sought his surviving friend’s company with the same eagerness. He thought of him kindly, but his thoughts were uneasy and fearful. He did call, indeed; but perhaps he felt relieved when he was denied entry. Perhaps, in his heart, he preferred to speak with Poole on the doorstep, surrounded by the air and sounds of the open city, rather than to be admitted into that house of voluntary imprisonment and sit and talk with its inscrutable recluse. Poole, indeed, had no very pleasant news to communicate. The doctor, it appeared, now more than ever confined himself to the cabinet over the laboratory, where he would sometimes even sleep; he was out of spirits, he had grown very silent, he did not read; it seemed as if he had something on his mind. Utterson became so used to the unvarying character of these reports that he gradually fell off in the frequency of his visits.

###CHAPTER### Incident at the Window

It happened on Sunday, while Mr. Utterson was on his usual walk with Mr. Enfield, that their route once again led them through that side street; and when they came in front of the door, both stopped to gaze at it.

“Well,” said Enfield, “that story is over, at least. We shall never see any more of Mr. Hyde.”

“I hope not,” said Utterson. “Did I ever tell you that I once saw him, and shared your feeling of repulsion?”

“It was impossible to do the one without the other,” answered Enfield. “And by the way, what a fool you must have thought me, for not knowing that this was a back entrance to Dr. Jekyll’s! It was partly your own fault that I discovered it, even when I did.”

“So you found it out, did you?” said Utterson. “But if that is so, we might step into the courtyard and take a look at the windows. To tell you the truth, I am uneasy about poor Jekyll; and even from outside, I feel as if the presence of a friend might do him good.”

The courtyard was very cool, a little damp, and full of early twilight, though the sky high overhead was still bright with sunset. The middle of the three windows was half open; and sitting close beside it, taking the air with an infinitely sad expression, like a dejected prisoner, Utterson saw Dr. Jekyll.

“What! Jekyll! Jekyll!” he cried. “I trust you are better.”

“I am very low, Utterson,” replied the doctor drearily, “very low. It will not last long, thank God.”

“You stay indoors too much,” said the lawyer. “You should be out, stirring your circulation like Mr. Enfield and me. (This is my cousin—Mr. Enfield—Dr. Jekyll.) Come now; get your hat and take a quick walk with us.”

“You are very good,” sighed the other. “I would like to very much; but no, no, no, it is quite impossible; I dare not. But indeed, Utterson, I am very glad to see you; this is really a great pleasure; I would ask you and Mr. Enfield up, but the place is really not fit.”

“Well, then,” said the lawyer, good-naturedly, “the best thing we can do is to stay down here and speak with you from where we are.”

“That is just what I was about to venture to propose,” replied the doctor with a smile. But the words were hardly uttered before the smile was struck from his face and replaced by an expression of such abject terror and despair that it froze the blood of the two gentlemen below. They saw it only for a glimpse, for the window was instantly thrust down; but that glimpse was enough, and they turned and left the courtyard without a word. In silence, too, they walked back through the side street; and it was not until they had come into a nearby thoroughfare, where even on a Sunday there were still some stirrings of life, that Mr. Utterson at last turned and looked at his companion. They were both pale, and there was an answering horror in their eyes.

“God forgive us, God forgive us,” said Mr. Utterson.

But Mr. Enfield only nodded his head very seriously, and walked on again in silence.

###CHAPTER### The Last Night

One evening after dinner, Mr. Utterson was sitting by his fireside when Poole paid him a surprise visit.

“Good heavens, Poole, what brings you here?” he cried. Then, taking a second look at him, he added, “What’s wrong? Is the doctor ill?”

“Mr. Utterson,” said the man, “there is something wrong.”

“Take a seat, and here is a glass of wine for you,” said the lawyer. “Now, take your time, and tell me plainly what you want.”

“You know the doctor’s habits, sir,” replied Poole, “and how he shuts himself away. Well, he’s shut himself up again in the cabinet, and I don’t like it, sir—I swear I don’t like it. Mr. Utterson, sir, I’m afraid.”

“Now, my good man,” said the lawyer, “speak plainly. What are you afraid of?”

“I’ve been afraid for about a week,” Poole returned, stubbornly ignoring the question, “and I can’t bear it any longer.”

The man’s appearance fully supported his words. His manner had changed for the worse, and except for the moment when he first admitted his terror, he had not once looked the lawyer in the face. Even now, he sat with the wine glass untouched on his knee, his eyes fixed on a corner of the floor. “I can’t bear it any longer,” he repeated.

“Come,” said the lawyer, “I see you have some good reason, Poole. I see there is something seriously wrong. Try to tell me what it is.”

“I think there’s been foul play,” said Poole, hoarsely.

“Foul play!” cried the lawyer, very frightened and therefore rather irritated. “What foul play! What do you mean?”

“I dare not say, sir,” was the answer, “but will you come with me and see for yourself?”

Mr. Utterson’s only answer was to get up and fetch his hat and overcoat. But he noticed with wonder the enormous relief that appeared on the butler’s face, and noticed perhaps with equal wonder that the wine was still untouched as Poole set it down to follow.

It was a wild, cold, typical March night, with a pale moon lying on her back as though the wind had tilted her, and flying wisps of cloud so thin and delicate they looked like lace. The wind made talking difficult and stung the blood into their faces. It seemed also to have swept the streets unusually empty of people; for Mr. Utterson thought he had never seen that part of London so deserted. He could have wished it otherwise; never in his life had he felt so sharp a desire to see and touch his fellow human beings. For, struggle as he might, a crushing sense of coming disaster bore down upon his mind. The square, when they reached it, was full of wind and dust, and the thin trees in the garden were lashing themselves against the railing. Poole, who had kept a pace or two ahead all the way, now stopped in the middle of the pavement. In spite of the biting weather, he took off his hat and mopped his brow with a red pocket-handkerchief. But for all the hurry of his coming, it was not the sweat of effort that he wiped away, but the moisture of some choking anguish; his face was white, and his voice, when he spoke, was harsh and broken.

“Well, sir,” he said, “here we are, and God grant there’s nothing wrong.”

“Amen, Poole,” said the lawyer.

Then the servant knocked in a very cautious manner; the door was opened on the chain; and a voice from inside asked, “Is that you, Poole?”

“It’s all right,” said Poole. “Open the door.”

When they entered the hall, it was brightly lit and the fire was built up high. Around the hearth, all the servants—men and women—stood huddled together like a flock of sheep. At the sight of Mr. Utterson, the housemaid broke into hysterical whimpering. The cook cried out, “Thank God! It’s Mr. Utterson,” and ran forward as if to embrace him.

“What, what? Are you all here?” said the lawyer peevishly. “This is very irregular, very improper; your master would be far from pleased.”

“They’re all afraid,” said Poole.

A blank silence followed. No one protested; only the maid lifted her voice and now wept loudly.

“Hold your tongue!” Poole said to her, with a ferocity that showed his own jangled nerves. Indeed, when the girl had so suddenly raised her lament, they all started and turned towards the inner door with faces of dreadful expectation. “And now,” continued the butler, addressing the knife-boy, “bring me a candle, and we’ll get this over with at once.” Then he asked Mr. Utterson to follow him and led the way to the back garden.

“Now, sir,” he said, “come as quietly as you can. I want you to hear, but I don’t want you to be heard. And see here, sir, if by any chance he should ask you in, don’t go.”

At this unexpected turn, Mr. Utterson’s nerves gave a jerk that nearly threw him off balance. But he gathered his courage and followed the butler into the laboratory building, through the surgical theatre with its clutter of crates and bottles, to the foot of the stairs. There, Poole motioned him to stand aside and listen. Poole himself set down the candle, made a great and obvious effort to steady his nerves, mounted the steps, and knocked with a somewhat uncertain hand on the red baize of the cabinet door.

“Mr. Utterson, sir, asking to see you,” he called. Even as he did so, he once more made a violent sign for the lawyer to listen.

A voice answered from within: “Tell him I cannot see anyone,” it said complainingly.

“Thank you, sir,” said Poole, with a note of something like triumph in his voice. Taking up his candle, he led Mr. Utterson back across the yard and into the great kitchen, where the fire was out and beetles were leaping on the floor.

“Sir,” he said, looking Mr. Utterson in the eyes, “was that my master’s voice?”

“It seems very much changed,” replied the lawyer, very pale, but meeting his gaze.

“Changed? Well, yes, I suppose so,” said the butler. “Have I been twenty years in this man’s house, only to be deceived about his voice? No, sir. Master’s been made away with—he was made away with eight days ago, when we heard him cry out upon the name of God. And who is in there instead of him, and why it stays there, is a thing that cries out to Heaven, Mr. Utterson!”

“That is a very strange tale, Poole; it’s rather a wild tale, my man,” said Mr. Utterson, biting his finger. “Suppose it were as you suppose—supposing Dr. Jekyll to have been—well, murdered. What could induce the murderer to stay? That won’t hold water; it just doesn’t make sense.”

“Well, Mr. Utterson, you are a hard man to satisfy, but I’ll do it yet,” said Poole. “All this last week, the person—or whatever it is—living in that cabinet has been crying night and day for some kind of medicine, and he can’t get it to his satisfaction. The master sometimes used to write his orders on a sheet of paper and throw it on the stairs. This past week we’ve had nothing else: nothing but notes, a closed door, and meals left there to be smuggled in when no one was looking. Well, sir, every day—yes, and two or three times a day—there have been orders and complaints, and I’ve been sent rushing to every wholesale chemist in town. Every time I brought the stuff back, there would be another note telling me to return it because it wasn’t pure, and another order to a different firm. This drug is wanted very badly, sir, whatever it’s for.”

“Do you have any of these notes?” asked Mr. Utterson.

Poole searched his pocket and handed over a crumpled note. The lawyer bent closer to the candle and examined it carefully. The note said: “Dr. Jekyll sends his compliments to Messrs. Maw. He assures them that their last sample is impure and quite useless for his current purpose. In the year 18—, Dr. J. purchased a somewhat large quantity from Messrs. M. He now begs them to search with the most diligent care, and if any of the same quality remains, to send it to him immediately. Cost is no object. The importance of this to Dr. J. can hardly be overstated.” Up to this point, the letter had been composed enough, but here, with a sudden splutter of the pen, the writer’s emotion broke loose. “For God’s sake,” he added, “find me some of the old.”

“This is a strange note,” said Mr. Utterson, and then sharply, “How come you have it open?”

“The man at Maw’s was very angry, sir, and he threw it back at me like it was dirt,” replied Poole.

“This is unquestionably the doctor’s handwriting, you know,” said Mr. Utterson.

“I thought it looked like it,” said the servant rather sulkily; then, in a different tone, “But what does handwriting matter? I’ve seen him!”

“Seen him?” repeated Mr. Utterson. “Well?”

“That’s it!” said Poole. “It happened like this. I came suddenly into the theatre from the garden. It seems he had slipped out to look for this drug or whatever it is; the cabinet door was open, and there he was at the far end of the room digging through the crates. He looked up when I came in, gave a kind of cry, and rushed upstairs into the cabinet. I only saw him for a minute, but my hair stood on end like quills. Sir, if that was my master, why did he have a mask on his face? If it was my master, why did he cry out like a rat and run from me? I have served him long enough. And then…” The man paused and passed his hand over his face.

“These are all very strange circumstances,” said Mr. Utterson, “but I think I begin to see daylight. Your master, Poole, is plainly seized with one of those diseases that both torture and deform the sufferer; hence, for all I know, the change in his voice; hence the mask and his avoidance of his friends; hence his eagerness to find this drug, by which the poor soul retains some hope of ultimate recovery—God grant he is not deceived! That is my explanation; it is sad enough, Poole, yes, and appalling to think about; but it is plain and natural, hangs together well, and frees us from all extreme alarms.”

"Sir," said the butler, his face turning a blotchy pale, "that thing was not my master, and that's the truth. My master"—here he looked around and began to whisper—"is a tall, well-built man, and this was more like a dwarf." Utterson tried to protest. "Oh, sir," cried Poole, "do you think I don't know my master after twenty years? Do you think I don't know how high his head reaches in the cabinet door, where I saw him every morning of my life? No, sir, that masked thing was never Dr. Jekyll—God knows what it was, but it was never Dr. Jekyll; and I believe in my heart that murder has been done."

"Poole," replied the lawyer, "if you say that, it becomes my duty to make sure. Much as I want to spare your master's feelings, and much as I am puzzled by this note that seems to prove he is still alive, I will consider it my duty to break down that door."

"Ah, Mr. Utterson, now you're talking!" cried the butler.

"And now comes the second question," resumed Utterson: "Who is going to do it?"

"Why, you and me, sir," was the undaunted reply.

"That's very well said," replied the lawyer; "and whatever comes of it, I'll make it my business to see that you don't lose by it."

"There is an axe in the theatre," continued Poole; "and you could take the kitchen poker for yourself."

The lawyer took that crude but heavy tool in his hand and balanced it. "Do you know, Poole," he said, looking up, "that you and I are about to put ourselves in a position of some danger?"

"You may well say so, sir," returned the butler.

"It is well, then, that we should be honest," said the other. "We both think more than we have said; let us make a clean breast of it. This masked figure that you saw—did you recognise it?"

"Well, sir, it moved so quickly, and the creature was so bent over, that I could hardly swear to it," was the answer. "But if you mean, was it Mr. Hyde?—why, yes, I think it was! You see, it was much the same size; and it had the same quick, light way of moving; and then who else could have got in by the laboratory door? You haven't forgotten, sir, that at the time of the murder he still had the key with him? But that's not all. I don't know, Mr. Utterson, if you ever met this Mr. Hyde?"

"Yes," said the lawyer, "I once spoke with him."

"Then you must know as well as the rest of us that there was something strange about that gentleman—something that gave a man a turn—I don't know quite how to put it, sir, beyond this: that you felt in your marrow a kind of cold and thin feeling."

"I admit I felt something of what you describe," said Mr. Utterson.

"Quite so, sir," returned Poole. "Well, when that masked thing like a monkey jumped from among the chemicals and whipped into the cabinet, it went down my spine like ice. Oh, I know it's not evidence, Mr. Utterson; I'm book-learned enough for that; but a man has his feelings, and I give you my Bible-oath it was Mr. Hyde!"

"Ay, ay," said the lawyer. "My fears lean the same way. Evil, I fear, was founded—evil was bound to come—from that connection. Ay, truly, I believe you; I believe poor Harry is killed; and I believe his murderer (for what purpose, God alone can tell) is still lurking in his victim's room. Well, let our name be vengeance. Call Bradshaw."

The footman came when called, very pale and nervous.

"Pull yourself together, Bradshaw," said the lawyer. "I know this suspense is wearing on all of you, but we intend to put an end to it now. Poole, here, and I are going to force our way into the cabinet. If all is well, my shoulders are broad enough to bear the blame. Meanwhile, you and the boy must go round the corner with a pair of good sticks and take up your position at the laboratory door, in case anything is really wrong or any criminal tries to escape out the back. We give you ten minutes to get to your places."

As Bradshaw left, the lawyer looked at his watch. "And now, Poole, let us get to ours," he said, and taking the poker under his arm, led the way into the yard. The clouds had banked over the moon, and it was now quite dark. The wind, which only broke in puffs and draughts into that deep courtyard, tossed the candlelight back and forth around their steps until they reached the shelter of the theatre. There they sat down silently to wait. London hummed solemnly all around; but closer by, the stillness was broken only by the sound of a footstep moving back and forth on the cabinet floor.

"So he walks all day, sir," whispered Poole; "yes, and most of the night. Only when a new sample comes from the chemist, there's a bit of a break. Ah, a guilty conscience is such an enemy to rest! Ah, sir, there's foully spilled blood in every step of it! But listen again, a little closer—put your heart in your ears, Mr. Utterson, and tell me, is that the doctor's footstep?"

The steps fell lightly and oddly, with a certain swing, even though they moved so slowly; it was indeed different from the heavy, creaking tread of Henry Jekyll. Utterson sighed. "Is there never anything else?" he asked.

Poole nodded. "Once," he said. "Once I heard it weeping!"

"Weeping? What do you mean?" said the lawyer, feeling a sudden chill of horror.

"Weeping like a woman or a lost soul," said the butler. "I came away with that on my heart. I could have wept too."

But now the ten minutes came to an end. Poole dug out the axe from under a stack of packing straw; they set the candle on the nearest table to light their attack; and they approached with bated breath to where that patient footstep was still pacing up and down, up and down, in the quiet of the night.

"Jekyll," cried Utterson in a loud voice, "I demand to see you." He paused a moment, but there came no reply. "I give you fair warning, our suspicions are aroused, and I must and will see you," he resumed; "if not by fair means, then by foul—if not with your consent, then by brute force!"

"Utterson," said the voice, "for God's sake, have mercy!"

"Ah, that's not Jekyll's voice—it's Hyde's!" cried Utterson. "Break down the door, Poole!"

Poole swung the axe over his shoulder; the blow shook the building, and the red baize door jumped against the lock and hinges. A dismal screech, like that of a terrified animal, rang from the cabinet. He raised the axe again, and again the panels crashed and the frame jumped; four times he struck. But the wood was tough and the fittings were excellently made; it was not until the fifth blow that the lock burst and the wreck of the door fell inward onto the carpet.

The besiegers, appalled by the noise they had made and the silence that followed, stepped back a little and peered in. There in the quiet lamplight lay the cabinet before their eyes. A good fire crackled on the hearth, the kettle sang its thin tune, a drawer or two stood open, papers were neatly arranged on the business table, and nearer the fire, the tea things were set out. You would have said it was the quietest room, and except for the glass-fronted cupboards full of chemicals, the most ordinary room in London that night.

Right in the middle lay the body of a man, painfully contorted and still twitching. They drew near on tiptoe, turned him on his back, and saw the face of Edward Hyde. He was dressed in clothes far too large for him—clothes of the doctor's size. The sinews of his face still moved with a semblance of life, but life was quite gone. From the crushed vial in his hand and the strong smell of bitter almonds hanging in the air, Utterson knew that he was looking on the body of a suicide.

"We have come too late," he said sternly, "whether to save or punish. Hyde has gone to his account; and now it only remains for us to find the body of your master."

The greater part of the building was occupied by the theatre, which filled almost the whole ground floor and was lit from above, and by the cabinet, which formed an upper storey at one end and looked onto the court. A corridor joined the theatre to the door on the side street; and the cabinet communicated with this corridor separately by a second flight of stairs. There were also a few dark closets and a spacious cellar. All these they now examined thoroughly. Each closet needed only a glance, for all were empty, and all, from the dust that fell from their doors, had stood long unopened. The cellar, indeed, was filled with crazy lumber, mostly dating from the time of the surgeon who was Jekyll's predecessor; but even as they opened the door, the fall of a perfect mat of cobweb that had sealed the entrance for years told them further search was useless. Nowhere was there any trace of Henry Jekyll, dead or alive.

Poole stamped on the flagstones of the corridor. "He must be buried here," he said, listening to the sound.

"Or he may have fled," said Utterson, and he turned to examine the door in the side street. It was locked; and lying nearby on the flagstones, they found the key, already stained with rust.

"This does not look like use," observed the lawyer.

"Use!" echoed Poole. "Don't you see, sir, it's broken? Much as if a man had stamped on it."

"Ay," continued Utterson, "and the fractures, too, are rusty." The two men looked at each other in alarm. "This is beyond me, Poole," said the lawyer. "Let us go back to the cabinet."

They climbed the stairs in silence, still casting an occasional awestruck glance at the dead body, and went on to examine the contents of the cabinet more thoroughly. At one table, there were traces of chemical work. Various measured heaps of some white salt lay on glass saucers, as though for an experiment from which the unhappy man had been interrupted.

"That is the same drug I was always bringing him," said Poole; and even as he spoke, the kettle boiled over with a startling noise.

This brought them to the fireside, where the easy-chair was drawn up cosily and the tea things stood ready at the sitter’s elbow, the very sugar already in the cup. Several books rested on a shelf; one lay open beside the tea things, and Utterson was amazed to find it was a copy of a pious work for which Jekyll had several times expressed great esteem. It was annotated in his own handwriting with startling blasphemies.

Next, as they continued their review of the chamber, the searchers came to the cheval-glass and looked into its depths with involuntary horror. But it was angled so that it showed them nothing but the rosy glow playing across the ceiling, the fire sparkling in a hundred reflections along the glazed cabinet fronts, and their own pale, fearful faces as they leaned in to look.

“This glass has seen some strange things, sir,” whispered Poole.

“And surely none stranger than itself,” echoed the lawyer in the same tones. “For what did Jekyll”—he startled at the name, then mastered his weakness—“what could Jekyll want with it?” he said.

“You may well say that!” said Poole.

Next they turned to the business table. On the desk, among a neat array of papers, a large envelope lay uppermost and bore the name of Mr. Utterson in the doctor’s hand. The lawyer unsealed it, and several enclosures fell to the floor. The first was a will, drawn up in the same eccentric terms as the one he had returned six months before, to serve as a testament in case of death and as a deed of gift in case of disappearance. But in place of the name of Edward Hyde, the lawyer read, with indescribable amazement, the name of Gabriel John Utterson. He looked at Poole, then back at the paper, and finally at the dead criminal stretched out upon the carpet.

“My head spins,” he said. “He has been in possession all these days; he had no reason to like me; he must have raged to see himself displaced—and yet he has not destroyed this document.”

He snatched up the next paper; it was a brief note in the doctor’s hand and dated at the top. “O Poole!” the lawyer cried, “he was alive and here this very day. He cannot have been disposed of in so short a time; he must still be alive—he must have fled! And then, why flee? And how? And in that case, can we venture to declare this a suicide? O, we must be careful. I foresee that we may yet involve your master in some dire catastrophe.”

“Why don’t you read it, sir?” asked Poole.

“Because I am afraid,” replied the lawyer solemnly. “God grant I have no reason to be!” And with that he raised the paper to his eyes and read as follows:

“My dear Utterson,—When this falls into your hands, I shall have disappeared. I cannot foresee under what exact circumstances, but my instinct and all the circumstances of my nameless situation tell me that the end is certain and must come early. Go then, and first read the narrative which Lanyon warned me he was to place in your hands; and if you care to hear more, turn to the confession of

“Your unworthy and unhappy friend,

“HENRY JEKYLL.”

“There was a third enclosure?” asked Utterson.

“Here, sir,” said Poole, and placed in his hands a sizable packet sealed in several places.

The lawyer put it in his pocket. “I would say nothing of this paper. If your master has fled or is dead, we may at least protect his reputation. It is now ten o’clock; I must go home and read these documents in quiet, but I shall be back before midnight, when we will send for the police.”

They went out, locking the door of the theatre behind them. Utterson once more left the servants gathered around the fire in the hall. He trudged back to his office to read the two narratives that would now explain the mystery.

###CHAPTER### Dr. Lanyon’s Narrative

On the ninth of January, just four days ago, the evening post brought me a registered envelope. It was addressed in the handwriting of my colleague and old school friend, Henry Jekyll. I was quite surprised. We rarely wrote to each other; indeed, I had dined with him the night before. I could think of nothing in our dealings that would justify sending a registered letter. The contents increased my astonishment, for this is what the letter said:

“10th December, 18—.

“Dear Lanyon, You are one of my oldest friends. Although we have sometimes disagreed on scientific matters, I cannot remember, at least on my side, any coldness in our affection. There was never a day when, if you had said to me, ‘Jekyll, my life, my honour, my reason, depend upon you,’ I would not have sacrificed my left hand to help you. Lanyon, my life, my honour, my reason, are all at your mercy. If you fail me tonight, I am lost. After this introduction, you might suppose I am going to ask you for something dishonourable. Judge for yourself.

“I want you to postpone all other engagements for tonight—yes, even if you were summoned to the bedside of an emperor. Take a cab (unless your carriage is actually at the door), and with this letter in your hand for guidance, drive straight to my house. Poole, my butler, has his orders. You will find him waiting for you with a locksmith. The door of my private study is then to be forced open. You are to go in alone, open the glass-fronted cabinet (marked E) on the left, breaking the lock if it is shut, and pull out, with all its contents as they stand, the fourth drawer from the top (or the third from the bottom). In my extreme distress of mind, I have a morbid fear of giving you the wrong directions; but even if I am mistaken, you can identify the correct drawer by its contents: some powders, a small bottle, and a paper notebook. I beg you to carry this drawer back with you to Cavendish Square exactly as it is.

“That is the first part of my request. Now for the second. If you set out at once on receiving this, you should be back long before midnight. But I will allow you that margin, not only because I fear obstacles that cannot be prevented or foreseen, but also because an hour when your servants are in bed is better suited for what remains to be done. At midnight, then, I must ask you to be alone in your consulting room, to admit into the house with your own hand a man who will present himself in my name, and to place in his hands the drawer you will have brought from my study. Then you will have played your part and earned my full gratitude. Five minutes later, if you insist on an explanation, you will understand that these arrangements are of vital importance, and that by neglecting even one of them, however fantastic they seem, you might have charged your conscience with my death or the shipwreck of my reason.

“Though I am confident you will not treat this appeal lightly, my heart sinks and my hand trembles at the bare thought of such a possibility. Think of me at this hour, in a strange place, labouring under a blackness of distress that no imagination can exaggerate, and yet well aware that, if you will only serve me promptly, my troubles will roll away like a story that is told. Serve me, my dear Lanyon, and save

“Your friend,

“H.J.

P.S. I had already sealed this when a fresh terror struck my soul. It is possible the post office may fail me, and this letter may not reach you until tomorrow morning. In that case, dear Lanyon, do my errand whenever it is most convenient for you during the day; and once more expect my messenger at midnight. By then it may already be too late; and if that night passes without event, you will know that you have seen the last of Henry Jekyll.

Upon reading this letter, I was sure my colleague was insane. But until that was proved beyond all possible doubt, I felt bound to do as he asked. The less I understood this jumble, the less I could judge its importance; and an appeal worded like that could not be set aside without grave responsibility. I accordingly rose from the table, got into a hansom, and drove straight to Jekyll’s house. The butler was waiting for my arrival. He had received a registered letter of instruction by the same post as mine, and had sent at once for a locksmith and a carpenter. The tradesmen arrived while we were still speaking. We moved together to old Dr. Denman’s surgical theatre, from which (as you are no doubt aware) Jekyll’s private cabinet is most conveniently entered. The door was very strong, the lock excellent. The carpenter declared he would have great trouble and would have to do a lot of damage if force were used; and the locksmith was near despair. But this last was a handy fellow, and after two hours’ work, the door stood open. The press marked E was unlocked. I took out the drawer, had it filled with straw and tied in a sheet, and returned with it to Cavendish Square.

Here I proceeded to examine its contents. The powders were neatly enough made up, but not with the precision of a dispensing chemist. It was plain they were of Jekyll’s private manufacture. When I opened one of the wrappers, I found what seemed to me a simple white crystalline salt. The phial, which I next examined, was about half full of a blood-red liquid. It was highly pungent to smell and seemed to me to contain phosphorus and some volatile ether. I could make no guess at the other ingredients. The book was an ordinary notebook and contained little but a series of dates. These covered a period of many years, but I noticed that the entries had ceased almost a year ago and quite abruptly. Here and there a brief remark was added to a date, usually no more than a single word: “double” occurring perhaps six times in a total of several hundred entries. Once, very early in the list and followed by several exclamation marks, was “total failure!!!” All this, though it whetted my curiosity, told me little that was definite. Here were a phial of some salt, and the record of a series of experiments that had led—like too many of Jekyll’s investigations—to no practical use. How could the presence of these items in my house affect the honour, sanity, or life of my flighty colleague? If his messenger could go to one place, why could he not go to another? And even if there was some impediment, why was this gentleman to be received by me in secret? The more I reflected, the more convinced I grew that I was dealing with a case of cerebral disease. Although I dismissed my servants to bed, I loaded an old revolver so that I might be found in some posture of self-defence.

Scarcely had twelve o’clock rung out over London when the knocker sounded very gently on the door. I went myself at the summons, and found a small man crouching against the pillars of the portico.

“Have you come from Dr. Jekyll?” I asked.

He signalled “yes” with a constrained gesture; and when I asked him to enter, he would not obey without a searching backward glance into the darkness of the square. Not far off, a policeman was advancing with his bull’s-eye lantern open; and at the sight, I thought my visitor started and made greater haste.

These particulars struck me, I confess, disagreeably; and as I followed him into the bright light of the consulting room, I kept my hand ready on my weapon. Here, at last, I had a chance of clearly seeing him. I had never set eyes on him before, so much was certain. He was small, as I have said; I was struck besides with the shocking expression on his face, with his remarkable combination of great muscular activity and great apparent debility of constitution, and—last but not least—with the odd, subjective disturbance caused by his neighbourhood. This bore some resemblance to incipient rigor, and was accompanied by a marked sinking of the pulse. At the time, I set it down to some idiosyncratic, personal distaste, and merely wondered at the acuteness of the symptoms; but I have since had reason to believe the cause lies much deeper in the nature of man, and turns on some nobler hinge than the principle of hatred.

This person (who had thus, from the first moment of his entrance, aroused in me what I can only describe as a disgustful curiosity) was dressed in a fashion that would have made an ordinary person laughable; that is to say, his clothes, although of rich and sober fabric, were enormously too large for him in every measurement—the trousers hanging on his legs and rolled up to keep them from the ground, the waist of the coat below his haunches, and the collar sprawling wide upon his shoulders. Strange to relate, this ludicrous accoutrement was far from moving me to laughter. Rather, as there was something abnormal and misbegotten in the very essence of the creature that now faced me—something seizing, surprising and revolting—this fresh disparity seemed but to fit in with and to reinforce it; so that to my interest in the man’s nature and character, there was added a curiosity as to his origin, his life, his fortune and status in the world.

These observations, though they have taken so great a space to set down, were yet the work of a few seconds. My visitor was, indeed, on fire with sombre excitement.

“Have you got it?” he cried. “Have you got it?” And so lively was his impatience that he even laid his hand upon my arm and sought to shake me.

I put him back, conscious at his touch of a certain icy pang along my blood. “Come, sir,” said I. “You forget that I have not yet the pleasure of your acquaintance. Be seated, if you please.” And I showed him an example, and sat down myself in my customary seat, mustering as fair an imitation of my ordinary manner to a patient as the lateness of the hour, the nature of my preoccupations, and the horror I had of my visitor would suffer me to muster.

“I beg your pardon, Dr. Lanyon,” he replied politely enough. “What you say is perfectly reasonable; my impatience has gotten the better of my manners. I come here at the request of your colleague, Dr. Henry Jekyll, on a matter of some importance; and I understood…” He paused and put his hand to his throat, and I could see, despite his composed manner, that he was struggling against the onset of hysteria—“I understood, a drawer…”

But here I took pity on my visitor’s suspense, and perhaps a little on my own growing curiosity.

“There it is, sir,” I said, pointing to the drawer, which lay on the floor behind a table and was still covered with the sheet.

He sprang to it, then paused, and laid his hand upon his heart; I could hear his teeth grind with the convulsive movement of his jaws; his face was so ghastly that I grew alarmed for both his life and his reason.

“Compose yourself,” I said.

He turned a dreadful smile on me, and as if with the decision of despair, snatched away the sheet. At the sight of the contents, he let out one loud sob of such immense relief that I sat petrified. And the next moment, in a voice that was already fairly well under control, he asked, “Do you have a graduated glass?”

I rose from my chair with some effort and gave him what he asked.

He thanked me with a smiling nod, measured out a few minims of the red tincture and added one of the powders. The mixture, at first a reddish hue, began, as the crystals melted, to brighten in colour, to effervesce audibly, and to throw off small fumes of vapour. Suddenly, and at the same moment, the boiling ceased and the compound changed to a dark purple, which then faded more slowly to a watery green. My visitor, who had watched these transformations with a sharp eye, smiled, set the glass down on the table, and then turned and looked at me with a scrutinizing air.

“And now,” he said, “to settle what remains. Will you be wise? will you be guided? will you allow me to take this glass in my hand and to leave your house without further discussion? or has the greed of curiosity too great a hold on you? Think before you answer, for it shall be done as you decide. As you decide, you shall remain as you were before, and neither richer nor wiser, unless the sense of service rendered to a man in mortal distress may be counted as a kind of riches of the soul. Or, if you shall so prefer to choose otherwise, a new realm of knowledge and new avenues to fame and power shall be laid open to you, here, in this room, upon the instant; and your sight shall be blasted by a prodigy to stagger the unbelief of Satan.”

“Sir,” I said, affecting a coolness that I was far from truly possessing, “you speak in riddles, and you will perhaps not wonder that I listen with no very strong belief. But I have gone too far in the way of inexplicable services to stop before I see the end.”

“It is well,” replied my visitor. “Lanyon, you remember your vows: what follows is under the seal of our profession. And now, you who have so long been bound to the most narrow and material views, you who have denied the virtue of transcendental medicine, you who have mocked your superiors—behold!”

He raised the glass to his lips and drank it in one gulp. A cry followed; he reeled, staggered, clutched at the table and held on, staring with bloodshot eyes, gasping with open mouth. And as I watched, I thought I saw a change—he seemed to swell—his face turned suddenly black, and the features seemed to melt and alter. The next moment, I had sprung to my feet and leaped back against the wall, my arms raised to shield me from that monstrous sight, my mind drowning in terror.

“O God!” I screamed, and “O God!” again and again. For there before my eyes—pale and shaken, half fainting, and groping before him with his hands like a man restored from death—stood Henry Jekyll!

What he told me in the next hour I cannot bring myself to write down. I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard, and my soul sickened at it. Yet now, when that sight has faded from my eyes, I ask myself if I believe it, and I cannot answer. My life is shaken to its core; I cannot sleep; the most dreadful terror sits beside me at every hour of day and night. I feel that my days are numbered and I must die, and yet I shall die incredulous. As for the moral depravity that man unveiled to me, even with tears of repentance, I cannot, even in memory, dwell on it without a jolt of horror. I will say only one thing, Utterson, and that—if you can bring yourself to believe it—will be more than enough. The creature who crept into my house that night was, by Jekyll’s own confession, known by the name of Hyde and hunted in every corner of the land as the murderer of Carew.

HASTIE LANYON.

###CHAPTER### Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

I was born in the year 18— to a large fortune. I was also gifted with excellent abilities, naturally inclined to hard work, and fond of the respect of the wise and good among my peers. Thus, it might have been supposed, I had every guarantee of an honorable and distinguished future. Indeed, the worst of my faults was a certain restless cheerfulness of temperament—a quality that has made many people happy, but one I found hard to reconcile with my overbearing desire to hold my head high and wear a more than usually serious expression in public. Hence, I concealed my pleasures. And when I reached years of reflection, began to look around me, and took stock of my progress and position in the world, I found myself already committed to a deep double life. Many a man would have openly flaunted such irregularities as I was guilty of; but because of the high ideals I had set for myself, I regarded them with an almost morbid sense of shame and hid them away. Thus, it was the demanding nature of my aspirations, rather than any special degradation in my faults, that made me what I was. It dug an even deeper trench than in most men, cutting apart in me those provinces of good and evil that divide and combine man's dual nature. In this case, I was driven to reflect deeply and persistently on that hard law of life which lies at the root of religion and is one of the most abundant sources of distress. Though such a profound double-dealer, I was in no way a hypocrite. Both sides of me were completely sincere. I was no more myself when I cast off restraint and plunged into shame than when I worked, in the light of day, to advance knowledge or relieve sorrow and suffering. And it so happened that the direction of my scientific studies—which led entirely toward the mystic and the transcendental—reacted and cast a strong light on this awareness of the constant war within my members. Day by day, from both sides of my intelligence—the moral and the intellectual—I drew steadily nearer to that truth by whose partial discovery I have been doomed to such a dreadful shipwreck: that man is not truly one, but truly two. I say two, because my own knowledge does not go beyond that point. Others will follow, others will outstrip me along the same lines; and I venture the guess that man will ultimately be known as a mere community of diverse, conflicting, independent inhabitants. For my part, from the nature of my life, I advanced unfailingly in one direction and one direction only. It was on the moral side, and in my own person, that I learned to recognize the thorough and primitive duality of man. I saw that, of the two natures that contended in the field of my consciousness, even if I could rightly be said to be either, it was only because I was radically both. And from an early date, even before my scientific discoveries had begun to suggest the barest possibility of such a miracle, I had learned to dwell with pleasure, as on a beloved daydream, on the thought of separating these elements. If each, I told myself, could be housed in separate identities, life would be relieved of all that was unbearable. The unjust might go his way, delivered from the aspirations and remorse of his more upright twin; and the just could walk steadfastly and securely on his upward path, doing the good things in which he found his pleasure, and no longer exposed to disgrace and penitence at the hands of this external evil. It was the curse of mankind that these ill-matched bundles were bound together—that in the agonized womb of consciousness, these polar twins should be continuously struggling. How, then, were they to be separated?

I was deep in these reflections when, as I said, a sidelight began to shine on the subject from the laboratory table. I began to understand more deeply than ever before the trembling immateriality, the mist-like transience, of this seemingly solid body we walk around in. I found that certain agents have the power to shake and pull back that fleshy garment, just as a wind might toss the curtains of a pavilion. For two good reasons, I will not go deeply into this scientific part of my confession. First, I have been forced to learn that the doom and burden of our life is bound forever on man’s shoulders. When we try to cast it off, it only returns with a more unfamiliar and more awful pressure. Second, as my narrative will, alas, make all too evident, my discoveries were incomplete. Enough, then, to say that I not only recognized my natural body from the mere aura and radiance of certain powers that made up my spirit, but I also managed to compound a drug. This drug would dethrone those powers from their supremacy and substitute a second form and face. They were none the less natural to me because they were the expression, and bore the stamp, of lower elements in my soul.

I hesitated long before I put this theory to the test of practice. I knew well that I risked death. Any drug that so potently controlled and shook the very fortress of identity might, by the tiniest overdose or at the slightest wrong moment of taking it, utterly blot out that intangible tabernacle I hoped to change. But the temptation of a discovery so singular and profound at last overcame the warnings of alarm. I had long since prepared my tincture. I at once purchased from a firm of wholesale chemists a large quantity of a particular salt which I knew from my experiments to be the last ingredient required. Late one accursed night, I compounded the elements, watched them boil and smoke together in the glass, and when the boiling had subsided, with a strong glow of courage, drank off the potion.

The most racking pains followed: a grinding in the bones, deadly nausea, and a horror of the spirit that cannot be exceeded at the hour of birth or death. Then these agonies began swiftly to subside, and I came to myself as if out of a great sickness. There was something strange in my sensations, something indescribably new and, from its very novelty, incredibly sweet. I felt younger, lighter, happier in body. Within, I was conscious of a dizzying recklessness, a current of disordered sensual images running like a mill-race in my fancy, a dissolution of the bonds of obligation, an unknown but not an innocent freedom of the soul. I knew myself, at the first breath of this new life, to be more wicked, tenfold more wicked, sold a slave to my original evil. And the thought, in that moment, braced and delighted me like wine. I stretched out my hands, exulting in the freshness of these sensations; and in the act, I was suddenly aware that I had lost in stature.

At that time, there was no mirror in my room. The one that stands beside me as I write was brought there later, and for the very purpose of these transformations. The night, however, was far gone into the morning—the morning, black as it was, was nearly ripe for the birth of day. Everyone in my house was locked in the deepest hours of slumber. Flushed with hope and triumph, I decided to venture in my new shape as far as my bedroom. I crossed the yard, where the constellations looked down upon me—I could have thought, with wonder—as if I were the first creature of that sort their unsleeping vigilance had ever discovered. I stole through the corridors, a stranger in my own house. Coming to my room, I saw for the first time the appearance of Edward Hyde.

I must speak here by theory alone, saying not what I know, but what I suppose to be most probable. The evil side of my nature, to which I had now transferred the stamping force, was less robust and less developed than the good side I had just deposed. Moreover, my life had been, after all, nine-tenths a life of effort, virtue, and control; that evil side had been much less exercised and much less exhausted. And so, I think, it came about that Edward Hyde was so much smaller, slighter, and younger than Henry Jekyll. Just as goodness shone from the face of the one, evil was written broadly and plainly on the face of the other. Besides—I must still believe it is the lethal side of man—evil had left on that body an imprint of deformity and decay. And yet, when I looked upon that ugly idol in the glass, I felt no repugnance, rather a leap of welcome. This, too, was myself. It seemed natural and human. To my eyes it bore a livelier image of the spirit, more express and single, than the imperfect and divided face I had been used to calling mine. And in that I was doubtless right. I have observed that when I wore the semblance of Edward Hyde, no one could come near me at first without a visible misgiving of the flesh. This, I think, was because all human beings as we meet them are commingled of good and evil; and Edward Hyde, alone in the ranks of mankind, was pure evil.

I lingered only a moment at the mirror. The second and conclusive experiment had yet to be attempted. It still remained to be seen whether I had lost my identity beyond redemption and must flee before daylight from a house that was no longer mine. Hurrying back to my cabinet, I once more prepared and drank the cup, once more suffered the pangs of dissolution, and came to myself once more with the character, the stature, and the face of Henry Jekyll.

That night I had come to the fatal crossroads. If I had approached my discovery in a nobler spirit, if I had risked the experiment while under the influence of generous or pious aspirations, everything would have been different; and from these agonies of death and birth I would have come forth an angel instead of a fiend. The drug had no moral preference; it was neither satanic nor divine; it merely shook the doors of the prison of my nature, and like the captives of Philippi, what was chained within rushed out. At that time my virtue slumbered; my evil, kept awake by ambition, was alert and swift to seize the opportunity; and the thing that was brought forth was Edward Hyde. So, although I now had two characters as well as two appearances, one was wholly evil, and the other was still the old Henry Jekyll—that incongruous mixture whose reform and improvement I had already learned to despair of. The change was thus entirely for the worse.

Even at that time, I had not conquered my aversion to the dryness of a life of study. I would still be in a cheerful mood at times; and as my pleasures were (to say the least) undignified, and I was not only well known and highly respected but also growing into an elderly man, this inconsistency in my life was daily becoming more unwelcome. It was in this direction that my new power tempted me until I fell into slavery. I had only to drink the potion, to cast off at once the body of the noted professor, and to put on, like a thick cloak, that of Edward Hyde. I smiled at the notion; it seemed amusing to me at the time; and I made my preparations with the most meticulous care. I took and furnished that house in Soho, to which Hyde was tracked by the police, and hired as a housekeeper a creature I knew to be both silent and unscrupulous. On the other hand, I announced to my servants that a Mr. Hyde (whom I described) was to have full freedom and authority in my house in the square; and to ward off mishaps, I even visited and made myself a familiar sight in my second character. Next I drew up that will to which you so strongly objected; so that if anything happened to me in the person of Dr. Jekyll, I could step into the person of Edward Hyde without financial loss. And thus fortified, as I thought, on every side, I began to profit by the strange protections of my position.

Men have hired assassins before to carry out their crimes, while their own person and reputation remained safe. I was the first who ever did so for his pleasures. I was the first who could plod in the public eye carrying a load of genial respectability, and in a moment, like a schoolboy, strip off these borrowed garments and plunge headlong into a sea of freedom. But for me, wrapped in my impenetrable cloak, safety was complete. Think of it—I did not even exist! Let me just slip through my laboratory door, give me only a second or two to mix and swallow the potion I always kept ready; and whatever Hyde had done, Edward Hyde would pass away like the fog of breath on a mirror; and in his place, quietly at home, trimming the midnight lamp in his study, a man who could afford to laugh at suspicion would be Henry Jekyll.

The pleasures I hurried to seek in my disguise were, as I have said, lacking dignity; I would scarcely use a harsher word. But in the hands of Edward Hyde, they soon began to turn monstrous. When I returned from these outings, I was often plunged into a kind of wonder at the depravity I had experienced by proxy. This familiar spirit that I had called out of my own soul, and sent out alone to do as he pleased, was a being inherently evil and villainous. His every act and thought centered on himself; he drank pleasure with animal-like greed from any degree of cruelty, no matter how extreme; he was as relentless as a man of stone. Henry Jekyll was at times horrified by the acts of Edward Hyde; but the situation was beyond ordinary moral laws, and it stealthily loosened the grip of conscience. It was Hyde, after all, and Hyde alone, who was guilty. Jekyll was no worse; he woke up again with his good qualities seemingly undamaged. He would even hurry, where possible, to undo the evil Hyde had done. And so his conscience slumbered.

I have no intention of going into the details of the disgraceful acts I secretly allowed (for even now I can scarcely admit that I committed them). I only want to point out the warnings and the gradual steps by which my punishment approached. I encountered one incident that, since it brought no consequences, I will do no more than mention. An act of cruelty to a child stirred the anger of a passer-by, whom I recognized the other day as your relative. The doctor and the child's family joined him. There were moments when I feared for my life. In the end, to calm their very justified anger, Edward Hyde had to bring them to the door and pay them with a cheque drawn in Henry Jekyll's name. But this danger was easily removed for the future. I opened an account at another bank in the name of Edward Hyde himself. By slanting my own handwriting backwards, I supplied my double with a signature. Then I thought I sat beyond the reach of fate.

About two months before the murder of Sir Danvers, I had gone out on one of my adventures, returned late, and woke up the next morning in bed with rather odd sensations. I looked around me in vain. I saw the decent furniture and the tall proportions of my room on the square, and I recognized the pattern of the bed curtains and the design of the mahogany frame—all in vain. Something kept insisting that I was not where I was, that I had not woken up where I seemed to be, but in the little room in Soho where I was used to sleeping in the body of Edward Hyde. I smiled to myself, and in my analytical way, I began lazily to examine the elements of this illusion, sometimes even drifting back into a comfortable morning doze as I did so. I was still doing this when, in a more wakeful moment, my eyes fell on my hand. Now, as you have often remarked, the hand of Henry Jekyll was professional in shape and size: large, firm, white and handsome. But the hand I now saw, clearly, in the yellow light of a mid-London morning, lying half-closed on the bedclothes, was lean, sinewy, knuckly, with a dark pallor and thickly shaded with a swarthy growth of hair. It was the hand of Edward Hyde.

I must have stared at it for nearly half a minute, lost in a blank stupor of amazement, before terror woke in my breast as sudden and startling as the crash of cymbals; leaping from my bed, I rushed to the mirror. At the sight that met my eyes, my blood turned to something exquisitely thin and icy. Yes, I had gone to bed Henry Jekyll, I had awakened Edward Hyde. How could this be explained? I asked myself. Then, with another surge of terror—how could it be remedied? It was well into the morning; the servants were up; all my drugs were in the cabinet—a long journey: down two flights of stairs, through the back passage, across the open court, and through the anatomical theatre, from where I stood horror-struck. It might indeed be possible to cover my face; but what use was that, when I could not hide the alteration in my stature? Then, with an overwhelming sweetness of relief, I remembered that the servants were already used to the coming and going of my second self. I soon dressed, as well as I was able, in clothes of my own size; I soon passed through the house, where Bradshaw stared and drew back at seeing Mr. Hyde at such an hour and in such strange attire; and ten minutes later, Dr. Jekyll had returned to his own shape and was sitting down, with a darkened brow, to pretend to eat breakfast.

My appetite was very small. This inexplicable incident, this reversal of my previous experience, seemed, like the Babylonian finger on the wall, to be spelling out the letters of my judgment. I began to reflect more seriously than ever before on the issues and possibilities of my double existence. That part of me which I had the power to project had lately been much exercised and nourished. Lately, it had seemed to me as though the body of Edward Hyde had grown in stature, as though (when I wore that form) I were conscious of a more generous tide of blood. I began to see a danger that, if this continued much longer, the balance of my nature might be permanently overthrown, the power of voluntary change forfeited, and the character of Edward Hyde become irrevocably mine. The power of the drug had not always been equally displayed. Once, very early in my career, it had totally failed me; since then I had been obliged on more than one occasion to double the amount, and once, with infinite risk of death, to treble it; and these rare uncertainties had until now cast the only shadow on my contentment. Now, however, in the light of that morning's accident, I was led to observe that whereas, in the beginning, the difficulty had been to throw off the body of Jekyll, it had of late gradually but decidedly transferred itself to the other side. Everything therefore seemed to point to this: I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self, and becoming slowly merged with my second and worse.

Between these two, I now felt I had to choose. My two natures shared memory, but all other faculties were very unequally divided between them. Jekyll, who was a composite, sometimes with the most delicate awareness, and sometimes with greedy relish, threw himself into and shared the pleasures and adventures of Hyde. But Hyde was indifferent to Jekyll, or only remembered him as a mountain bandit remembers the cave where he hides from pursuit. Jekyll had more than a father’s concern; Hyde had more than a son’s indifference. To side with Jekyll was to give up those appetites I had long secretly indulged and lately begun to pamper. To side with Hyde was to give up a thousand interests and aspirations, and to become, at a stroke and forever, despised and friendless. The trade-off might seem one-sided. Yet there was another factor to weigh: while Jekyll would suffer painfully in the torments of self-denial, Hyde would not even be conscious of all he had lost. Strange as my circumstances were, the terms of this debate are as old and commonplace as humankind. Much the same temptations and fears cast the die for any tempted and trembling sinner. And it turned out for me, as it does for the vast majority of my fellows, that I chose the better path and was found lacking in the strength to keep to it.

Yes, I preferred the aging and discontented doctor, surrounded by friends and cherishing decent hopes, and I resolutely said goodbye to the liberty, the relative youth, the light step, the leaping impulses and secret pleasures I had enjoyed in the disguise of Hyde. I made this choice perhaps with some unconscious reservation, for I neither gave up the house in Soho nor destroyed Edward Hyde’s clothes, which still lay ready in my cabinet. For two months, however, I was true to my determination; for two months I led a life of such severity as I had never before achieved, and I enjoyed the compensations of an approving conscience. But at last time began to erase the freshness of my alarm; the praises of conscience began to grow routine; I began to be tortured with throes and longings, as of Hyde struggling after freedom. And at last, in an hour of moral weakness, I once again compounded and swallowed the transforming draught.

I do not suppose that a drunkard, when he reasons with himself about his vice, is affected once in five hundred times by the dangers he runs because of his brutish, physical numbness. Neither had I, long as I had considered my position, made enough allowance for the complete moral numbness and unfeeling readiness for evil that were Edward Hyde's leading traits. Yet it was by these that I was punished. My devil had been long caged; he came out roaring. Even as I took the potion, I was conscious of a more unbridled, more furious urge toward evil. It must have been this, I suppose, that stirred in my soul the storm of impatience with which I listened to the polite words of my unhappy victim. I declare, at least before God, that no morally sane man could have been guilty of such a crime on so pitiful a provocation, and that I struck in no more reasonable spirit than that in which a sick child might break a plaything. But I had willingly stripped myself of all those balancing instincts by which even the worst of us walks with some steadiness among temptations. In my case, to be tempted, however slightly, was to fall.

Instantly the spirit of hell awoke in me and raged. With an ecstasy of glee, I battered the unresisting body, tasting delight from every blow. It was not until weariness had begun to set in that I was suddenly, at the height of my frenzy, struck through the heart by a cold thrill of terror. A mist cleared; I saw my life was forfeit, and fled from the scene of these excesses, at once exulting and trembling, my lust for evil gratified and stimulated, my love of life wound up to the highest pitch. I ran to the house in Soho, and (to make assurance doubly sure) destroyed my papers. Thence I set out through the lamplit streets, in the same divided ecstasy of mind, gloating on my crime, light-headedly devising others in the future, and yet still hurrying and still listening behind me for the footsteps of the avenger. Hyde had a song on his lips as he mixed the potion, and as he drank it, he toasted the dead man. The pangs of transformation had not finished tearing through him before Henry Jekyll, with streaming tears of gratitude and remorse, fell upon his knees and lifted his clasped hands to God. The veil of self-indulgence was torn from head to foot. I saw my life as a whole: I followed it up from the days of childhood, when I had walked holding my father’s hand, and through the self-denying toils of my professional life, to arrive again and again, with the same sense of unreality, at the damned horrors of the evening. I could have screamed aloud; I sought with tears and prayers to smother the crowd of hideous images and sounds with which my memory swarmed against me. And still, between the prayers, the ugly face of my iniquity stared into my soul. As the sharpness of this remorse began to die away, it was succeeded by a sense of joy. The problem of my conduct was solved. Hyde was from then on impossible; whether I wanted to or not, I was now confined to the better part of my being. Oh, how I rejoiced to think of it! With what willing humility I embraced once more the restrictions of a normal life! With what heartfelt renunciation I locked the door by which I had so often gone and come, and ground the key under my heel!

The next day, news came that the murder had not been overlooked. Hyde's guilt was obvious to everyone, and the victim was a man highly respected by the public. It was not only a crime—it had been a tragic folly. I think I was glad to know this. I think I was glad that my better impulses were now supported and guarded by the fear of the gallows. Jekyll was now my city of refuge; if Hyde so much as peeped out for an instant, the hands of all men would be raised to seize and kill him.

I resolved to redeem the past by my future conduct, and I can honestly say that my resolve did some good. You know yourself how earnestly I worked to relieve suffering in the last months of last year. You know that much was done for others, and that the days passed quietly, almost happily, for me. I cannot truly say that I grew tired of this good and innocent life; I think instead that I enjoyed it more fully every day. But I was still cursed with my divided purpose. As the first sharpness of my penitence wore off, the lower side of me—so long indulged, so recently chained down—began to growl for freedom. Not that I dreamed of bringing Hyde back to life; the bare idea of that would startle me into a frenzy. No, it was in my own person that I was once more tempted to toy with my conscience. And it was as an ordinary, secret sinner that I at last fell to the assaults of temptation.

All things come to an end; the largest measure is filled at last. This brief indulgence of my evil finally destroyed the balance of my soul. And yet I was not alarmed; the fall seemed natural, like a return to the old days before I made my discovery. It was a fine, clear January day, wet underfoot where the frost had melted but cloudless overhead. Regent's Park was full of winter chirrupings and sweet with spring scents. I sat in the sun on a bench; the animal within me licked the chops of memory; the spiritual side was a little drowsy, promising future penitence but not yet moved to begin. After all, I reflected, I was like my neighbours. Then I smiled, comparing myself with other men, comparing my active good-will with the lazy cruelty of their neglect. At the very moment of that vain-glorious thought, a qualm came over me—a horrid nausea and a most deadly shuddering. These passed away and left me faint. Then, as faintness subsided, I began to notice a change in the temper of my thoughts: a greater boldness, a contempt of danger, a loosening of the bonds of obligation. I looked down; my clothes hung shapelessly on my shrunken limbs; the hand on my knee was corded and hairy. I was once more Edward Hyde. A moment before I had been secure in the respect of all, wealthy, beloved—the table laid for me in the dining room at home. Now I was the common quarry of mankind, hunted, houseless, a known murderer, bound for the gallows.

My reason wavered, but it did not entirely fail me. I have often noticed that in my second character my faculties seemed sharper and my spirits more tightly strung; so, where Jekyll might have given way, Hyde rose to meet the urgency of the moment. My drugs were in a cupboard of my cabinet. How was I to reach them? That was the problem I set myself to solve, pressing my hands to my temples. I had closed the laboratory door. If I tried to enter through the house, my own servants would send me to the gallows. I saw that I must use another person, and thought of Lanyon. How could he be reached? How persuaded? Even if I escaped capture in the streets, how could I get into his presence? And how should I—an unknown, repulsive visitor—prevail on the famous doctor to ransack the study of his colleague, Dr. Jekyll? Then I remembered that one part of my original character remained to me: I could write in my own hand. Once I had seized on that kindling spark, the path I must follow lit up from end to end.

Then I straightened my clothes as well as I could, hailed a passing hansom, and drove to a hotel in Portland Street whose name I happened to remember. At my appearance (which was indeed comical, however tragic a fate these garments covered) the driver could not hide his amusement. I gnashed my teeth at him with a rush of devilish fury, and the smile withered from his face—luckily for him, and even luckier for myself, for in another instant I would certainly have dragged him from his perch. At the inn, as I entered, I cast so black a look around me that the attendants trembled; they did not exchange a single glance in my presence, but meekly took my orders, led me to a private room, and brought me what I needed to write. Hyde in danger of his life was a creature new to me: shaken with frantic anger, wound up to the pitch of murder, craving to inflict pain. Yet the creature was cunning; he mastered his fury with a great effort of will. He composed his two important letters, one to Lanyon and one to Poole, and to have actual proof they were sent he dispatched them with instructions that they be registered. Afterwards, he sat all day over the fire in the private room, gnawing his nails; there he dined, sitting alone with his fears, the waiter visibly quailing under his eye; and from there, when night had fully come, he set off huddled in the corner of a closed cab, driven to and fro about the city streets. He, I say—I cannot say, I. That child of Hell had nothing human; nothing lived in him but fear and hatred. And when at last, thinking the driver had begun to grow suspicious, he discharged the cab and ventured on foot, dressed in his misfitting clothes, an object marked for every observer, into the midst of the night-time crowd—these two base passions raged within him like a tempest. He walked fast, hunted by his fears, chattering to himself, skulking through the less frequented thoroughfares, counting the minutes that still separated him from midnight. Once a woman spoke to him, offering, I think, a box of lights. He smote her in the face, and she fled.

When I came to myself at Lanyon’s, the horror of my old friend perhaps affected me somewhat. I do not know; it was at least only a drop in the ocean compared to the abhorrence with which I looked back upon those hours. A change had come over me. It was no longer the fear of the gallows. It was the horror of being Hyde that racked me. I received Lanyon’s condemnation partly in a dream. It was also partly in a dream that I came home to my own house and got into bed. After the exhaustion of the day, I slept a deep, profound sleep that not even the nightmares tormenting me could break. I awoke in the morning shaken, weakened, but refreshed. I still hated and feared the thought of the brute that slept within me. I had not, of course, forgotten the appalling dangers of the day before. But I was once more at home, in my own house and close to my drugs; and gratitude for my escape shone so brightly in my soul that it almost rivalled the brightness of hope.

I was stepping leisurely across the court after breakfast, enjoying the chill of the air, when I was suddenly seized again by those indescribable sensations that signaled the change. I had barely enough time to reach the shelter of my cabinet before I was once again raging and freezing with Hyde’s passions. This time it took a double dose to bring me back to myself; and alas, six hours later, as I sat sadly staring into the fire, the pangs returned and I had to take the drug again. In short, from that day on, only by a great effort, as if doing gymnastics, and only under the immediate stimulation of the drug, could I wear Jekyll’s face. At all hours of the day and night, I would be seized by that warning shudder; above all, if I slept, or even dozed for a moment in my chair, I always awoke as Hyde. Under the strain of this constantly impending doom and the sleeplessness I now condemned myself to—yes, beyond what I thought humanly possible—I became, in my own person, a creature eaten up and hollowed out by fever, languidly weak in both body and mind, and solely occupied by one thought: the horror of my other self. But when I slept, or when the medicine’s power wore off, I would leap almost without transition (for the pangs of transformation grew less marked each day) into a mind brimming with images of terror, a soul boiling with causeless hatreds, and a body that seemed not strong enough to contain the raging energies of life. Hyde’s powers seemed to have grown with Jekyll’s sickliness. And certainly the hatred that now divided them was equal on both sides. For Jekyll, it was a matter of vital instinct. He had now seen the full deformity of that creature who shared with him some phenomena of consciousness and was co-heir with him to death; and beyond these shared links, which in themselves caused the most acute part of his distress, he thought of Hyde, for all his vital energy, as something not only hellish but inorganic. This was the shocking thing: that the slime of the pit seemed to utter cries and voices; that the shapeless dust gesticulated and sinned; that what was dead, and had no form, should seize the offices of life. And this too: that this insurgent horror was knit to him closer than a wife, closer than an eye; lay caged in his flesh, where he heard it mutter and felt it struggle to be born; and at every hour of weakness, and in the trusting depths of slumber, it prevailed against him and thrust him out of life. Hyde’s hatred for Jekyll was of a different kind. His terror of the gallows continually drove him to commit temporary suicide and return to his subordinate place as a part rather than a person; but he loathed the necessity, he loathed the despondency into which Jekyll had now fallen, and he resented the dislike with which he was regarded. Hence the ape-like tricks he would play on me: scrawling blasphemies in my own hand on the pages of my books, burning letters, and destroying my father’s portrait; and indeed, if not for his fear of death, he would long ago have ruined himself merely to drag me down with him. But his love of life is astonishing. I go further: I, who sicken and freeze at the very thought of him, when I recall the abjectness and passion of this attachment, and when I know how he fears my power to cut him off by suicide, I find it in my heart to pity him.

It is useless to prolong this description, and time fails me terribly. No one has ever suffered such torments; let that be enough. Yet even to these, habit brought—not relief—but a certain callousness of soul, a certain despairing acceptance. My punishment might have gone on for years, but for the last calamity which has now fallen and which has finally severed me from my own face and nature. My supply of the salt, which I had never renewed since the first experiment, began to run low. I sent out for a fresh supply and mixed the draught; the mixture boiled up, and the first colour change happened, but not the second. I drank it, and it had no effect. You will learn from Poole how I had all London searched; it was in vain. I am now convinced that my first supply was impure, and that the unknown impurity gave the draught its power.

About a week has passed, and I am now finishing this statement under the influence of the last of the old powders. So this is the last time—short of a miracle—that Henry Jekyll can think his own thoughts or see his own face (now how sadly altered!) in the mirror. I must not delay too long in finishing this writing; for if my narrative has escaped destruction so far, it is only by a combination of great prudence and great good luck. Should the pangs of transformation seize me while writing, Hyde will tear it to pieces; but if some time passes after I set it aside, his extreme selfishness and narrow focus on the moment will probably save it once again from his ape-like spite. And indeed the doom closing in on us both has already changed and crushed him. Half an hour from now, when I once more and forever take on that hated personality, I know how I shall sit shuddering and weeping in my chair, or pace up and down this room (my last earthly refuge) with a strained and terrified ecstasy of listening, catching every sound of menace. Will Hyde die on the scaffold? Or will he find the courage to take his own life at the last moment? God knows; I am careless; this is my true hour of death, and what follows concerns another than myself. Here then, as I lay down the pen and proceed to seal up my confession, I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.
