TITLE: The Mirror Sisters
AUTHOR: Unbekannt
DESCRIPTION: Two sisters in an old house on Gloucester Lane notice that their reflections in the hall mirror are no longer keeping time.

## Chapter One: The Glass in the Hall

The house at the end of Gloucester Lane had belonged to the Thwaite family for three generations, and in all that time nobody had dared to move the mirror in the hall. It hung between the coat hooks and the barometer, tall and narrow, its silver worn thin at the edges like frost that had begun to melt. Visitors said it made the hall look longer. The sisters said nothing about it at all, which in that house amounted to a confession.

Imogen Thwaite was the elder by eleven minutes, and she had never once let Rosalind forget it. She kept the accounts, wound the clocks on Sundays, and answered letters from Worcester solicitors in a hand so upright it seemed to stand to attention. Rosalind, who preferred to be called Ros, kept nothing at all except a jar of sea glass and a habit of talking to the cat as if it were a magistrate.

On the first wet evening of October, Ros came in from the garden with her hair dripping and stopped dead in front of the mirror.

"Imogen," she said. "Come here. Quickly."

"I am in the middle of the gas bill," Imogen called from the study. "Whatever it is, it can wait for the gas bill."

"It can't. She's moving."

Imogen put down her pen. There was only one "she" in that house who could be said to move inside a mirror, and the sisters had agreed, years ago, never to speak of her. She walked into the hall with the deliberate calm of a woman who intends to prove her younger sister wrong.

The glass showed the hall, the coat hooks, the barometer, and the two of them standing side by side: one sister neat and grey-cardiganed, the other muddy and bright-eyed. That was all. Then the reflection of Ros lifted a hand, a fraction of a second after Ros had lifted hers.

"Did you see that?" Ros whispered.

"I saw you wave at yourself," Imogen said. "It is not a crime, merely a waste of an evening."

"She was late. My reflection was late, Imogen. Like a bad telephone line."

Imogen looked again, longer this time. The narrator of this story will admit what Imogen would not: for a moment, the reflected Imogen did not blink when the real one did. It simply watched, patient and slightly amused, as though it knew the ending of a book that the real Imogen had only just begun.

"Grandmother used to say the glass came from a house in Leicester," Imogen said slowly. "She said it remembered things."

"She also said the cat could predict the weather."

"The cat can predict the weather. It sleeps under the stairs when it is about to rain."

Ros laughed, and the laugh echoed down the hall in a way that sounded, to both of them, faintly like two laughs. She stepped closer to the glass until her breath clouded it.

"Do you remember what Grandmother read to us?" she asked. "From that green book with the chessboard on the cover?"

Imogen remembered. Their grandmother had sat in the window seat with Through the Looking-Glass open on her knee, and she had always paused at the same line, tapping the page with one finger: "It's a poor sort of memory that only works backwards." Then she would look at the mirror in the hall and say nothing more.

"It was a story for children," Imogen said.

"Everything is a story for children until it happens to you."

The rain grew heavier. Somewhere in the house a clock struck seven, and then, very faintly, a second clock struck seven a moment later, although there was only one clock in the hall. Imogen took her sister by the wrist.

"We are going to have supper," she said firmly, "and tomorrow I am going to telephone Mr. Ashworth."

"The glazier? You think the mirror needs mending?"

"I think," said Imogen, "that the mirror needs a second opinion."

Behind them, in the glass, the two reflected sisters did not turn to go. They stayed exactly where they were, side by side, and waited.

## Chapter Two: Mr. Ashworth Calls

Mr. Gideon Ashworth arrived the next morning in a van that said Ashworth and Sons, Glaziers, Worcester, although there had been no sons for twenty years and the van had not seen Worcester since the spring. He was a broad, quiet man with a pencil behind his ear and the careful hands of someone who spends his life near things that break.

"Morning, Miss Thwaite," he said to Imogen at the door. Then, to Ros, who was peering over her sister's shoulder: "And Miss Thwaite."

"You may call us Imogen and Rosalind," Imogen said. "It saves confusion."

"It saves nothing," Ros said. "He has called us both Miss Thwaite since we were nine."

Mr. Ashworth smiled at that, but the smile faded when he saw the mirror. He set down his toolbox very gently, as if the floor might object, and stood in front of the glass for a long time without speaking.

"Where did this come from?" he asked at last.

"Leicester," said Imogen. "Before the war. Our grandmother brought it."

"Not Leicester," Mr. Ashworth said. "Not originally. This is old Venetian work, mercury backing, very fine. My father told me about a glass like this once. He said a family in Shrewsbury had one, and they sold it because the children kept talking to it."

Ros glanced at her sister. Imogen kept her eyes on the glazier.

"And what did the children say?" Ros asked.

"Nobody knew. That was the trouble. They only ever talked to it when they were alone." He crouched and ran a fingertip along the bottom of the frame, where the gilt had worn away. "Has it been doing anything unusual?"

"It has been late," Ros said.

"Late?"

"My reflection. Last night it was late. Only by a moment, but late."

Mr. Ashworth did not laugh, which Imogen would later count in his favour. He stood up, took the pencil from behind his ear, and held it up in front of the glass. Then he moved it slowly from left to right. The three of them watched the reflected pencil follow, smooth and obedient, without the slightest delay.

"Seems punctual enough this morning," he said.

"It's showing off," said Ros.

"Rosalind."

"Well, it is. It knows we have company."

Mr. Ashworth looked from one sister to the other, and something in his weathered face softened. "My mother used to say that old glass is like an old dog," he said. "It behaves for visitors and saves its tricks for the family. I can take it down for you, if you want. Wrap it, store it in the workshop, and you'll never have to look at it again."

"No," said both sisters at once.

The word rang in the narrow hall, and the mirror seemed, for an instant, to hold it a little longer than it should.

Imogen cleared her throat. "What I meant to say, Mr. Ashworth, is that we would like to know whether it is safe."

"Safe?" He considered the question with the seriousness of a man pricing a window. "The glass is sound. The frame is sound. Whether it's safe depends on what you mean to do with it."

"We mean to leave it exactly where it is," said Imogen.

"And to watch it," added Ros softly.

Mr. Ashworth picked up his toolbox. At the door he paused, rain beading on the shoulders of his coat, and looked back down the hall.

"If I were you," he said, "I'd stop asking whether your reflections are late. I'd start asking whether they're early."

When the van had gone, the sisters stood together in front of the glass. Neither spoke. In the reflection, very slowly, Imogen's hand reached for Ros's hand, and the real Imogen, a heartbeat afterwards, found that she was doing the same.

"Did you see that?" she whispered.

"I saw it," said Ros. "And this time, Imogen, it was early."
